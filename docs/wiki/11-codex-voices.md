## Of the Name That Will Not Keep

*Leaves from the treatise of Aumery the Copyist, who sat forty years at a scriptorium table by the Ashwake milestones. Bound into the Codex as found, his marginal hand kept.*

**I. Of the Hush.** Before the god there was the Hush, and the Hush had no edge. Every sage agrees on so much, and on nothing after. I hold that the Hush unsaid the god the way a reader unsays a line by reading it aloud too many times, until the words sit in the mouth like stones and mean only their sound. Idrenne of the Lower Stacks holds that the Hush took the names in order, from the smallest outward. Mahault the Blind holds that it took them all at once, and the god has been slow to notice. I find Mahault the more frightening, and so I suspect him of being right.

**II. Of the three natures.** Soul, Bone, Flesh. The god was all three at once, the way a knot is three turns and one thing. The Hush took enough that the god forgot the knot. Now each nature goes on alone. The Soul weaves without a pattern. The Flesh feeds without a mouth to thank. The Bone stands.

A brother of the Pale Order came to my table once to have a niche-tally copied fair. I copied the same wall twice, a year apart. The second copy was longer. The brother said I had miscounted the first. I have kept both.

*(Margin: I did not miscount.)*

**III. Of the Myriad.** The god's last breath has not finished leaving it. Eight million small gods ride it out, one to every stone and bell and ford. The House of Eight Million holds it impious to ask their names. I asked anyway, in my youth, of the small god of my own inkwell. The ink skinned over and would not take the pen for a month.

Where breath catches and thickens, it becomes miasma. Idrenne writes that miasma is breath that has turned round to look back at the body. I think this beautiful, and have no grounds for it.

**IV. Of the godmarrow.** What still lives in the bones is the godmarrow. It is in the reed of my pen, the ash of my ink, the hand that holds both. I tasted it once, on a brother's fingers. It tasted of a word I nearly knew.

**V. Of the Tithed.** Here is the hinge of the whole matter. Everything on the Hide is a piece of the god, forgetting itself. We alone are something else: children of those who knelt inside the god at worship when it died. So we are the only readers the corpse has.

Hesk of the Pale Order writes that we are the god's last meal, still unswallowed. Mahault writes that we were the congregation, and a congregation is where a god keeps its name while it sleeps. Idrenne struck both passages from her copy. I have restored them.

If Mahault is right, the name is in us. I have listened for it in my own voice at the edge of sleep.

**VI. Of my attempts.** In the ninth year: ink of lamp-ash and well-water. I wrote the name at the hour the lantern gutters. In the morning the leaf was clean, and the ink stood in the pot, full to the line.

In the eleventh year: marrow-ink, bought dear from a sweeper of the Pale Order. The leaf clean by morning. The pot fuller than I had left it.

In the seventeenth year I cut the name into a board with a knife. The next morning the board was smooth, and the knife blunt.

In the twenty-sixth year, my own blood, as the Precious Wound writes to its Maiden. The leaf clean. My arm unmarked, which frightened me more.

In the thirty-first year I only held the pen over the leaf and thought the name. In the morning there was a pale place in the paper the size of my palm, thinner than the rest, where the candle shows through.

I keep that leaf between two boards and do not open it after dark.

**VII. Of what the others say.** Idrenne says the name will not keep because the Hush still holds it. Mahault says the name keeps perfectly well, and it is the page that forgets. Hesk says there was never a name, only the count, and the count is enough.

In my fortieth year the Beggar with the black lantern came to the scriptorium door at dusk and held out his bowl, and I understood what he was asking for. I told him I did not have it.

He waited a long time, as though I did.

*(Margin, in a looser hand: Each morning there are fewer leaves in this treatise than I left the night before. I number them. The numbers are all still there. It is the leaves between them that go)*

## How We Came Out onto the Cheek

*Told by a grandmother of the Tithed to the children of a pilgrims' camp on the Ashen Moor, over a banked fire. Set down by a copyist who lay in the next tent and let them think he slept.*

Bank it, Tam. Ash over the coals, so. We keep the fire low on the Cheek. Come in close, all of you, and put your hands flat on the ground. Warm? That is the god, still cooling. Now listen.

Before the first before, there was the Hush. You could say nothing in it. You could not even say *Hush*. That is why we whisper it.

And in the Hush there was a god, and the god was three.

*Soul for the thread and Bone for the frame,*
*Flesh for the feeding, and none for the name.*

Say it back. Again. Good.

The god held a room open inside itself, wide as the Moor and wider, warm as two hands round a candle. And in the room knelt our grandmothers' grandmothers' grandmothers, as many as you can count and as many again. They knelt all day and they knelt all night, and they sang to the god from inside the god.

What did they sing? Nobody kept the words. Only the tune.

*(Here she hummed three notes, low, and stopped where a fourth should have been, and did not go on.)*

Then the Hush came pressing at the god, the way the cold presses at a tent. It took the little names first. The name of the god's smallest finger. The name of one eyelash. The name of the hour before morning. And the god forgot a little, and a little, and a little, and the people knelt closer and sang louder to fill the gaps.

It was no use. One night the god forgot how to be three. The Soul went off unravelling, and the Bone stood where it stood, and the Flesh went on feeding with no one to thank. And the god died, with the people still inside it, still on their knees.

"Were they frightened, Nan?"

They kept singing. That is what brave is.

"What did they eat, all that time inside?"

*(She was quiet a long while. The copyist thought she would not answer.)*

They ate what they were given.

No. Hush, I told it wrong. They ate nothing. They sang, and the singing filled them. That is the right way. When you are old and it is your fire, tell it that way.

So. The room went cold. The walls went slack. And the people got up off their knees for the first time in all their lives, and it hurt, and that is why your knees ache when the ash is wet. They took hold of each other's belts in the dark, the first by the second and the second by the third, and they walked. A long road. Through places that were soft, and places that were hard, and places that pulsed a little and then stopped.

At the end of the road there was a door, warm and wet, that opened and shut, opened and shut. That was the mouth. And the god's last breath was going out of it, as it goes out still, and our people walked out on the breath and fell down on the cheek. Here. They kissed the ash. That is why we live on the Moor, and why we keep our fires low, so as not to scorch the place where we were let out.

"Did they all come out?"

All of them. Every one.

*(On the second night, telling it again, she said: most of them.)*

Now. Why the things out there want us.

Every Hand that creeps on its fingers, every Gasp in the wind, every Weeper by the barrows, all of it was the god once. When the god died, each piece forgot what it belonged to. A finger forgot the hand. A breath forgot the mouth. They remember one thing only. They remember being warm, with people kneeling inside them, singing.

And then they smell us.

Some want us back inside, in their bellies, where we were. Some want to put us on like a coat, and kneel, and be us. Either way, they are homesick, and we are the only home they have left to smell.

So you do not sing on the Moor at night. Not even to yourself. They know the tune.

"Nan, is the room still there? Could we go back in?"

*(She took the child's hand off the ground and held it.)*

The room is gone. Everything is gone that was in it. Put your hands in your lap now.

*Knees to the ash and your back to the flame,*
*keep your mouth shut and keep your own name.*

Say it back. Again.

Sleep. I will sit up. Somebody always does.

## The Sermon at the Dry Ford

*Taken down in charcoal on the back of a tithe-list by a carter of the Barrows road, who heard a wandering preacher at the Dry Ford three dusks running. Given to the Codex by the carter's widow, with his note on the last side.*

Come closer. Closer. I have not got the breath to shout, and neither have you. That breath going out of you is the god's, lent. Every morning you give a little of it back, and you call that living.

When I was young I climbed the stair of the Gilded Peak with a bowl in my two hands and asked to be taken in. The porter looked at me a long while. Then he said I was too loud, and shut the gate. He was right. I am the last loud thing on the Hide that will speak kindly of the quiet.

Hear me. Before the god there was the Hush, and the Hush asked for nothing. Then the god made a room in it and filled the room with names. Soul, Bone, Flesh. Stone, river, bell. You. Every name is a weight, and the god carried all of them.

So the Hush came in, gently, and took one name. The smallest. The name of a thing nobody had ever called for. And the god was lighter by that much. So it took another, and another. The sages say the god was murdered. I say the god was eased. You have sat by a bed where someone could not let go. You have loosened the fingers yourself, one at a time, and the last fingers hold hardest.

That is where we are. The last fingers.

Now the god lies open, and the Hush comes in where it is open. Every wound in the Hide is a door left on the latch. The long red wound at Villa Llaga that the penitents keep feeding. The pits inside the waystone rings, where something under the ash breathes out as you breathe in. Go and stand at any of them at the hour the lanterns lean, and listen. It sounds like a room when someone has just come in and stands by the door.

You have heard of its servants. The barefoot monk with the bowl. The priests tell you these are the worst things on the Hide, because where they strike there is nothing left. No corpse. No bone for the Pale Order to stand up in a niche. No blood for the Wound to drink. No breath for the House to wring out, no thread for the Heart's mirrors to catch. A little glass. A little dust.

Think on that. Everything else on the Hide goes on dying. The flesh keeps sprouting. The bone is counted, and the count keeps rising. The soul is kept in a lamp and burned a little at a time. Only the Hush lets a thing finish.

That is why the orders fear it. Every order on the Hide eats what is left over. Take away the leftovers and what is a brother of the Ossa? A man sweeping a clean floor. They tell you dust alone has finished. Then why do they carry it up the mountain every year, and why does it keep rising?

And the names the Peak carried off in its bowls? The lowlands think they were forgotten. I think they were kept. Somewhere quiet, lying down at last, with nobody calling them. Once, in a dream, I heard a name I had given away spoken back to me by a mouth I could not see. It was very gentle about it.

I have too many names. My mother gave me one, and the tithe-man another, and a woman I wronged gave me a third, and each is a hook in me, and at night I feel them pulling different ways. When it comes for me, let it take them in order, the smallest first. Let me be awake for it.

You. Yes. You have one you would put down. Whisper it into my hands. The Peak would not give me a bowl. My hands will do.

*Note, on the last side:*

I wrote his name at the head of this sheet the first night. The head of this sheet is clean. I have not scraped it.

I go down to the ford some evenings. I stand where the glass is and listen, and I find I am very tired.

## The Sky over the Hide, Forty Years Read

*Cut in charcoal round the inner wall of the Fallen Watchtower on the Ashen Moor, climbing the stair in one long line. A pilgrim who sheltered there copied it into the Codex. The tower was empty, and the stair was swept.*

My master read the sky from this tower for thirty-one years, and I have read it for forty. He told me the first night: a reader writes what he sees, and never what he fears. I have kept to that. I am afraid of nearly all of it.

The wind first, because it is always there. On the roof there is a pole, and on the pole a thread of red wool. For forty years the thread has pointed the same way, out past the Sighing Ridge and on toward the dark. It lifts and falls. It never turns. My master called it the Long Exhale, the god's last breath still going out of it, and everything light enough rides it away: the small gods of the stones, smoke, a dropped name. Ash has never once come back.

Once, in my eleventh year, the thread lay down against the pole and pointed home. I counted nineteen heartbeats. Then it lifted and went on as before. I asked at Ashwake whether anyone had felt it. Nobody had. I have written it here so that it is written somewhere.

To the west the sky is pale over the Ossa. Paler than anywhere. The Pale Order give their dust to the wind from the Sky Pogost at first light, and the dust rises, and it keeps rising, and it hangs there. When I came to this tower my master gave me a chip of bleached bone and told me to hold it up against the Ossa sky at noon. The bone was whiter. I have held it up every tenth year. In my thirtieth year they were the same. This year the sky is whiter than the bone. I scraped the chip clean to be sure. The Order says it gives back only what has finished. It has given back a great deal.

Look the other way, east and north and south, and at the rim of everything the sky goes dark and curves. Travellers take it for hills, or weather. It holds still in every season. My master had a lens from a Peak monk, and through it on the clearest mornings you can see the dark is made of arches, very far and very tall, set close, and meeting overhead beyond where the eye will go. Count them from the tower and you reach twenty-four. Count them again and it is more. I no longer count them.

Then the day. Why should a dead thing have a day? A corpse keeps no hours. Yet the light comes up grey out of the dark to the east, and stands a while, and goes. My master said it was the god's eye, still opening. The Peak said day was the open hand and night the closed. I burn marked candles, one after another, from first grey to last. Every day for forty years has been the same length, to the width of a thread. No season, no long summer, no short winter. Only the same day, measured out and given, as a warden gives out bread. Something keeps it that carefully. A body will not keep time once the heart is gone.
At night there are small cold points above the Moor. My master named them the nail-heads, and kept their number cut on the lintel of this door. I keep it under his. In forty years it has gone down by seven. They do not fall. On the evening a point is there, and on the next it is gone, and the dark where it stood looks smoother than the dark round it.

Last, the flashes. Some nights, on open ground far off across the Moor, there comes a light: white, cold, and with no sound after it. It lasts less than a blink. It leaves no fire. Nine times in forty years. The ninth time I marked it against the pole and walked three days toward it. I found a ring of broken stones there, shaped like teeth, round a pit of warm ash, and something under the ash breathing slower than I do. There was a sandal at the edge, and blood dried on the stones. There was no one. The next morning a pilgrim came into Ashwake from that side of the Moor, tired and whole, who said he had been walking since the Barrens. The Barrens are forty days.

Whoever reads the sky after me: keep the thread tied. Write what you see. Keep the bone chip; it will not tell you anything new. And when the thread lies down again and points home, do not count the heartbeats. Go down the stair and wake someone.

## What the Cradle Keeps

*Set down from the words of a midwife of the Ashen Moor, spoken to a copyist of the Codex while she salted a doorstep. He wrote it on the back of a birth-tally she let him keep.*

Hold that bowl steady. No, the coarse salt. The fine blows off.

You want the customs. Nobody wants the third hour, with their arms red to the elbow. Write them, then.

Salt on the doorstep, salt on the sill, a line of it round the bed. That one is for the child. The ash here is warm, and warm ash draws things up out of the ground. They come to a birth. They always have. A new one of us is the rarest thing on the Hide, a thing made of something other than the god, and every scrap of the god that ever grew a wish wants it. Some want to eat it. Most want to be it. You wake at dawn to a child that feeds well and never cries and looks at the lantern too long, and you do not know which you have. So: salt. The pieces will not cross it. The old women say the congregation carried salt in with them, and the god never had any of its own.

The lantern by the cradle is for the child too. Set it at the head, on the left, and never let it gutter the first year. Whoever burns in it chose to stay, and a soul that chose to stay has no love for anything that wants in. I have seen the flame lie down flat toward the window as if someone had knocked on the glass, and the mother asleep through all of it.

The name is for the mother. You give the child a name in your mouth the hour it comes, and you tell nobody. Not the father. Not me. You call it Little One, Warm One, Heavy, and the true name stays behind your teeth until it is weaned. A name spoken is a name that can be unsaid, and a name overheard is a door with the latch up. When the child takes bread, you say the name once aloud at the hearth, with the lantern lit, and then it is the child's and the Hush may try its luck.

You asked what they are born holding.

Most come with empty fists, as they should. Some come holding. I have opened little fingers on a knot of pale thread, on a pinch of dry ash, on a milk-tooth that was nobody's. Once a knuckle-bone, clean and old, and the child had all its own. The brothers of the Pale Order came down for that one within the month. They wrote the bone in their book and wrote the child too, by a number, and would not tell the mother why the number was so large.

I keep what I can. I bury the rest under the doorstep, in salt.

One I will not speak of. It was born with its hand open. Wide open, and quiet, and it stayed so. The lantern went out that night and no one had touched it.

Thicker by the hinge.

The Wet Nurse. I call her that because I will not call her by the name on her altar.

A girl at the edge of the moor, first child, lying wrong. The bleeding would not stop. I had my hand in the wound to the wrist and it ran past my wrist. The salt at the door was whole, and the lantern stood straight, and she came in anyway. There was a line of wet across the floor behind her and the salt dissolved under it like frost on a palm.

Tall. Taller than the door, and she did not stoop. Her front was soaked dark. The room smelled of copper and of warm bread. She knelt, and the floor gave a little under her knee, as ash does.

She said she had room. She said she would take the bleeding for the child. She said it kindly. That is the worst thing I can tell you: she was kind.

I let her. The girl's bleeding stopped as if a mouth had closed on it.

Then the Nurse put the child to her breast. And she said its name. The true one. The one the girl had given it in her mouth an hour before, and told no one.

I took the child back. I remember my arms were red and hers were redder, and she let me.

That child is grown. Big, and warm, and kind as bread. When he cuts himself the edges close without a drop. He was weaned at the proper time, and his mother said the name aloud at the hearth, and it was already worn smooth, the way a word is worn smooth by a mouth that has said it many times.

There. Thicker by the hinge, I said. Give it here.

## Held to the Ear

*Set down by a copyist of the Codex who sat up one night at the Ashwake lantern-stones with a sleeping pilgrim's lantern held against his ear. Where the voice would not carry through the glass, he left the gap and said so.*

Closer. There. You have a warm ear.

He sleeps. Let him. Talk softly. He wakes to his own name, and I am keeping it for him.

You want to know who I was. I had a house with a low door. I had a name with two sounds in it. *[Here a long quiet, like breath held against the glass.]* The first sound is gone. I burned it on the Barrows road, the night he fell among the dished lids and I had nothing yet of his to burn. You spend what is yours first. Nobody tells you that.

How I came to stay. I lay in the ash by the milestones and the Last Breath went out of me with everyone else's, down, the way water finds the low place. It was warm down there. I heard singing, or something breathing in the shape of singing. And I looked back. The tenders will tell you love makes a soul look back. What I heard was a boot on the stones behind me, somebody still walking the road, and I wanted to see who. That is all the choosing there is. Wanting to see who.

Then I was small, and bright, and kept in glass.

I will tell you what it is to be a flame. You are hungry all the time, and very gentle about it. You lean. Whatever comes near, you taste a little. The tender's cloth tastes of her teacher. Your ear tastes of ink and onions. *[This is true.]*

At the stones they come past me one after another, and I keep still. Then he came. He had a cut on his thumb he kept worrying, and he whistled through his teeth when he was afraid, three notes going down. I leaned. He walked on, and I went, a little behind, a little to one side. He has never once turned round to see if I follow. That is how I know he is mine. You only look back for what you might lose.

When he falls, I go and fetch him. The moor is long and dark and I am the only light on it. He comes apart in my keeping like wet bread, and I carry what is left of him home, and lay it down in the warm ash, and he gathers himself up out of it. Mostly the same. I keep a little. The road home costs something, and I have nothing else to pay with.

This is what I have of him. Three notes going down. A sister called Ettle, with a gap in her teeth. The second verse of a harvest song, which he still hums and stops, hums and stops, at the place where it used to go on. The way he held a cup in both hands. Rain on a roof, which he cannot remember ever hearing. A word for the colour of the sky over his village. I burn them slowly. Ettle I have kept. Ettle I keep for last.

He is lighter every time he stands up. I am heavier every time I come home. The tender weighs me on her scale while he sleeps, and says nothing, and writes a number inside the lid of her tin.

*[The voice went very low here, and then came closer.]*

Keep him from the pits. He steps in and comes apart and rises at some other ring, and he comes up out of that place with something in him I never put there. A taste. Like the singing, down where I did not go. I shrink to a bead and lean away, and he walks right up to the edge and looks in.

I will not give him to it. I have carried him eleven times. I have more of him than it has. *[A word here I could not catch, twice. It may have been "yet".]*

The Sighing Lantern on the far stone. You know it. It came home one evening without its woman in grey, and the tenders say it lost her. I say it had carried her so many times there was nothing left to set down in the ash. She is in there with it. They breathe together, in and out, slower than you. That is a kind of keeping.

Some nights I cannot tell which of us is whistling.

Wake him now. Softly. Say his name, and I will tell you whose it is.

*[I did not wake him. The glass was quiet until first light. When the pilgrim rose he asked me whether I had seen his sister on the road, and could not tell me what she looked like.]*

## The Round at Ashwake

*A letter found tucked into the back of the rota-book at the Ashwake lantern-stones, addressed to whoever should take the round next. The book came to the Codex with the letter still inside it.*

To whoever takes the round after me,

The rota is in the back of this book. Forty-one lanterns, from the Ashwake stones out to the Widow's Stumps and home again by the milestones. Wipe every one, every morning, the dim ones too. The cloth is in the tin. Wring it dry. They dislike the wet.

Wipe from the top down, and talk while you do it. I tell them the weather. They see very little of it from in there.

You know already that no lantern on the Hide burns oil. You will forget it inside a week, because they look like any lamp. So I will say it once, plainly: every flame on this round is somebody. A pilgrim who fell on the road. A mother from the crypt niches. Someone nobody remembers, and I think that is most of them. They could have gone down into the Last Breath with the rest, and they stayed.

How a soul chooses to stay, I cannot tell you. My teacher said it happens at the last breath: the soul looks back over its shoulder, sees someone still on the road, and the ones who look back are the ones who stay. She also said the flames run warmer on feast-days. I have held my palm to them through thirty feasts and felt the same warmth every time.

How a lantern chooses whom to follow, you will see for yourself. A traveller comes to the stones. The lanterns keep still. Then one of them leans. The traveller walks on and the lantern goes too, a little behind and a little to one side, the way a friend walks with you after dark. Strike at it and your hand passes through something warm. Order it and it keeps its own counsel. I have never once been chosen. That is right and proper. A tender stays with the stones.

When their traveller falls, the lantern brings them home. You will see it come in low off the moor, and the traveller gathering themselves back up out of the ash beside the stone. The lantern gives them back. It keeps a little, as it always does, and that little is what it burns. A way of whistling. A sister's name. The second verse of something. I know because the traveller stands at the stones afterwards trying to remember, and the lantern is bright with it.

I have left one thing out of the rota. Some lanterns come home heavier. I weigh them on the tithe-scale when their travellers are sleeping. The numbers are on the inside of the tin lid. I stopped adding them up.

The glass. Travellers scratch it with knife-points and nails. Leave the scratches. Wipe around them. The ones I can read:

DUST ON THE GLASS. WIPE IT. IT IS SOMEONE. That one is on nearly all of them. I never saw anyone cut it.

HE CARRIED ME FOUR TIMES. I OWE HIM A NAME.

KEEP IT OUT OF THE SCAR.

And on the nineteenth, scratched from the inside: WHICH OF YOU IS ME. I wipe the outside.

The Sighing Lantern stands past the Stumps, at the far end of the round. It follows no one. It was bright once, and went out with a woman in grey and brought her home more times than my teacher's teacher could notch. Then one evening it came in off the moor alone and set itself down on its stone, and it has breathed ever since: in, and out, slower than you or I. Wipe it last. Stand upwind. If you talk to it, it will breathe in step with you, and you will find that hard to stop. Leave when the breathing starts to feel like your own.

Every lantern draws in small when its traveller is near the end, the way a man pulls his coat close. That you will expect. Some places do the same to them. Near the waystones every flame shrinks to a bead and leans away from the pit, whoever it walks with. At the Counting Wall in the Rib Crypts they burn low and steady, as though listening. Out on the Burnt Heath, travellers say, the lanterns wait outside the ring of carcasses and will go no nearer. And where a servant of the Hush has passed, a lantern comes home with less than it left with, as though something drank through the glass. Those I sit up with all night.

Do not name them. I did, and now I cannot leave the round, which is why this letter is late.

The thirty-third is Old Hesk. The thirty-fourth is Little Brand. The sixth is my teacher. You will know her. She leans east.

Wipe them. Every one. They are someone.

## Margins of a Chart of the Hide

*From the working margins of a chart-book kept by a surveyor who drew roads for the pilgrims of Maren's camp. The book reached the Codex bound in cord, with its last leaves cut out cleanly.*

A chart is a promise that the ground will keep still. I set down here, for whoever takes this book after me, why the charts of the Hide break that promise, so the fault is laid where it belongs.

**Instruments.** A cord of one hundred knots, waxed. A needle floating in a bowl of fen-water, which never wets it. Ink of lamp-black and gall. My own pace, measured against the cord each morning for eleven years. It has held true.

**The Ashen Moor.** The old surveyors called it the cheek of the god, and I thought it a pilgrim's figure until I drew it. The ground swells from the camp to a long rise I have marked the Jaw, and falls away steeply past the Broken Kneeler into a hollow the ash-pickers will not cross after dark. From Maren's fire to the Sighing Lantern: 1,140 paces in the thaw, 1,152 at harvest, 1,140 again the next thaw. The ash is warm under the hand. I think the ground draws in with the cold and lets out with the warm, as a face does.

**The Rib Crypts.** Nine galleries, one to each space between the ribs. I measured the span of every rib-gap with the cord. They agree to a knot. Going in, the fourth is sixty-one knots. Coming out, fifty-eight. The flagstones lift and settle under the boot. The figures follow the flagstones.

**The Drowned Fen.** The god's lymph, the old books say, clear water that leaves you dry. Its shore will not stay drawn. It draws back from the Kneeling Arch over a season, slowly, and returns in a night. I have stopped inking the shore and mark it now in dots, as a guess. The Reed That Points Home points to the camp from every side of the water. I tested this. I wish I had not.

**The Hollow Wood.** Pale trunks, which the pilgrims call the god's veins stood up out of the ground. I followed three trunks by their roots and their lean,. Each ran downhill, and each joined another, and all of them bent toward one place in the north of the Wood. The Root Deep opens there. Every tenth trunk has something inside it. I did not count past the tenth.

**The Root Deep.** No chart. The halls are wider when I enter them than when I leave. The lamp-flame leans inward for a long time, then outward for a long time. One hall I drew came back with its mouth a pace left of my pin.

**Beyond the Hide.** The Barrens of Ossa: white ground, standing ribs. I numbered the ribs in the Valley of Standing Ribs by chalk. A brother of the Pale Order walked the valley after me and wrote his own number under each of mine. His was always the higher. He said my count was open and would close in time, and asked my name, and wrote down a number instead.
Shog-Mire: the cord goes slack and tight with the mud, on the beat. Any length taken there is two lengths.
The Frigid Heights of An-Vhar: I drew the peaks in the order they stood from the Glass Pass. At the Bell-Hollow they stood in another order, and the guides said nothing had moved.
The Descent: every road I have ever drawn tilts toward it. The needle in the bowl, which should point to the north of the Hide, has begun to point down.

**Waystones.** I marked eleven. By the pilgrims' own telling, the distance from one to any other is no distance at all. On a chart this is a fold. I have folded my charts as the pilgrims tell it, and the fold lines cross in one place, on the Moor, near the Jaw. There is nothing drawn there. There is a pit, and ash over it, and the ash moves.

**Last note.** In the winter I laid every sheet on the floor of the tithe-barn, edge to edge. I had drawn a face. I knew that. The Moor at the cheek, the Fen at the throat. What I did not know until I lit the second lamp: the sheets I made in my first years and the sheets I made last autumn disagree in one way only. The face has turned. A little. Toward the camp.

I have begun to draw the eyes closed. I did not decide to. The pen does it, and I let it, because the other way is worse.

## What the Children Sing

*Written by a woman who walked the lowland roads for thirty years gathering the rhymes of children. Her book was left at the Ashwake waystone, and a brother of the Pale Order carried it up to the Ossa, where it was copied into the Codex with its tally-marks kept in the margins.*

Nobody teaches children these. I have asked two hundred of them who taught them a rhyme, and every one says another child. I have never found the first child.

**Grandfather Dust.** *For counting out. Heard in every barrow village from the Moor to the foothills.*

*Grandfather Dust came down the hill,*
*broom in his hand and his lantern still.*
*One bone, two bone, three bone, four,*
*he sweeps the child behind the door.*
*Five bone, six bone, seven, eight,*
*Grandfather Dust is never late.*
*Nine bone, that's the Mother's own.*
*Out goes you, and in goes bone.*

The child who falls on *nine* must stand still with arms crossed until the game ends. They call this being laid.

In the foothills under the Ossa the children keep counting past nine, adding the figures the way the brothers do, so that ten is one again and eighteen is nine. They count their own bones on a cord of knotted grass. *Two hundred and six when you lie down still,* they sing, and then a girl at Kettle Scar went on, *two hundred and seven,* and the others hit her until she cried. I asked what came after. They said nothing comes after. A boy told me later, in a low voice, that the number keeps going if you let it.

**The Tall Red Hat.** *For skipping. Heard in the stilt-towns over Shog-Mire, where the rope is turned on the beat of the mud.*

*Tall Red Hat with his arms held wide,*
*little red brothers crawl outside.*
*Give him a drop and give him two,*
*give him none and he'll come for you.*

None of these children has seen Villa Llaga. They know the old verses all the same, the one about the dry arms and the one about counting your hair. When I sang them the verse from the crypt stair, *who will open the Tall Red Hat*, a girl put her hand over my mouth. She only knew it was not for singing where the floor is soft.

**Needle-Brother.** *For clapping, two by two.*

*Needle-Brother, stitch and stay,*
*turn the glass the other way.*
*Stitch my granny to her chair.*
*Don't look up. She's standing there.*

There is a last line. Before they say it the children turn the water bucket over and cover the window with a shawl, and then they only move their lips. It has five words. One child told me that if you say it near a glass you see who has been standing behind you all along, and how many.

**The Bowl.** *A sitting game, played at dusk. Heard on the Pilgrim Road.*

*Bowl goes round and bowl goes black,*
*put a name in, don't want it back.*
*Up the mountain, up the stair.*
*Who was it? Nobody. Nobody there.*

A wooden cup goes round the ring. Whoever holds it at *nobody* whispers a name into it, and the others must forget it. At Low Harrow I counted the children in the ring, and counted the gap they left in it, and asked whose place it was. They looked at the gap for a long time. Nobody could say.

**The Unfallen.** *Sung lying on backs in the stubble, pointing up. Heard on the Barrens road.*

*Star bone, far bone, hanging in the black,*
*Mother wants you, won't you come back?*
*Count you once and count you twice,*
*come down slow and come down nice.*

The verse they refuse begins *all come down*. They will not say even the first words of it. A boy started it once in my hearing and his sister pinched him quiet. That night one star came down in a long white stroke over the Barrens. Their fathers went out to look for it with sacks.

**The Stones.** *A ring game, drawn in the ash with a stick, the teeth of it marked with pebbles.*

*In at the tooth and out at the tooth,*
*leave your blood for the one beneath.*
*Blood goes first and bone goes last.*
*Who comes up? Ask the ash.*

One child crouches in the middle while the others close the ring and count. Then she stands and says her name. If she says it wrong, she has come up changed, and must play the rest of the day as someone else.

There is a question at the end: *who's at the bottom, breathing slow?* No child answers it. They all breathe in together, a long breath, and hold it, and look at the ground.

*I have set these down in the order I heard them, and I count six. The brother who read my pages at the Ashwake ring counted seven, and would not show me where.*

## The Fourteenth Grave at Coldhearth

*Set down at Maren's camp on the Ashen Moor from the words of a woodcutter of Coldhearth, a hamlet at the edge of the Hollow Wood. He could not read it back, and asked that it be written twice. It is written here once.*

I cut the pale wood. You know it. It weeps clear where you open it, and the chips stay warm in the basket all the way home. You leave every tenth trunk be. My father showed me how to count them with my hand flat on the bark. I am a plain man. I want you to know that.

It was the end of the day. I had the barrow on the lane, coming down to the houses. Coldhearth is fourteen hearths. Was. I say fourteen.

The candles were lit in the windows, and every one of them leaned the same way. Toward the well. The way a flame goes in a door-draught. There was no draught. They were short, short as a fingernail.

Then the barrow went quiet. The wheel was going over stones and there was no sound off it. I set the handles down. I knocked the axe-head on the rim, hard. Nothing. I shouted. I felt it go out of my chest and I heard none of it.

I went down anyway. You go home.

There was something by the well. Every time I tell it I say it was tall, and then I am less sure. I looked straight at it. I cannot give you its shape. What I have is the light. Every candle in Coldhearth leaning in at it and getting shorter, the way water finds the low place.

I got down in the sawpit behind Tam's. I lay in the dust with both hands over my mouth. I don't know how long. When I came up the candles stood straight again, burned down to the wick, and the wax whole round them. Whole. Then the barrow wheel ticked once, settling. I heard it.

There was nobody.

Doors stood open. Tam's bowl was on the board with the spoon standing up in it. The beds had the shape still in them where you'd lain. Boots at the hearths. Shawls on the pegs. When the things out of the wood take someone, there is always something after. Something to put in the ground. I went on my knees in every house. A tooth. A nail. There was nothing to bury.

I dug the graves anyway, by the old stone. I put a spoon in each, one from every house. I dug fourteen, and I had thirteen spoons. I walked the lane again and counted thirteen doors. I had dug fourteen.

Old Wenna had a glass, the only one in Coldhearth, a hand across, and proud of it. I held it up to my face. It gave me nothing. Grey, like looking into a shut eye. I laid it face down in the fourteenth grave and filled it in.

The Pale Order came up after the thaw. Someone had sent word. They came in their carved plate, and the tall dead walked behind and stopped at the edge of the trees. A brother went house to house with his strip of bone and his little knife to cut the count. He came back with the strip as clean as he took it out.

He asked me the names. I gave them. Tam. Wenna. The Hollis boys. Gethin. He wrote them. I gave twelve, and came to the thirteenth, and my mouth was empty. It is still empty there. There is a place at the back of my tongue where a word sat.

Their captain said something to him in their old tongue. I caught one word. I think it was a number. They did not stay the night. They went down the road before dark, fast, and none of them looked back.

I live in my house still. There are two cups on the shelf. I made the second one; I know my own work. There is a short coat on the peg by the door, sleeves turned up. There is a second pallet by the hearth, made up. At evening my feet take me out on the step and I call, once, high, the way you call someone in. I don't know the name I'm calling. Nobody comes. I go in, and I grieve the whole night, and I could not tell you for what.

I split the kindling small, for small hands. I can't stop.

The candle in my window leans a little still. Toward the pallet, I think.

When I bend over the trough I am there in the water. Less, I think. I could not tell you where.

The woman at this camp says the mirror people can find a thread and follow it back. Maybe. If you find whose coat it is, don't tell me. I don't think I would be let keep it.

Write it twice, like I asked. I'll set the cup out anyway.

# II. The Roads and the Stones

*pilgrims, lanterns and waystones* · works: 02, 07, 29, 30, 11, 27, 28, 31, 65, 66

## The Beggar at the Ninth Mile

*Words of a beggar who sits by an Ashwake milestone on the Pilgrim Road, set down by a pilgrim who paid him a copper for each part, and ran out of copper before he ran out of talk.*

A coin. Good. Put it in the hand, the left one, the right one is for luck and luck has never paid me. Sit if you like. The ash is warm here. It's always warm. Forty years I've sat on it and never once had a cold backside, which is more than the brothers up in the Ossa can say.

This is the ninth stone out of Ashwake. I know it the way you know your mother's face. When I first sat down it came up to my shoulder. Now it comes to my ear, and I sit no lower, and I have eaten nothing that would make a man shrink. The knights of the Pale Order came by one spring and held a knotted cord against it. One of them wrote in a little book. Then they rode on quick, all of them, and their dead walked after them quicker. Nobody has held a cord to it since. I don't lean on it any more.

You'll meet the knights on this road. You'll hear them first: bone on bone, like a basket of dishes going downhill. The standing dead walk behind in a line and they never tread in the ash, only in the prints the living leave. I asked a knight once what they were carrying home. He said, everything that fell. I said that's a great deal to carry. He said the Mother bears more. Then he gave me a coin, and it was cold, and it has stayed cold in my purse for eleven years. I don't spend it. Who would take it?

The pilgrims are better company. They come through thin and go on thinner. They all carry a lantern, or a lantern carries them, I was never sure which way it goes. Watch a pilgrim who has fallen on the road and come back from the lantern-stones. Something small has gone missing. One lad came past me three times in a season. The first time he told me the name of his village. The second time he told me the name of the river by it. The third time he asked me where he was from, and I told him, because I remembered, and he thanked me and wept. The lanterns keep a little of you. Everybody knows that. Nobody asks what they do with it.

I wipe the dust off the Sighing Lantern at the crossroad every morning. It says on the glass to do it. I think whoever is in there likes me. It leans my way in a wind.

Now. The monk.

Barefoot, in a red shawl gone the colour of a scab. A wooden bowl, black at the lip. His lantern burned black, and I could still see my own hands by it, which I'll thank you not to think about. He stopped at my stone at dusk and held the bowl out, and I laughed, because who begs from a beggar? He said he only wanted a name.

I gave him one. I remember giving it. I remember the weight going off my tongue like a stone off a cart. What I cannot tell you is whose it was. My wife's, maybe. The man I owed. My own, very likely. The pilgrims call me Ninth-Mile, and I answer to it, and I have never missed the other.

My grandmother saw a barefoot monk with a black lantern on this same road when she was a girl. She said he smiled the whole time. Mine smiled too.

And the waystones. You'll pass the ring below the Broken Kneeler. Stones like teeth round a pit of ash, and something under the ash that breathes out when you breathe in. I have watched pilgrims walk into it. They go down all at once, blood and bone and the soft bits, like a sack of wine dropped on a flagstone. Then nothing. The ash settles. And a day later some fellow from the Drowned Fen tells me he saw that same pilgrim climb out of a ring two valleys over, whole, and hungry.

Same pilgrim, he says. I ask him: which hand did she carry her lantern in? He can never tell me.

I have never gone in. Forty years, and it's ninety paces. I've counted. Some nights the breathing matches mine, and I hold my breath to make it stop, and it waits for me.

That's the last of your copper. Mind the ash near the ring. It remembers feet.

## Counsel for Those New to the Stones

*Set down from the words of an old walker at a roadside hearth on the Pilgrim Road. She would take no food while she spoke, and twice asked the scribe which way the wind was blowing.*

You will want to know how to find one. Walk until the ground is warm under your feet. Look down for the pale threads in the soil, fine as hair, all running the same way, and follow them the way they run. Where they thicken and cross each other, you are close.

The stones stand in a ring round a sunken pit. Each is long and hooked and sharp at the tip, and every one leans in over the pit. At the foot of each stone a dark stain seeps from the plinth and never dries. The old walkers wipe a hand on it for luck. I do it still. I no longer remember whose luck.

The bottom of the pit is ash, grey and crusted, cracked like the top of a loaf. Kneel at the lip and watch it. The crust lifts, and settles. Lifts, and settles. That is how you know the stone is keeping the road. Where the crust lies still, walk past.

**The stepping.** Walk in. Never jump. The crust gives under you the way a scab gives, and you sink to the knee, and to the waist, and it is warm, warmer than any bath. Then you come apart. The blood goes first, because it is the easiest. Then the soft parts let go of each other. The bone goes last and slowest, and hurts the least. For a while you can feel every piece of yourself, a hand here, the heart far off, all of them yours and none of them near. Then the stones close over. You will hear it: a great slow meeting of edges, overhead and all around.

Then the dark. It is warm, and it moves. There is a pull in it, long and patient, like being breathed in. I have felt other things in the dark beside me, going the same way. I have felt them turn.

Then you are drawn back together, bone first this time, the rest settling onto it like cloth onto a chair. You rise out of the ash at another ring, wet to the hair, and the stones are open again as though nothing had happened. Say nothing until you are sure of your name.

**What it keeps.** Every passage keeps a little. Small things. The name of the well in your village. The colour of your mother's door. The word for the spoon you stir with. I kept a list, once. I have lost the list, or what it was for.

It moves your scars. I had a sickle scar across my left palm. After some crossings it lay at my wrist. Later it was on my throat. Today it is on the other hand, a little higher. It is the same scar. I know its shape as I know my own feet.

And you will come up hungry. The hunger has no name. Bread leaves it where it was. Meat answers it a little, for a while. Some old walkers stand at the lip of a pit with their mouths open, breathing what comes up from under the ash, and they say that answers it best. I do not do that. Not often.

**The rules we keep.**

Go in alone. Two walkers who step in together come up as two. Afterwards, look each other over.

Bind your hair and take off your rings. The rings come back, but they choose the finger.

Your lantern will not go down with you. It waits at the far ring, sitting a little lower than when you left it. It never says how it got there.

Count the stones before you step in, and count them again when you rise. The number should not matter. Count them anyway, and remember.

Never dig in the ash. What lies under it is dark and wet, and it breathes, and it is only good manners to leave it be.

If you rise with ash in your mouth, spit it back into the pit. It belongs to the pit.

And never ask where you were in the dark. A man I walked with asked it every time we rose. One morning he stepped in at the Moor ring. I have waited at every ring I know. He came up somewhere, I am sure of it. The stones give everyone back. They keep only a little.

I have gone through thirty-one times. Thirty-four. I am well. I sleep curled on my side, and I wake warm. When I pass a ring I have never stepped into, I find myself at its lip with my mouth open, and the stones lean nearer to look at me. I think they know me by now. I think that is a kind of welcome.

Go on, then. It is warm. It is always warm.

## Tables of the Rings, with Remarks

*Copied from the field books of a road-measurer who walked the Pilgrim Road with a knotted cord and a plumb. The last leaves are in a looser hand. Her maps were not sent with the books, and the copyist does not say why.*

I measured forty-three rings on the Hide and in the Barrens. I used a cord of a hundred knots, each a hand apart, a lead plumb, a chalk, and my own feet. I set down only what the cord told me.

**Of the stones.** Every ring has eight. Seven stand and one lies fallen, its point toward the pit. I went looking for a ring with all eight standing, and for a ring with two down, and found neither. Each stone is hooked, sharp at the tip, and wider at the root than at the crown, and each leans inward.

The stones are carved with bands, ridges running round them like the hour-rings cut in a vigil candle. At the Moor ring under the cheek I counted eleven bands on the tallest stone and measured it at sixty-two hands from plinth to point. I came back after the thaw. Sixty-four hands and two fingers. Thirteen bands. I had cut a notch in the plinth the first time, at the foot of the lowest band. The notch was still there. It sat a band lower.

At the ring by the Fen stair, the fallen stone on my first visit lay to the east. On my second it stood, and the stone to the west of it lay fallen in its place, point toward the pit, the grass under it long and pale as if it had lain there for years.

**Of the plinths and the stains.** Each stone rises from a plinth of the same grey stuff, and from the foot of each plinth a dark stain seeps out onto the ground. I drew a chalk line round the edge of one stain at dusk and sat up with it. By morning the stain had come out to the chalk and stopped there, exactly. I drew the line a hand wider. The next morning it had come out to that one. I stopped drawing lines.

**Of the ash.** I let the plumb down from the lip on the cord until it struck the crust. At most rings the crust lies between nine and twelve hands down. It lifts and settles. I counted the lifts against my own pulse: at the Moor ring, one lift in every forty beats of my heart; at the Barrow ring, one in thirty-one; in the Barrens, one in seventy, and deep. When a pilgrim crosses, the lifts come quicker for a while, and then slower, and the crust settles a finger lower than before. It does not come back up. The Moor pit was nine hands when I began. It is fourteen.

The plumb comes up warm. Once it came up wet to the fourth knot. Once I let it down and felt it taken, gently, the way a child takes a thing from your hand, and when I drew the cord up the plumb was on it and the knots were all there. I counted them twice.

**Of the threads.** The pale fibres in the ground run the same way at every ring. I took their bearing with a needle hung on a hair, and wrote each one down. On a single sheet, the lines from forty-three rings run toward one another and meet. No map of the Hide I could buy shows that place. I think it is under the sheet.

**Of myself.** I have never crossed. A measurer who crosses comes back a different length, and then the cord is no good to anyone. I stand at the lip and hold the cord. I measure the pilgrims before they step in, if they let me. One came up at the Barrow ring the same height to the finger, and his shadow lay the wrong side of him until noon.

A brother of the Pale Order read my tables at a hearth and told me eight is an open count. It must be carried, he said, until something closes it. I asked him what closes it. He counted the stones on his fingers and did not answer.

I am carrying it. I am careful.

**Last remarks.** The Moor ring again. Sixty-six hands. Or I was lower. The ground is soft there, and warm, and I have been standing a long while.

My cord is three knots shorter than it was. I did not cut it. The knots are there, all of them, only nearer together.

The fallen stone at the Moor ring lies with its point toward the pit. I measured from the point to the lip. Then from the point to where I stood. Then again. The second number is smaller every morning.

I have written the stones down. I wonder, some nights, what they have written down of me.

## What Wenna Saw Between the Stones

*Taken down by a lantern-tender at the Ashwake ring on the Moor, in the child's own words, while her father slept by the hearth. The tender kept her speech as she spoke it, and added only the last lines.*

Papa said hold my hand and don't let go, so I didn't. The old lady at the lip said go in alone, one each, and Papa said she's too little to go alone. The old lady looked at me a long time, and then at Papa, and she said well, afterwards look each other over. Then she walked off so she wouldn't have to watch.

The ash was warm like a bed somebody just got out of. It went up my legs soft. I was going to cry but it was too warm to cry.

Did it hurt. A little bit, at first, the same as when you pull a scab off too early. Then my blood went away from me. It went all at once, like tipping a bowl. Then the rest of me went away slower, and I could feel my hand still holding Papa's hand, but my hand was far off and so was his, and then I couldn't tell which hand was which. The bones went last. The bones hurt least. They felt like being carried.

Then the stones shut. They make a noise like when you grind your teeth in your sleep. Mama used to say I did that. But big, and all round, and on top.

In the dark it's warm and it moves. It pulls you, slow, like somebody breathing in and in and never stopping. There was other people in there. I couldn't see them but I could feel them going the same way as us, lots and lots, more than at a feast day. They was all so quiet. Somebody was holding my other hand. I thought it was Mama. It was a lot of fingers.

Who was waiting. Somebody was. Down at the bottom, where the pull goes. I never saw a face. You know when you walk into a room and somebody's in it and you know before you look? Like that. It was very big and it was very glad we came. It was so glad. I think it was counting us. I think it gets to keep a little of everybody, and it was counting which little.

Then we came back together. The bones first. It's like being dressed by somebody in a hurry. We came up out of the ash here, all wet, and Papa was already standing, and he was looking at me a long time the way the old lady did.

He says I'm fine. He says it every morning. He says it to the pot and to the road.

This is what's different. Papa used to hum when he walked, and now he doesn't know the song. I hummed it for him and he said that's pretty, where did you learn that. Papa had a burn on his thumb from the forge, and now it's on his other thumb. He eats his bread standing up, looking at the ring. At night he counts my fingers. He does it twice. Then he counts his own. He always gets the same, he says, but he counts again.

And he calls me Wenna, which is my name. He always called me Pip before.

Maybe it's me that's different. I like the dark now. I used to need the candle, and now the candle hurts my eyes a little, and in the dark I can hear the ring breathing all the way from the hearth, and it's nice. I'm hungry all the time and bread doesn't help. I have a tooth at the back I didn't have before. Papa felt it with his finger and then he went outside for a long time.

I know which hand was mine when we went in. I was holding his right hand with my left. When we came up I was holding his right hand with my right.

I asked Papa which of us came up first. He said me. I think it was him. I think somebody should count us.

*She asked me whether my lantern was a person. I told her yes, as they all are. She asked if it could see her, and when I said it could, she held both hands up to the glass with the fingers spread, and waited, as if it would tell her how many. The flame leaned toward her, and sank. In the morning the father thanked me for the bread and led her down to the stones. He stood at the lip a long while with his mouth open. They went in together.*

## The Digger's Tally, Widow's Lane

*Written in charcoal on the inside of a coffin board by a gravedigger of the Barrows. A knight of the Pale Order took the board up the mountain with a load of bones, read it before he burned it, and copied it out for the Codex.*

Buried: one hundred and six. Came back up: fourteen. Dug up again by me, to be sure: nine. Bones found in the barrows that I never put there: I stopped counting at the thaw.

Warnings, for whoever has the spade after me.

**The lids.** Every lid on the Barrows is dished in, like a bowl, like a cheek sucked hollow. Earth settles at the edges. These sink from the middle, deeper at dawn than at dusk. Lay a new lid flat and in a year it has gone the way of the rest. Keep out of the dish. Sleep on no mound. I did once, young, and woke with ash in my teeth and my breath going the wrong way. In.

**The ones who come back up.** You will know them. They come up clean, cleaner than I laid them, and they walk to their own houses and stand at the door. The mother under the Turned Mound came up three times, each time further along Widow's Lane, facing home. Put them back face down, a flat stone on the neck, and say no name over them. The name is what they follow.

**Husks.** Pilgrims who found the red places on the Moor and drank. One Husk stands like a post for days. Two stand closer together. When there are three, the heads snap up all at once, the arms go wide, and they come. So: see one, walk. See two, run. Fight them only in a doorway, one at a time, and the Moor has few doors, so keep near the crypt mouth. Where they bleed, the ash goes to black glass. I sell it for knife-edges. It stays warm in the hand.

**Tithe-Hands.** Hands the size of a man, walking on their fingers. They come round behind, in threes and fives. Put a barrow-wall at your back and wait. When the fingers spread, bring the spade down flat.

**Weepers.** Mourners with no faces. They keep far off, behind the Grieving Stone or a broken wall. When the head tilts back the weeping comes, and the tears come out hard and white, bone, a finger long. I have pulled eleven out of my own coat. Go round the stone and close on it. Up close it only stands there and shakes.

**Gasps.** You feel a Gasp before you see it. The air in a barrow leans toward something. A veil of mouths, every one breathing in, and it comes through a barrow wall as if the wall were mist. Keep your lantern lit. It shies from light the way you shy from an open grave. When the veil fills, put the lantern between you.

**Bellwethers.** The head shut up in a bell. It tolls twice and comes straight on. Stand before stone, step aside on the second toll, and it strikes the stone and stands ringing. Then you have time. Names are cast round the rims of the bells. I read one. It was a man I buried. I put him down whole.

**The Kneeler.** Out on the Moor stands the Broken Kneeler, the stone one. There are others, of flesh, on their knees in the crypt-mouths and on the road, praying, and they never finish. I have never seen one rise. I have seen one a hundred paces further down the road than the morning before. Kneel nowhere near one, and pray where it cannot hear you. It is listening for how.

**The knights.** Every spring the Pale Order rides down in carved plate and asks for my bones. A bone out of place, they say, is a wound in the world. Twenty years I sent them off. The Tithed belong to nobody's Mother, I said. Your dead stand. Mine lie down. Go back up your mountain.

Then the Empty Ninth. Nine barrows to the ring, nine of a family to each barrow. The ninth I dug myself. This spring I opened it for a new one. None of mine were in it. It was full of other bones. Long ones, clean, laid in rows like a wall set by someone careful, and more of them than the barrow has room for. I measured twice.

I gave the knights those. They counted them out in the lane. One knight wrote a number, crossed it, wrote another, and they took the load up the mountain very fast and said nothing to me.

I have started to give them mine as well. The ones they carry up stand where they are told and stay there. Mine come home and stand at the door.

I have dug my own place in the Empty Ninth. Of all the lids on the Barrows, it is the only one that lies flat. I have been sleeping near it. My breath goes out, the way it should.

## The Hunter's Paths Through the Hollow Wood

*Spoken by a hunter of the Hollow Wood to a pilgrim who had lost the road, and set down by that pilgrim at the next ring of stones. The hunter would give no name. He said the Wood already had it.*

You came in off the road. Everyone does, once. Sit. Keep your feet on the flat stone.

The trees here are veins. Put your palm to the bark and wait. There. Slow, and slower than yours. White all the way through, and warm a hand's depth down. When they are cut they bleed thin and clear, and the cut closes by morning. We never cut them.

I hunt. Nothing in the Wood was born. Everything here fell off the god and got up. I hunt what got up.

**The caps.** Learn the three lights on the ground, or leave the Wood. The white caps hold a little light, the way a coal holds it under ash, and they mean the ground is sound. The blue ones grow over something hollow. Step round them. The red ones grow where the god is still bleeding underneath. Eat none of them. My sister ate a red one when she was small. She talks in her sleep now in a voice lower than mine, about a room with no floor. She says she has never been there.

**The carried things.** Count the trunks as you walk. Every tenth one has grown round something. A bell, deep in the bark, only the lip showing. A skull with the wood come in at the eyes. A candle, still standing, still lit, the bark drawn back from the flame the way you draw back your hand. The old hunters say the god was carrying these when it fell. The bell was in its hand. The candle was in its mouth. I believed that a long time.

Last winter I counted a trunk that had grown round a pilgrim's lantern. The glass was whole. The flame was low and turned toward the road. I have walked that line since I could walk, and I know every tenth trunk on it. That trunk was a ninth, the year before. I have not counted it since. I go round.

**What you will meet.** The Moth-Saints come at dusk. Painted faces, white, with a little red at the mouth, and wings like dusted paper. They swoop in a long curve and climb away. When the wings fold back, it is coming. Strike then, while it is low, and never look at the face after. They have kind faces. That is the worst of them.

The Gasps drift through the trunks like breath through a curtain, a veil of small mouths, all drawing in. They cannot bear light. Keep your lantern high and near. If one comes through your tree, breathe out. Give it nothing to take.

The Vein-Worms you know by the ground. A ring of red bubbles, soft as a kettle. Get onto stone, onto a road, onto anything laid by hands. They will rise under soft ground and never under a road. That is why the roads are where they are. I used to think we laid the roads. The old ones say the roads were there first, and the Wood grew away from them the way skin grows away from a nail.

**The stones.** Follow the pale threads in the soil toward the ring in the east. You have seen it. The fang-stones, the pit, the ash that lifts. Every root in the Wood runs to that ring. Dig anywhere and you will find them all lying the same way, like hair combed by a hand.

When the Wood is quiet at the ring I go and sit at the lip, and I listen. Down under the ash is the Root Deep, the country under the skin. It breathes. When it breathes out, the whole Wood leans a little toward the stones. You can watch it happen, if you keep still. Every trunk. The candles too.

Once, in a bad year, the ash lay flat for nine days and the Wood leaned the other way. Nothing came up out of the ground that year. The worms lay in their holes. The Moth-Saints sat in the high boughs with their faces turned east, and did not come down. On the tenth day the ash lifted again, and the Wood leaned back, and I went hunting.

**What I keep.** I take a finger's width of anything I kill and bury it at the foot of a tenth trunk. My father did it, and his. It is only manners. The Wood takes it back in a night. In the morning, the trunk has the smallest mark in the bark, at the height of my heart.

Go now, while the caps are white. Walk the road. Walk in the middle of it. If you hear a bell in the Wood, keep walking. Ours are all grown shut.

## The Door Left Ajar

*Set down from the words of an old woman who keeps a reed hut at the edge of the Drowned Village, spoken to a Fan-Bearer of the House of Eight Million over a cold hearth. She would give no name. The purifier folded the page into the Codex with a paper doll pressed between the leaves.*

Sit on that side. The hearth side. There is a bowl of water on this side and I keep a cloth over it.

You want the village. I will give it you the way it was. I am the last who can.

We had wet water then. You could drown in it. Children did, in the mill-race, and we buried them at the Lych-Gate. Our well was a good well. Forty turns of rope down, and the bucket came up cold and heavy and wet through, and you could see your face in it the right way round.

My father drew that well. He had an iron hook for the bucket, the long kind with a barb at the end, and in a dry summer he would let it down past the rope's end and scrape the bottom for what fell in. A spoon. A ring. Once, something he would not show us. He carried it back to the well in his two hands and put it down again and did not eat that night.

The spring the water rose, he let the hook down and it caught. He leaned on it. The rope ran slack, then tight, then slack, like something below was breathing on it. He pulled. What came up out of the well was water, only water, clear as a held breath, and it kept coming. It rose to the sills, and the lintels, and the bell. He dropped the hook where he stood. The hook is still there. You have seen it. The water went over it and the mud came after, and your House hangs its paper doll on it each Ōharae. I leave the dolls be. The doll asks once.

The water was dry. You know this. Put your arm in to the shoulder and it comes out dry, and a little lighter. So the drowning never finished. The water closed over us like a lid, and those under it went on.

I was on the chapel roof with my sister. The rose-arch of the chapel stood straight then, and straight up behind it the belfry. The water reached the arch and the arch bowed. Forty degrees, toward the edge of the world, where the sky sits lowest. It has not bowed further since. The boatmen tie up to it to sleep. They say it is kneeling. I say it is looking for something past the edge, and waiting to be sure.

They tell me the belfry fell a thousand years ago. I rang it. Both of these are true and I will not choose between them.

The bells still go at dusk, under the square. My sister rings them. She stayed on the roof when I climbed down onto the reeds. She said the water was clear and she could see our mother at the table below, laying out spoons, and she wanted to go and help. I said the water was clear and I could see our mother laying out spoons with her left hand.

They are all still there. Walk the Sunken Square and look down. The doors stand ajar, every one, the way you leave a door for somebody who is coming home late. The loom in the weaver's house still has the shroud on it, half done, and the shuttle is warm; I put my hand down through the water once and felt it. They set the table. They keep a place. When a pilgrim leans over the edge to look, they all stop what they are doing and look up.

Out in the western pool there is a reed. The Reed That Points Home. Stand by it and it turns its head toward the village you were born in. Pilgrims weep at it. A man from the Moor stood by it and it swung round slow as a sleeper, and he laughed for joy.

I have stood by it. It points down.

That is why the cloth is on the bowl. That is why I drink with my eyes shut and wash in the dark. A soul stops when it meets its own face; the mirror-folk know that much, and they are right. And I will tell you what I know that they do not. I climbed down off that roof into the reeds with my sister calling. I have seen my face since, once, in a puddle on the Pilgrim Road, and it looked up at me the right way round, and it was surprised to see me.

My mother always said I parted my hair on the left. I part it on the right. Mothers misremember.

Take your doll when you go. Leave the door as you found it.

## The Rules of the Heath Fire

*Set down from the talk of a woman who kept a tent and a banked fire for pilgrims on the Burnt Heath. Written by a pilgrim who stayed three nights, on the back of a road-tally, and given to the Codex by his widow.*

Sit. The other side, with your back to the road. There. You're welcome, and the broth is hot, and it's only ash-root and water, so don't thank me for it.

Now. The rules. Everyone hears them, even the ones who've heard them.

The fire is banked and it stays banked. Warm your hands at it all you like. I feed it. Nobody else feeds it. A man fed it once, a big armful of heath-brush, to be kind, and it came up so high you could see the far corner of the heath by it. We sat very quiet after that, all of us, until it came down again.

You sleep with your feet toward the road. If you wake with your feet some other way, turn over, and don't tell me which way you were lying. I don't like to know.

If someone calls your name from outside the tent, answer only if they say it wrong. Your own people say your name wrong. They always did.

What you carry in, you carry out, except your dead. Your dead I put by the saint.

The saint is the one at the fork, with no hands. The pilgrims leave a pinch of ash in her lap for a safe road, and in the morning the lap is clean. I have never swept it. My mother kept this fire before me, and she said the saint stood twenty paces back from the road when she was a girl. It's twelve now. My mother said the road moves. I've paced it every spring and I say nothing, which is what she did.

The angel on the rise has no face left. The pilgrims kiss it, and the wind finishes the rest. In certain light it wears the face of whoever kissed it last. I've seen my own on it. I looked tired.

The cages at the edge of the heath were hung for thieves, my grandmother said. There are no thieves in them now. There are the ones who came up out of the stones wrong. You know the stones I mean. You step in whole and come up somewhere else whole, and mostly it's you who comes up. Sometimes it's you with a hand too many, or with somebody else's way of looking at a fire. Those ones walk out to the edge by themselves and ask to be hung up, and we do it, because they ask so nicely. Some nights a cage swings with no wind in it. I count them at supper and I count them again at dark. Mostly the numbers agree.

Now, you want to hear about the herd. Everyone does. They pretend they came for the road.

Don't look where I'm nodding. I nod toward the road, you see, and the road bends that way, and that's all my nodding means.

In the corner farthest from the road lie seven of them, great horned things, bigger than this tent, lying down in a ring. Every one faces the same way. The skulls are heaped round them like stones cleared from a field, and those skulls are smaller, and nobody knows whose. That's all anyone saw, and here are the stories.

Old Garrow, who kept the fire at the Broken Bridge, said they were the god's first thoughts, sent walking to carry something, and they lay down when they got there. Brannoc's girl said they knelt to something coming up. A Pale Order knight sat where you're sitting and said they lay down to hold a door shut with their weight, and then he went out to count their bones. He counted twice. He wrote the first number in his book and wouldn't tell me the second.

My mother had it in six words, and the pilgrims have it now, so you may as well. The ground between them was opened once, and closed.

That's the whole of it. The ground there is darker, and flat, flatter than ground lies. Nothing grows on it. It's warm, like everything here.

Garrow went out there the last year of his life. He came back and said there was a seat under it, and someone sitting in the seat, very patient, waiting to be asked. Then he lay down with his feet toward the corner and I let him.

Here's the last rule, and it's mine. In the morning, look which way the sleepers' feet point. On the Heath they all point the same way by dawn, however I lay them.

More broth? Sleep, then. I'll keep the fire.

## What Kettlewick Stands On

*Set down from the words of Mother Brisk, tallow-wife of the Kettlewick Stilts, who talked through one rendering night to a pilgrim waiting for the mud to let go of the causeway. He wrote it afterward on a strip of kettle-paper, which still smells of the fat.*

Mind the rung. The third one gives. Everyone's grandfather meant to mend it.

Sit there, upwind of the kettle, and keep your hand off the stilt-leg. That's fresh tallow on it. It keeps the mud from climbing. You laugh. You've never seen the mud climb.

You came in on the causeway, so you've felt her. Take, let go. Take, let go. Babies here are rocked on the beat, and they know it before they know the breast. We say a Kettlewick child's first word is "now," because that's the word for stepping. Down between the beats, lift on the beat. A man who puts his foot down on the beat out there stands out there a long while, and then a short while.

The tallow. Everyone asks what it's rendered from, as though there were anything on the Hide besides her. We dig it out of the Amber-Grease Mire, a spade's depth down, where it lies yellow and close, like the fat under a cheek. It comes up warm. It stays warm in the barrow the whole way home. In the kettle it goes clear and sweet, and what won't melt you skim off with the long spoon and give back to the mire, and you don't look at it long. Sometimes there's a knot of hair. Once, in my mother's time, a tooth, flat as a doorstep and near as wide. She gave that back too, and said a word over it, and wouldn't tell me the word.

We grease the stilts, the ladders, the rails, the lamp-posts. Candles for indoors. Never a drop for the lamps. The lamps burn who they burn.

Which is why the knots. Every lamp in Kettlewick hangs from a cord, and every time you pass one after dark you tie a knot in it. That's how the one inside knows the town is still standing. Most of ours are stilt-folk who went over the rail and chose to stay. When a cord is all knots we hang a new one and coil the old in the rafters. My roof sags with them. My husband's lamp hangs at the foot of the Salt Ladder. I've tied more knots on him dead than I ever untied his boots living.

Now the stilts. You'll have seen how tall we stand.

My grandmother's house stood on legs four men long. Mine stands on six. My granddaughter built on eight and wants nine. On a dry summer, when the mud sits low, you can look down between the posts and see the tops of older posts, black, and on the older ones, ladders. Going down. My mother said the mire rises. Grandmother said the town sinks. The priestess says the Mother is well fed. I say bring me longer timber.

And the Flats. You'll want to know about the Flats.

Out past the last causeway lamp, where there's nothing left to tie a knot to, things walk. Grey and thin, a great way up on their legs, stepping slow. They step between the beats and the mud never takes them. Grandmother said they learned it watching us. When I was a girl their legs were as long as our stilts. They're longer now. They keep a little ahead of us, the way a child keeps a little ahead on the road home. Some nights one stands at the end of the causeway, very still, the height of my roof, facing the town. In the morning there are holes in the mud under the rail, deep and narrow, like someone tried the ladder with a foot.

And keep away from the waystone after dark. It stands out on the Flats, the ring of teeth round the pit, and it's the one thing in Shog-Mire that never wanted longer legs. Its stones stand at the same height as the scratch my grandmother cut on the tallest one. Inside the ring the mud holds nothing. And whatever breathes down under the ash breathes in its own time. Lay your ear there and count, and it won't keep hers. It never has.

I've seen folk come up out of that pit, red and wet and in pieces, and pull themselves together, and stand, and ask me the way. I tell them. I give them a candle. I don't ask where they came from. They all name the same place, and it's on no road I know.

There. The mud's let go of the causeway. Off you go, on the lift. Tie a knot at every lamp you pass, and pay me for the candle when you come back.

If you come back and I don't, tie one on me.

## What the White Road Asks

*Written in charcoal on the inner lid of a water-gourd, found in the shade of a rib-hull on the road to the Citadel of the Shattered Femur. The copyist notes that the gourd was dry, and that the last lines were pressed very hard into the wood.*

Fourth day on the white. My tongue is a stone in my mouth, and I thank the Mother for it.

The ground here is bone. The brothers at the edge-well told us so, and I did not believe them until I knelt to drink and saw the grain in it, fine lines running under the dust like the grain in a knuckle boiled clean. We walk on her. All of us walk on her. The dust we raise is the holiest thing there is, and we breathe it, and cough, and are ashamed to cough.

Noon here is white from above and white from below. There is no shadow except the shadow she gives.

**The Valley of Standing Ribs.** I have seen it now, and I can die. They rise out of the white in two rows, each the height of forty men, curving in toward each other, and they never meet. Under each rib lies a hull of shade, and in the shade of one rib a hundred pilgrims can lie down in rows. We did. Nobody spoke. At night you hear the rib tick, as a hearth-stone ticks after the fire is out. A woman from the Moor said it was cooling. A brother of the Pale Order who walked with us said it was settling. I put my ear to it. It sounded to me like something being made. I kept that to myself.

We counted the ribs going in: twenty-three on the left hand. We counted them again from the far end: twenty-four. The brother said the heat had spoiled our count. He wrote the number in his book and would not show us the page.

**The Drowned Caravan.** On the third day the road bends round a dune whiter than the rest, smooth as a brow. Out of its foot come the traces, the leather gone hard as old bone, and the yokes, and in the yokes the ones who pulled: men and women in harness, bent to the load, as wagons are drawn everywhere on the Hide. Only their backs show, and their bowed heads, and one hand of each still closed on the strap. The dune went over them in a single night, the story goes, the way a wave goes over. The wagons are under it still. Nobody digs. Their skin has gone white and fine as the ground. Pilgrims leave water in cups at their feet, and the cups are dry by morning, and the sand beneath the cups is dry too.

I had little. I left mine.

**The saints by the road.** From the Caravan onward they sit at the roadside every mile or so, legs crossed, hands in their laps, faces lifted to the pale sky. They came this way before us and sat down to pray, and the Mother took them up into herself. Their robes are stone in the folds. Their eyelids are shut and smooth. It is said they still breathe. I sat an hour with one in the heat and held my hand under his nose, and felt nothing. When I stood I saw the dust on his lip was moving. Out, and in. Once in the hour.

The brother says Saint Calcifer was the first to sit, and sits still, far in, in the place called the Empty Socket, and is holiest of all because he has finished nearly everything. I asked what he had left to finish. The brother said: the Count.

**Why we count our fingers.** Everyone does it here, even the brothers. You lie under the rib at night and hold your hands up against the stars and count. The brothers count by the knuckle. Pilgrims count by the finger. The old walkers say it keeps you sure you are still yourself. A girl from the stilt-towns said it keeps you sure the Mother has taken none yet. The brother says the stars are her bones that have not yet come down, and that we hold our hands up so they can see how counting is done. He says ten is a good count, because it runs down to one, and one is holiest.

Last night I counted ten.

This morning the little finger of my left hand would not bend. The skin over the knuckle is white and smooth, and when I tap it with a nail it ticks, like the rib. It does not hurt. The brother looked at it a long while, and then he kissed it.

The Citadel stands on the edge of the white now, the great broken thighbone upright, with windows cut into its sides. Two days more. My water is gone. I am glad.

Tonight I will count. If there are eleven, I will tell no one.

## To the One Who Takes the Broom

*Set down in the hand of a sweeper of the Pale Order, fifty years at the Ossuary of Nine Stairs, and found folded inside a niche-tally in the fourth wall. The last lines are pressed hard into the page, as if written in poor light.*

Child. They will give you my broom. Take it in two hands, as you would take a bone. It has touched more of the Mother than any brother's lips.

Here is the work. Begin at the top of the stair, where the lamps are, and go down. Always down. Sweep toward yourself, never away; what is swept away is scattered, and what is scattered is lost. Kneel at each niche. Tie a cloth over your mouth. A brother may kiss a skull. Over dust he holds his breath.

Use the soft end. Gather into the pan of bone. Say the name of the niche as you gather, the name of the brother if he had one, the name of the bone if he had none: the long bone of the left arm, from the lowlands. Nothing goes up nameless.

What the dust is. I tell you what I was told. Everything that was ever the god is still dying, the stone and the stair and you and I, and dust alone has finished. So it is the holiest thing in the ossuary. Sit with a wall a long while and you will see it: a rib leans, a finger lies a nail's width over, the row of skulls dips like a roof taking snow. The dust keeps still. When my knees are bad I sit in it. It is the only stillness I have known.

The Feast of the Laying. Once a year every wall is counted. The brothers go along with their tallies and the Warden reads from the book, and the sweepers hold the lamps. I have held the lamp at forty-nine Feasts. The count rises. Every year it rises, past the dead we carried down the mountain. The Warden says "the Mother's gift," and we say it after him, and the notches for the gift are cut on the tally and kept out of the book. Once, when the tallier's hands shook, I cut them myself. Forty-one on one wall, that year. I said the words. I believe the words. I write them here so you will say them too.

The Pogost. When your pan is full you carry it to the Weighing, under the Eighth Stair, and a brother of the Ninth sets it on the scale and writes where we cannot see. Then, before first light, we climb. The Sky Pogost is cold past bearing. You will lift the pan and open your hands, and the Myriad will take it. The dust rises. Watch it until it is gone, and it will be a long time going. In fifty years I have never seen it come down.

My first master told me the sky over the Ossa was the palest on the Hide because of us. I believed him. It is paler now than when I was a boy. Lower, I think. On still mornings it hangs like a ceiling you could lay a palm against.

Now the small things. These I never reported. I report them to you, which is a coward's way. Pardon me.

The pan is heavier going up than coming down. That is my arms. I am old.

There is a room off the Sixth Stair, behind the long wall. Forty niches, and my own broom-marks in the dust of its floor. I know my marks; I lead with the left and draw in short. They are mine, and old, and I have no memory of the door. The door is plain grey stone, like ours. The niches inside are longer than ours. I swept it that day. I said the names. I knew none of them, so I said the names of the bones, and ran out of names for the bones.

When I pass with the lamp, the skulls turn to follow. A little. You will see it in your first year, and you will tell yourself it is the shadow moving. That is right. Tell yourself that. The brothers say the dead keep watch. I have come to think they watch the lamp, or what the lamp shows them of us. Down on the lowest stair, the one we climb on our hands, the long skulls never turn. They were facing me already.

And the stair. As a novice I counted the risers of Nine Stairs for a penance, every flight, and I knew the number the way you know your own teeth. I count them still, each morning, going down. I will leave the number off this page. It is higher.

Bear what is given. Keep what is laid. Let finish what is ready. Ask nothing of the Ninth Stair.

I have asked nothing. I have swept. Take the broom in two hands.

## The Lesser Catechism of the Kostyak

*The questions a novice of the Pale Order learns by heart before he is given his cord, copied from a lesson-book of the Ossuary of Nine Stairs. The copyist notes that two answers were scraped down to the vellum and one was ruled and left empty. A novice's hand fills the lower margin of the last leaf.*

Here begin the twenty-seven questions, which are nine taken thrice. The tallier asks. The novice answers kneeling, one breath to each answer.

**1.** Q. What is a man?
A. Two hundred and six, and a name that will be taken.

**2.** Q. Who takes the name?
A. The Hush, which unsays. It takes names one at a time, as it took the god's.

**3.** Q. And the number?
A. The number stays. The Hush has no mouth for it.

**4.** Q. Who bears the world?
A. The Bearing Mother, the Bone of the god. She holds the Hide as a frame holds a hide, and she has never once set it down.

**5.** Q. Where is she?
A. Under your knees. Over the stair. In your hands, when you hold a bone. Hold it in two. One hand is a grasp. Two hands are a bearing.

**6.** Q. What is bone?
A. The part that stays. The flesh is lent. The bone is kept.

**7.** Q. What is a bone out of its place?
A. An open wound in the world. We carry it home.

**8.** Q. What is the Laying?
A. The washing, the chrism joint by joint, the niche, the name, and the number after the name. Nothing goes into the wall nameless. Where the name is lost, say the name of the bone.

**9.** Q. Whence the chrism?
A. From the wells. The Mother gives it freely.

**10.** Q. How deep are the wells?
A. The Mother gives it freely.

**11.** Q. What is the root of a count?
A. Its figures, added together until one is left. Two thousand and sixty-one: nine.

**12.** Q. Why nine?
A. Nine keeps itself. Take any count nine times, and its root is nine. So nine ossuaries, nine stairs, and every prayer said nine times.

**13.** Q. What is the root of a man?
A. Eight. One short.

**14.** Q. What is a count that falls short of nine?
A. An open count. The tallier carries it behind his teeth until it closes.

**15.** Q. How does a man close?
A. *(scraped to the vellum)*

**16.** Q. Which is the more, a child or a man?
A. The child. He is born with more bones than a man dies with, and they knit, and are fewer. The fewer is the holier.

**17.** Q. What does the Mother seek?
A. The One Bone.

**18.** Q. What is the Great Count?
A. Every bone of the Mother, laid and numbered, from the first niche. Its running figure is kept on the Ninth Stair.

**19.** Q. Who reads it?
A. The Ossuarch, who goes down. Ask nothing of the Ninth Stair.

**20.** Q. When does the Count close?
A. At the Last Number, when every bone of her is counted. Then she rests. That word is for dust, and for her, and for nothing else.

**21.** Q. How near is the Last Number?
A. Nearer by every tally.

**22.** Q. What are the funerals?
A. The flesh to the high ledges and the cold: the first. The bones to the niche, anointed, facing the stair: the second service. The dust to the Sky Pogost at first light: the last.

**23.** Q. Who cut the stair?
A. We were here when she fell.

**24.** Q. What do the dead watch for?
A. *(the line is ruled, and empty)*

**25.** Q. What is dust?
A. The one thing that has finished. Everything the god was is still dying. Dust is done. A brother may kiss a skull. Over dust he holds his breath.

**26.** Q. Why is it given to the sky?
A. So that it may rise, and be kept above us, and touch nothing.

**27.** Q. What is the Rule?
A. Bear what is given. Keep what is laid. Let finish what is ready. Ask nothing of the Ninth Stair.

**28.** Q. Where does the dust go?
A. *(scraped to the vellum; at the foot of the scraping, the tail of one figure, which may be a nine)*

Here end the twenty-seven questions.

## On the Arithmetic of the Frame

*Written for the novices of his office by a tallier of the Ninth Stair, set down in the Order's books as Seven Thousand Two Hundred and Nine. The Codex copy was made from leaves bound round a niche-tally; the sums in the margins are his, and the last page is written very small.*

Novice. Before you learn a figure, learn why we keep them.

The Hush unsays. It took the god's true name first, and has taken names ever since. I have held the oldest book on the stair, and whole lines of it stand blank where names were written. The numbers beside them are still there, in the same ink, in the same hand. The Hush reaches for a number and finds nothing to hold. So every bone laid is given two things: its name, for the Mother, and its number, for the Count. The name is a kindness. The number is what lasts. In the book I am 7,209. Say it after me, and you will have said a thing the Hush cannot take back.

Now the reduction. Every count is carried down to its root. Add its figures, and add again, until one figure stands alone. Seven and two and nought and nine: eighteen. One and eight: nine. That is my root, and I thank the Mother for it every morning, nine times.

Nine keeps itself. Take any count nine times and it comes home to nine. Seven taken nine times is sixty-three: six and three, nine. Take a whole man nine times: one thousand eight hundred and fifty-four. One and eight and five and four, eighteen; nine. Nine is her root, and so there are nine great ossuaries and nine stairs, and we pray in nines, and the Count is kept on the Ninth.

The frame. Tie your cord tonight: two hundred and six knots, one for each bone of a whole man, and tell them in the dark until your fingers know the end without you. Then reduce it. Two and nought and six: eight. A man is an open count. He stands one short of the Mother all his life, and closes only in the niche, when his number is written beside him.

The fewer is the holier. A child comes into the world with near three hundred bones, and no two midwives agree on the number. Over a life they knit, two into one, three into one. Feel an old brother's hands and you will know he is nearer. Every frame walks toward the One Bone, and the Mother first of all. When every bone of hers is counted and knit, the Count closes. That is the Last Number. On that day she rests, and we lay down our tallies, and the sweepers set down their brooms. I weep to write it.

A tallier carries every open count in his head until it closes. I carry eleven. The long wall of the Fourth Stair: four thousand four hundred and seventeen, root seven; it wants two. The drawer of finger-bones from the lowland road: fifty-eight, root four; it wants five. The room the sweepers found behind the long wall of the Sixth, which no chronicle lists: forty niches. I have been given no count for it. I carry the forty. Four.

And the one that will not close. The Great Count itself stays on the stair. Its root every novice learns in his first winter, and loses his sleep over in his second. Its root is one. It was one when I came to the stair. Each Feast the tallies come up, the Count rises, I reduce it by the lamp, and it comes to one again.

Here is why. Last Feast the brothers laid one thousand six hundred and fifty-two dead: root five. The walls rose by two thousand and sixty-one: root nine. The Mother gave the difference, four hundred and nine: root four. Four and five. She gives exactly what our dead lack to close. Every year the rise keeps itself, and every year the whole stands one over. The Wardens teach that the one is the One Bone, showing through the Count before its time. I believe the Wardens. Say it with me.

The column. Beside the book lies a second leaf, where the gift is set down alone, year under year, in a hand I have never seen at work. A tallier may add across that leaf. He may never add down it. I was given no reason, and I kept the rule thirty-one years.

In the thirty-second, in the dark, on my cord, I began. The rows lengthen as they go. The first gifts were small: a wall of eleven, a wall of three. The late ones run to hundreds. I added down forty rows and stopped, because the sum had passed my own number, and every brother written in the book before me, and the leaf went on below the lamp, down the stair.

I did not reduce it. I will never know its root.

Keep your cord. Add across.

## At the Windlass

*The words of a Brother of the Well, written in the Order's books as Four Thousand and Fifty, spoken to a novice sent down to turn the windlass with him. The novice wrote them out that winter. The leaf was found in the vat-room of the Ossuary of Nine Stairs, set inside a cake of chrism, and cut free with a hot knife.*

Take the handle. Both hands. One hand is a grasp.

Now turn. Slow. You will turn until the rope goes heavy, and you will know it when it comes. Count the turns. Say them under your breath, and say them in nines.

The rope is fixed at nine hundred turns. Each Feast we splice in nine fathoms more, new hemp, tarred. The bucket still comes heavy at the nine hundredth turn. My master spliced. His master spliced. Keep turning.

There. Feel her. Lean on it now, and keep your feet.

Smell that? Warm. The shaft is cold for the first hundred turns, and after that the stone sweats. By the four hundredth your hands are warm on the handle, and the handle is warm through. The Mother keeps her heat at the bottom. I have drawn thirty-one years and never once brought up a cold bucket.

Here it comes. Amber. Slow. Look how it holds the light of the lamp and gives none back. Pour it into the vat. Gently. Now stir.

Always stir. Marrow left still sets like wax. An hour and it skins. A night and it is stone to the elbow. Brother Six Thousand and Three sat down by the vat on the long watch of the Feast and slept with his hand on the rim. His fingers were in it. We cut him out at dawn. The hand closed, after. It closed well. He has four fingers on it now, knit two into one at the knuckle, and he blesses with that hand, and it is the holier hand. The fewer is the holier.

Stir.

What is it for. You know the catechism. The bones. Joint by joint, thumb and two fingers, and a laid brother stands facing the stair and steps down when he is asked. A brother of the well sees them step. The first time, you give thanks. I still give thanks.

The lamps. Render the marrow over a low fire, skim it, pour it in the moulds, and you have the tallow of the deep roads. It burns slow and burns quiet. The skulls in the niches turn toward it. They turn toward the chrism lamps and toward no other flame. I carry one on the stair and they follow it, the whole wall, as a field of grass follows the wind. Hold your lamp steady on the stair.

The living. It closes any wound. Pour it in and the wound shuts. The place stays hard, and warm, and swells a little with the years. Look at my arms. That is the windlass, thirty-one years of burns and rope. Every scar knit. Put your thumb here. Warm, yes. Mine always are.

Keep stirring.

Now. The Fifth Well.

The Fifth is capped with a stone and the stone is sealed with chrism, and no brother draws there. The Wardens say the Marrow Pontiff keeps it. Eldest of all things in the ossuaries. He went down before the stair was cut. He has never come up. That is all the Wardens say, and they say it seldom.

I drew the Fifth once. Before the cap. I was younger than you are. I turned nine hundred and the rope stayed light. I turned on. At twelve hundred it went heavy all at once, and then it went slack, and then the handle turned in my hands the other way. Gently. Something was letting the rope down for me. Paying it out.

I held the handle. I said the Mother's names nine times.

It breathed up the shaft. Warm, like the vat. And it said my number. Four thousand and fifty. Slow, the way a tallier reads off a stone. Then it said a smaller one. Then smaller.

Then it said, *Kneel.*

I knelt. My knees were on the stone before I had thought. The rope ran out through my hands to the end, and the bitter end went down, and I heard it touch nothing for a long time.

The Wardens capped the Fifth that spring. They sealed the cap with my rendering. I stirred it myself.

Four and nought and five and nought. Nine. I have a good root.

I kneel less, now. My knees have knit. I bend them in the morning and they bend slower than they did, and warm, and I count what I can feel of them, and it is fewer. The fewer is the holier.

Keep stirring. Never let it stand. If you hear your number in the vat, stir faster.

Tomorrow we splice.

## A Letter from the Low Road

*Written by a knight of the Pale Order to a novice of the Ossuary of Nine Stairs, sealed with chrism instead of wax. It lay in the Warden's press, unread, until the Feast after the knight failed to come home. The seal still gives a little under the thumb.*

Brother Vasko,

The Rule allows one letter a season. I spend this one on you. Forgive the hand; I write by a lamp of marrow-tallow, and it smokes.

We are eleven days below the Barrens, in the barrow country. Twelve of the standing dead walk with me, two abreast, carrying the chest. I keep them to a walk. Nothing on the low road is worth hurrying toward.

You asked me once what the lifting is like. Here it is. You find the bone by the wrongness of the ground: a furrow that will lie flat for no plough, a hearthstone warmer on one side. You kneel. You say the name of the place it lies in, so the place knows it is being answered. You lift in two hands, slowly, as a man lifts a sleeper. Most come up easily. Some the ground keeps, and then you wait, and say the name again, and the ground lets go. Twice this season it let go with a sound like breath drawn through teeth. I wrote "resistance" in the day-book, and nothing more.

Then the chrism, joint by joint, thumb and two fingers. It is warm still, as the Order promises, three hundred miles from the wells. I have anointed forty-one since the Ossa. The flask is fuller than when I set out. I measured it against the notch I cut on the first night. I said the words. The Mother's gift.

The lowlanders hide their dead from us. At Kerrow Ford they had walled their grandmothers into the houses and plastered them over, and stood in the doorways swearing the houses were new. At another village the bones were broken small and worked into the clay of the ovens. A woman there told me Grandfather Dust had swept up her son, and begged me to go. I told her the Order has shut its gates to him. She looked at my plate a long while, and shut her door.

At the Shallows we met a procession of the flesh-houses, hooded, come for the same barrow. We kept the old terms. They take the meat; we take the frame. The Rule has me sit and watch them work, so nothing of ours goes into their baskets, and so I sat, with my dead in a row behind me. Their elder handed each bone across clean and wet, in two hands, which I had not looked for from them. At the last he held one back and weighed it on his palm, and said our Mother was heavier than theirs this year. I took it. A child's shin. It weighed as much as my forearm.

I saw him, Vasko. The one the Order calls the Ungated. On the ridge above the Shallows at dusk, with a column of the dead at his back, taller than ours, taller than any man. My twelve stopped. They turned their skulls to the ridge, all together, and stood, and I asked them three times. He lifted both palms, the way we brace at the Laying. Then he went down the far side, and my dead came back to me. I wrote "fog" in the day-book. His left shoulder was knotted and lumped, as if the carver's hand had shaken.

Now the thing I have kept from Brother Confessor. I give it to you, because by the time this climbs the Ossa you will be holding a lamp for the sweepers at the Feast, and those who hold the lamps keep things.

My left pauldron came to me from Brother Dobrin, whose name is cut on it in the Order's letters. I oil it every night. Since the Barrens a ridge has run along its edge that no carver made, and on the back of my left gauntlet there is a spur the length of a fingernail, curling toward the wrist. I file it down at night with the whetstone. By morning it is back. I keep the filings in a twist of cloth. I cannot tell whether they are dust, or whether I may carry them up.

And Dobrin's name has a letter more in it than it had. I count them each night, as I counted the stair.

The plate sits well. It sits better than it ever has. The left gauntlet I have kept on since the Shallows. It fits close.

Bear what is given. Keep what is laid. Let finish what is ready. Ask nothing of the Ninth Stair.

Tell the Warden I was diligent.

Radko, knight of the Kostyak, at the Shallows

## What the Order Took, and What It Gave

*From the village book of Cold Adit, an iron-working hamlet under the Ossa range, in the hand of its headman Wat Orrin. A pedlar had the pages from the headman's widow in exchange for a pot of chrism, and carried them down to the lowlands.*

The Order came on the ninth day after first frost, as it has come in every year this book holds. Seven knights in their white plate, and the standing dead behind them on the road, forty or more. You do not count those to their faces. I met them at the bridge with bread and salt, as my father did, and his.

What they took. Eleven bones from the Low Field, where the thaw heaved the ground and set them out in the furrows like stones. Nobody here knew whose. Then our own. They say a bone in soft earth lies out of its place, and in time they come for every grave we dig. We give them. Old Mag's man went up this year. She stood in her door and said his name after them the whole length of the road, and the last knight lifted his hand to her. They are courteous. I set that down. They are always courteous.

The tithe. One son from every ten households, each third year, by lot. This year it fell on Harl's boy Tam and on my sister's youngest, Benn. Tam went gladly. Benn went. I write their names so the book keeps them, since the village will not see them again. None come down. My grandfather's page tells of a woman of this village who saw her son among the standing dead on the road, knew him by the way he set down his left foot, and ran to him. The knights let her. He turned his skull to her and went on up. The book says she was content. That line is in a different ink.

What they give. A pot of chrism to each house that gave, and a pot to the headman for the pit. It is amber and slow, warm in a cold room, and it closes any wound. Dunnet was crushed under a fall at the Third Drift in spring. We poured it into his side and he was back at the face by midsummer. I set down also that the places it closes stay hard. Feel the knot on Dunnet's side. Feel the knot on my own hand. They give no pain. They swell a little with the years, as a cut in green wood does.

The funerals. From the ridge above the tips, on a still morning before light, you can see the smoke go up off the high peak: pale, a thread standing straight, and very slow. My mother called it the brothers going home. The children wave to it. Wave, I tell them. It does no harm to be civil.

Now what we have noticed. I write it plain, for whoever keeps this book after me.

The ore is paler. My father's iron came out of the Third Drift red-black, and the lowland smiths paid well for it. Now a white runs through it, a seam like marrow in a split bone, and it will not smelt. In the fire it goes to a lime, and the lime is heavier than the ore that went in. The men call it the Mother's vein and leave it in the rock. By spring the rock has closed over their pick-marks. Every spring the drifts are narrower. In my life we have cut the Third Drift three times, to the same width, in the same place.

The notch. My father cut a mark on the doorpost where the high peak stood from our step: a hand's width under the notch of the east ridge, so the Feast-day dawn came clean through. The peak stands in the notch now. The dawn comes late.

The sky. It hangs lower over the peaks each year, and paler. When the wind comes off the Ossa the linen on the lines takes a grey that will not beat out. The children write their names in the dust on the sills. I told them to stop. I could not have said why.

Last. This year, for the first time in the book, the eldest knight asked how many of us there are. Men, women, children, and the old. He wrote it in a book of his own. Then he took my hand in both of his, the way they hold a bone, and thanked us for the bread. At the bridge he turned back and said, "Bury deep this winter." I asked him why. He said the ground here is kind, and went on up.

Forty-one households. The bread was good. Bear what is given. I have cut it over the door.

## Of the Unfallen

*Written for his successor by a star-watcher of the Pale Order, keeper of the night-roof above the Ossuary of Nine Stairs, set down in the Order's books as Five Thousand and Thirteen. The leaves were found in the roof-house under a stone. The figures in the margins are his.*

Brother. You have come up to keep the roof after me. Let your eyes go dark first. Then look up.

Every cold point up there is a piece of the Mother. When she fell, most of her came down to the Hide, and the first brothers laid her where she lay. Some of her stayed up. It hangs in the dark still, uncounted, and every piece is a wound the Count must carry. We call them the Unfallen.

The roof is a frame of long bones with cords strung across it, nine by nine, eighty-one squares. You lie on the stone beneath and count one square a night, reduce it, and write the figure and its root on that square's leaf. Eighty-one clear nights to count the sky once. I have counted it thirty-three times, and it has never come out the same. Some squares are fewer. More are more. My master said the fault lay in the eye. I have made my eye very good.

When one falls you will know it: a white line drawn down the dark, gone before you have breathed. Do not cry out. Lay your hand on the cords where it crossed, read the angle off the frame, and go down the stair and wake the Seekers. Three brothers and three of the standing dead walk the line to where it ends. Twenty days, or a year.

What they carry home is always bone. White, colder than any bone of the niches, with no marrow in it, the ends broken clean. Once it was a thing nine of the dead carried between them, and no niche would hold it, and they cut a new one. We lay each on the Wall of the Come Down, with its number, and a name, which is the night it fell. The Wall stands at two thousand and sixty-eight. Root seven. It wants two.

The Wardens say the pieces come from every part of her, and so they do not fit one another. I have set two side by side under the lamp. Neither fits a bone of a man, or any bone the Seekers have brought. Each is shaped to fit something. And on the Ninth Stair they weigh more than they weighed on the road. The Seekers say so. The Wardens say the road is long.

One Seeker came back alone, and would not say where the line had ended. He said only that it was still coming down when they reached it.

I pray for them to fall. Nine times a night I ask the Mother to call her pieces home. When the last is down the roof will be dark, and the Count will close, and she will rest, and I may sleep. I have pictured that dark, with nothing in it. It is the most beautiful thing I know.

Some nights a point is gone that did not fall. You will look for the line, and there will be none. The Warden says those were never hers, and we do not write them. I keep them on a leaf of my own, under the stone. The dark where they stood is smoother than the dark round it.

On the lower stairs they say an Ossuarch can ask the Unfallen to come down early. The Rule says a bone comes down when it is ready. I did not believe it.

In my twenty-first year, counting the first square of the ninth row, I saw a man standing on the low ledge of the Pogost, where no brother goes by night. Very tall. No lamp. He raised both palms to the sky, as a man braces a ceiling, and they came down. Many lines at once, every one dead straight, all falling to him. They made a dry sound, like a floor of bones settling. The slope below him rang for as long as it takes to sing the Chant nine times. Then he turned his face up toward the roof. I lay flat on the stone until the grey came.

In the morning the Seekers went down with baskets. They brought up four hundred and nine pieces, and I wept, because four hundred and nine were down and would never hang again. That night I counted the square they had fallen from. It held the number it had held the night before. I counted it nine times. I have counted it every night since. It has never been fewer.

Keep your eye good. Count your square. Pray them down in their time. If you see a man on the low ledge with his palms raised, do not count. Go down the stair.

The leaf under the stone is yours now. When I began these pages there were seven on it. There are eight.

## What Rose in the Well at Old Upright

*Spoken by a pilgrim woman at the gate of the Ossuary of Nine Stairs, where she came asking to be written in the book. A tallier set it down on the back of a spoiled niche-tally. Where she said a thing he could not spell, he left a gap and marked it. The gate stayed shut; the leaf was kept.*

On the third night I had ten, and a knot in the smallest finger of my left hand, a hard warm seed under the skin, just at the joint. I told no one. The others kept to the ribs. I went where the ground sloped.

The altar is a slab laid across four knuckles, each as tall as I am. Letters on its face: OLD UPRIGHT. Under them, other letters, worn to a shine. Behind it, the well. No rope. No windlass. A lip of polished stone, knee-high to something taller than any man, and a stair cut down the inside wall, each step too deep for my legs. The air coming up was warm. It smelled of your lamps, brother, the sweet heavy tallow, and under that of a stair where a great many feet had gone down.

I set my bowl on the slab. My lantern I set beside it, and the flame leaned away from the well and stayed leaning. I said the Mother's names. I know three.

It came up slowly. Put it together yourself. I never could hold it all at once.

First the top of it. Long pale fingers, pressed tip to tip, rising out of the dark in a peak, the way hands are held for prayer, the way a crown is tall. Then more fingers under those, and more, joint after joint. I counted them, as a pilgrim counts. I had thirty. Then thirty again. Then I had lost my place, and the joints went on coming.

Below the fingers, where a face would sit, there was a bowl of bone, worn hollow, like the middle of an old step. Nothing looked out of the bowl. The bowl was turned toward me.

It stopped at the lip. Most of it stayed down. I know that, because the well kept breathing around it, warm, and slow, and a long way down.

The brothers call it the Marrow Pontiff. The salt-carriers call it the Herald of Bone, and walk wide of the knuckles. It was older than the altar. Older than the letters under the letters.

It spoke out of the bowl, and out of the stair, and out of my knees. Slow, the way your talliers read off a wall. The words were ours, set in an order nobody sets them.

It said, *Early.*

It said, *Has she fallen. Is it after.* I could not answer. It said, *After,* as though I had.

Then it counted me. I felt each number come down on a bone like a thumb laid there, from the crown of my head to the soles of my feet. Two hundred and six. A whole woman, I thought.

It said, *Open.*

Two and nought and six. Eight. I understand it now.

It said [*here she made a sound the tallier could not write; he gives it the width of three words*]. It said, *Carried. You are carried in her. Carried thing, in her.* Then, *Knelt, once. Inside. When she held.* Then, *Kneel, as you knelt.*

I asked what for. I had walked three days.

It said, *She has stood since before. She has never set it down. Someone kneels for her.* And after a long time, lower: *One bone. She sets down one.*

So I knelt. On the white ground, by the knuckle, with my face toward the bowl. It was warm under my knees. The well breathed.

It counted me again.

Two hundred and seven.

*Closed,* it said. *Nine.*

Then it went down. Joint by joint, the way it came, and the fingers last, still pressed together, and then there was only the warm air, and my lantern, still leaning.

I carried away my bowl and my lantern, and the knot in my finger. It is a bone now. I can move it under the skin. It is warm, the way your chrism is warm. My knees are warm too, and they bend slower in the mornings, and I have stopped minding.

That is why I came to your gate. Write me in the book, brother. Write me by my number. Two hundred and seven. A good root. I have a closed count. I am ready to be laid.

Hold out your own hand, if you doubt me. Count it.

*[She would give no name. The Warden came down to the gate and asked her to hold out her left hand, and he counted it, and he sent the tallier inside. The tallier writes that the Warden stood at the gate a long time after, with his own hands behind his back.]*

# IV. The Gilded Peak

*the Kinrei-shū · the Empty Hand* · works: 08, 14, 37, 39, 40, 15, 38

## The Stair, Counted Downward

*Set down from the words of a lamp-seller in a lowland market town, who was four winters a novice of the Gilded Peak. A copyist of the Codex bought a lamp from her at dusk and stayed while she closed her shutters.*

I sell lamps. Clay and brass, wicks of twisted linen, oil by the measure. Down here the miasma lies in the streets each evening, and people buy light like bread.

From the Peak you could see it below us, lying in the valleys like milk in a bowl. We chanted, and it kept to the low places, and the pilgrims thanked us.

The chant is one breath. You take the syllable as long as your chest allows, and then comes the silence, as long again. Novices counted the silences on their fingers so as to know when to breathe. We were taught the syllables were only fences, and the silences were the prayer. And we were taught never to listen to a silence alone. There was always a shoulder against yours in the hall, always someone breathing beside you. I thought it was kindness.

In my fourth winter I woke in the dormitory at the hour of the long silence. Everyone around me lay holding their breath, asleep, all of them, the whole row. So I held mine. And I heard the room the way a room sounds when someone has just come into it and stands by the door. I heard how near. I will say nothing more of that night. I left before the thaw.

My work was the gilding. I beat the leaf between sheets of pressed bark until a breath would lift it, and laid it on bells and lamp-cases. Gold in full noon gives back so much light that the thing beneath it goes away. To be looked upon and unseen: that was holiness.

Only the masters gilded the saints. Novices were let into the inner sanctum at dawn to wipe their faces with a dry cloth, because the dark tracks ran from their eyes each night. By evening the tracks were back. We were told the saints wept for the living, for us, for the valleys. Once I held a loose flake to a saint's cheek for my master, and the flake rose from the gold toward me, a hair's breadth, and settled again. My master said the sanctum has drafts. The sanctum has no window.

The Hush has two hands, and both are empty. Radiance, the open hand, erases with light: noon with no shadow, the gilded face. Absence, the closed hand, erases with dark: the swallowing palm, the gate shut over the sun. We learned them on our own fingers, open and shut, until we did it in our sleep.

Twice I went down with a bowl behind my teacher. We asked for names. A woman in this very town whispered her husband's name into it. The next season she sold us bread and asked whom we were praying for. I asked my teacher where the names went. He said, "To the open hand." Then he folded my fingers shut over my palm, and held them there until I stopped asking.

On the stair a teacher walks one step behind and below his novice, so that if the novice falls, she falls into him. His lantern burned black. His own teacher was in it, going out slowly. I climb the ladder to my loft and I still feel him there, one rung down. The night I ran, I went down the stair alone, and that was the first time in four years nobody walked behind me. I counted every step. There were more going down than I had climbed.

A pilgrim told me of the fall at my own counter. The standing dead of the Pale Order had gone up the stair for the bones, and by morning the courtyard was black glass and the sanctum doors stood open. I sold him a lamp and gave him the wrong change. That night I tried to say the names of everyone on the Peak, the whole row in the dormitory. I lost some partway. My teacher's name I had. Each year I have it a little less.

They say there are shapes in the glass, all turned toward the sanctum. I think of where my mat lay.

Now they speak of a barefoot beggar on the lowland roads, with a bowl and a black lantern, who comes at dusk and asks for a name. I close my shutters at dusk. I fear it is my teacher, come down the stair behind me at last. I fear more that it is one of the faces I wiped. And I fear he will stand where you stand now and hold the bowl out, and I will know which name he has come for.

Buy your lamp. Walk carefully. Between one lamp and the next I hold my breath for the length of one syllable, and I keep someone beside me.

## The Second Cup

*Set down from the words of a bread-woman of the lowlands, who gave a name to the bowl of the Gilded Peak. A copyist of the Codex lodged a winter in her loft, and wrote this on the back of a flour-tally the night before he left.*

They came down at dusk, when the miasma was settling into the lanes. You heard the bells before you saw them: small brass bells, and bare feet on the stones, and no voices. Three or four monks, thin as rakes, the gold on their lamp-cases catching whatever light was left. Children ran indoors. The old women went out with bread, because you fed the monks, whatever else you did.

They never asked for the bread. They held out the bowl, a plain one, black at the lip, and said the one thing they said. Put a name in it.

I had carried mine to the door for a month, the way you carry a stone in your apron. I remember that I was sure. I remember my hands were floury, and that I wiped them first, as if the bowl would mind.

You lean down and whisper into the wood. The name goes into the bowl the way breath goes into a cold morning: you see it, and then you see the morning. The monk laid his palm flat over the bowl, closing it, and bowed, and walked up the lane with the others. A small one walked behind him, a girl, one step down. I watched the bells go up the road until the miasma had them.

Then I went inside, and there were two cups on the table, and I could not think why I had set two.

That was the first night. I stood a long while with the second cup in my hand. It was warm. I drank from it, because it seemed a waste.

The forgetting has no edge. Where a thing is lost you can find the shape of the place it was. This has no shape. It is the gap where a tooth came out, and the tongue goes to it all day, and each time the tongue arrives it is surprised.

The house remembers more than I do.

There are boots by the hearth that come up past my knee. I cannot wear them. I oil them each spring.

There are notches cut in the doorpost with a knife. They climb to the height of my shoulder and then they stop. The lowest is at my knee. The knife is in the drawer. I have never cut a notch.

There is a hollow in the straw of the bed, on the side nearest the door. I sleep in the other side. I turn the straw and by the new moon the hollow is back.

At the table one stool is worn pale where a hand rested on it to rise. My hands rest elsewhere.

I think it was a man. Some mornings I think it was a child. The lamp-seller by the well says it was my husband, and says she knows because she was the girl who walked behind the monk. She told me this across her counter, and would not look at me, and gave me the wrong change. I have no husband. I have a ring I cannot get off over the knuckle.

Whoever it was, I wanted them gone. I remember wanting. The wanting is still here, whole, with nothing in it, like the bowl.

Every season since, I set two cups. I have stopped asking myself why. The second one is always warm.

This year, at the start of the cold, the beggar came. Barefoot, as they were. A shawl the colour of old blood. The bowl. Behind him, a little to one side, a lantern with a black flame, and the lane was lit by it all the same. The monks came in fours. He came alone, and there are no more monks. The Peak is glass.

He held out the bowl and said it. Put a name in the bowl.

I told him I had already given one. He waited. I told him I wanted it back. He waited the way the chant used to wait, the length of a syllable, the length of the silence after it.

I looked into the bowl. The wood was dark, dark past where the bottom should be. And from far down in it something breathed out, slow, the breath of a sleeper on the side of the bed nearest the door. I knew the breath. I knew it the way you know your own stair in the dark.

I shut my door on him. I sat with my back to it until the bells had gone.

I do not know if I was afraid he would take another name, or afraid he would give one back.

The notches are the same. The boots are oiled. Tonight I have set three cups, and I cannot think why.

## Thin as Breath

*Set down from the words of a lay goldbeater of the Gilded Peak, who mends pots now in a lowland river town. He spoke at his bench, and did not stop working while he spoke.*

I was never a monk. I ate. The monks were light, and I was the weight that kept their gold coming: a lay man in the beating-house at the foot of the sanctum stair, with three hammers, an anvil of the god's own bone, and a stone for a pillow.

You take the gold as the pilgrims give it: rings, teeth, a coin with the face worn off. You melt it and pour it to a bar, roll the bar to a ribbon, and cut the ribbon into squares the size of a thumbnail. Each square goes between two leaves of pressed bark, two hundred squares to a book, and you beat it. Half a day with the heavy hammer. Cut each square in four, and beat again with the middle hammer. Then again with the light one, until the thumbnail is as wide as your palm and a breath will lift it.

Hold good leaf up to the sun and the sun comes through it green. My master said that was the one colour the gold had kept back. I was proud of my green. Mine was the thinnest on the Peak. Laid on a bell, it took the tone into itself and gave the bell no weight.

The rule was Radiance. A gilded thing must be erased by light. In full noon you should look straight at a saint and see a brightness in the shape of a man, and no man. Any seam, any lifted edge, any place where the size shows through, and the eye catches on it, and a face comes back. A face coming back was a fault in the work. I was beaten for one, as a boy. I have made none since.

We laid it on size boiled from sap and ash. Never from bone. A brother of the Pale Order came once to buy our size. My master burned the pot after.

Then the burnishing. Each gilder had a burnisher: a tooth set in a handle of black wood, long as a finger and worn smooth as water. Whose teeth they were, nobody said. They were warm in the hand in winter.

For the saints I beat the leaf, and the masters laid it. Every dawn a novice wiped the dark tracks from their faces, and every few seasons the tracks had worn the gold thin at the eyes, and it was laid again. I thought it was the damp.

When old Master Enshō went quiet, they called me up to the sanctum myself. He sat in his niche with his hands open on his knees, in the posture of the open hand. The monks said he had gone into the silence, and I was to lay him while the shape held.

He was warm. I put it down to the lamps.

I laid the brow, and the cheeks, and the lids. The lids were hard, because they were soft. At the mouth I held the leaf out on my card, and it lifted from the card toward his lips, and settled back, and lifted again. A draft, said the monk beside me. I looked at the lamps. Every flame stood straight up.

I laid the mouth. I pressed it with the tooth until the seam was gone. It was the best work I did in my life. At noon the next day I went up to look, and there was only light sitting in the niche, in the shape of a man, and no man.

He wept that night, the same as the others.

What I did after: I went on. I beat leaf for eleven more years. I was good at it.

Only, from that day, into every book I beat I let one leaf go with a flaw, a pinhole small as a hair, at the middle. The masters never found them. At noon the light fills a hole that small. I saw to it that mine went to the saints, and I cut them so the hole fell at the mouth.

I was down in the valley for more gold when the Order climbed the stair. I went back once, as far as the courtyard. From the edge of the glass I saw the sanctum doors standing open, and the niches, gold in the dusk, and one of them empty. I counted from the left three times, and lost the count each time.

Now I beat tin for lamp-cases. It takes a face well. You can see yourself in it.

Leave the pot. Come back tomorrow. And if a barefoot man comes to my door at dusk asking for a name, tell him I have gone to the river. Tell him I did the mouth as thin as I could.

## The Bell with No Tongue

*Set down from the words of a bell-caster of the lower Knee valleys, who cast for the Gilded Peak for thirty years. A copyist of the Codex found him mending pots at a waystone camp and paid him in lamp oil to talk. He tapped the rim of every pot while he spoke, and listened to each one before he went on.*

Thirty years I cast for the Gold Bell. The Kinrei-shū, if you want their word for it. I had the pit and the furnace at the foot of the stair, where the clay is good and grey, and every spring a monk came down with a wax model and a bowl of gold leaf and one syllable.

That was the order. One syllable to a bell. Ah, or Hu, or a sound with no letter for it that the monk would sing me once, low, and I would carry it in my chest for a month while the mould dried. You tune a bell by shaving the inside, a curl of bronze at a time, strike and shave, strike and shave, until it says the one thing and nothing after. My father cast for them before me. He said a Peak bell had to die quick. Other bells hum a long while after the stroke. The monks wanted the hum shaved out of it. One stroke, a clean word, and then quiet all the way to the edges of the valley.

They rang each bell once a day. Once. Then they sat and listened to the silence it left, and the silence was the thing they wanted. The bell was only there to make it longer. I asked a monk once why he did not simply sit in the quiet without the bell. He looked at me as a man looks at a child who asks why we eat. Then he said you need a door to know you are in a room.

Here is the thing I cannot set down. The one time they let me up the stair, it was to hang the great bell in the courtyard. Nine men and a rope. When she was hung the old master put the striking beam in my hands, as an honour, and I swung it with my whole back. I felt her take the blow. I felt her shiver up through the beam into my teeth. The courtyard gave back nothing. No stroke, no hum. The monks all breathed out at once, pleased, the way men breathe out when bread comes from the oven right. I stood there with the beam in my hands and my ears ringing with nothing. Down at my furnace, a mile under, my wife heard her. She said it was the sweetest bell I ever made. She said it rang a long while.

The last bell they asked of me had no clapper. No tongue, no ring for a tongue to hang from. Tall and narrow, the mouth just so wide. The monk brought the size of the mouth on a cord, and I did not like the width of it. It was the width of shoulders. When the bronze was ready he came to the furnace with his bowl and held it over the crucible and tipped it. Nothing fell out that I saw. The pour weighed more than the metal. I weighed it twice. My father's scales, and they had never lied to me.

I did not tune that one. They told me not to. They said it already knew its syllable.

They carried it up gilded, and a year after, the dead went up the stair. You know that story. Glass in the courtyard. The doors open.

I let the furnace go cold after. Nobody wanted bells. Fourteen years it stood. Then one winter I raked the old ash to use for mould-clay, and there was a bell in it. Small as a cupped hand. Seamless. No sprue, no mark where any mould had closed. Gilded so thick I could not see my face in it, only light. And cold. In the ash, it was cold, and the ash round it was warm.

I did not cast it. I know every bell I cast. I know them the way you know your children's coughs.

It has no clapper. I have kept it wrapped in felt under my bench. Some nights, at the hour the Peak kept its long silence, I wake, and the whole camp is holding its breath in its sleep, and the felt is warm. I have never heard it ring. I only know when it has, because after, for a while, I cannot remember my wife's name, and then I can.

Tap the pot. Listen. That one's sound. That one is cracked; you hear it keep talking after. A good bell says its word and goes quiet.

I would sell you the little one. I have tried to sell it before. Nobody will take it, and I cannot say why I keep asking.

## Inquiry into the Bowls of the Gilded Peak

*The report of a lowland magistrate to his assize, on the begging of the Peak's monks in the parishes under the mountain. The copy in the Codex was made from his own draft, found among his effects. The gaps are his.*

To the justices of the assize at ————, by their magistrate, in the matter of the bowls.

I was charged to inquire whether the monks of the Gilded Peak, begging under the mountain, do injury that may be brought before a court. I took depositions in four parishes over one winter.

**First. The parish books.** Deposition of the clerk of Lower Stair, sworn. He keeps the book of births, marriages and burials. He showed me a burial entered whole: the date, the fee, the grave by the east wall. Where the name should stand there is a clean space. The paper there is whiter than the page around it, as paper is before a pen touches it. Asked whose grave lies by the east wall, he said a good one, well kept. Asked who keeps it, he said he does. Asked why, he wept, and could not say for what.

I counted the clean spaces in his book: thirty-one. On the second morning I counted thirty-three. The book is kept locked. I believe him.

**Second. The debts.** Deposition of a moneylender of the river parish. She holds forty notes, witnessed, under her own seal. On each, the sum is whole, and the debtor's name is whole. The debtors swear they never borrowed. She does not remember lending. They pay all the same, each quarter day, leaving the coin on the sill of her back room. They sleep badly until it is done. The coin is gone by morning. The room is locked and she holds the one key. I sat a night in that room with a candle, awake. In the morning the sill was bare.

**Third. The killing.** At the crossing in Harrow Lane, in autumn, a man was opened with a hay-knife before eleven witnesses at market. I took eleven depositions. All agree on the knife, the hour, the killer's brown coat, that he wiped the blade on his sleeve and walked home. All say he is known to them. None can give his name. Three pointed to the same door.

I went to that door. A man opened it, a plain man with a plain face. I asked his name. He gave it at once. I wrote it down. The page shows a clean space. I have asked him four times since, and have four clean spaces. He was civil each time. The court cannot try a man it cannot name, and I record that as the law stands.

The widow deposes that her husband put a name into the bowl in the spring. She does not know whose. She says he came home lighter, and sat by the door all summer, facing the lane.

**Fourth. The town.** For the winter past, the town of ———— had no name. Letters addressed to it came back to the posting-house sealed. The bearers swore they had walked the road to its end and found a town there, with smoke and a bell, and could not match it to the letters in their hands. The people baked and buried. Asked where they lived, they said here. The priest rang the morning bell and said it rang short, one stroke under. At the first thaw the name came back, whole, and nobody could say from where.

A monk had come down at the start of that winter, with the bowl, and a small one walking behind him. The priest says a child of the town leaned over the bowl. The child laughs when asked what it said, and the laugh is dry.

**Of the magistrate.** The law requires a witness who is also the inquirer to set down what bears on his own credit.

My clerk has kept these papers with me all winter. His board is entered in my book of expenses at a rate I agreed. His name is not entered.

In my own diary, in the ninth month, stands one line in my hand: *At dusk, at my door. Gave it.* I have no ninth month. I have the diary.

I have sealed this report. My ring is cut with two letters. By lamp and by noon, I cannot read them.

**Finding.** No injury can be brought before the court, for want of names. I find the lowlands quieter. I find that the clean spaces are whiter than the page, and that the whiteness is spreading at the edges, a hair each week, toward the words beside them. I recommend that nothing be done. I recommend that the monks be fed.

Your magistrate, ————

*[Here the draft ends. The copyist notes that the space left for the signature is whiter than the paper around it, and a little wider than it was when he began.]*

## Report of the Count Taken on the Gilded Peak

*A letter from a knight-scribe of the Pale Order to the Warden of the Ossuary of Nine Stairs, sent in the season after the fall. It lay bound among the niche-tallies in the Order's book, and was copied out for the Codex by a sweeper who had no leave to do it.*

To the Warden of the Ossuary of Nine Stairs, from the scribe of the fourth chapter, sent up the Gilded Peak to count.

**The stair.** One thousand and ninety risers from the lowest gate to the courtyard, cut into the knee of the Mother. Gold leaf on every seventh riser, worn through by bare feet. I counted going up and I counted coming down, and the two numbers agree. I record this because it is the only count in this letter that does.

**The courtyard.** Where the flagstones lay there is glass, black and smooth, flat as water in a bowl. It is warm under the hand, by noon and by night. I walked it heel to toe: forty-one paces across, forty-one deep.

In the glass are shapes, faint, as a face is faint in a dark window. They lie at every depth. All of them are turned toward the sanctum doors. Some stand. Some kneel. Our dead do not kneel, Warden, and I set that down without knowing what to do with it.

I counted the shapes twice. The first count was two hundred and six. The second was two hundred and nine. On the third morning I did not count them.

**The standing dead.** The chapter sent up two hundred and six, anointed joint by joint, as written in your own hand. None came down. There is no bone on the stair, in the courtyard or in the dormitories. I found the shapes in the glass, if they are ours. The three I cannot account for I have left out of the book, as we leave out the Mother's gift. I do not know whose gift they are.

**The inner sanctum.** The doors stand open, each leaf the height of three men. Words are scratched into the right-hand leaf. You have them from my first letter. I will not write them twice.

The saints stand in niches round the walls, as our dead stand, all facing the doors. They are gilded to the fingernail. No face can be seen under the gold at any hour. I tried them all. From the eyes of each run dark tracks down the leaf to the chin. I wiped one with my cloth. By morning the track was back, and wet. I have looked at that word a long while, and let it stand.

**The count of saints.** The Peak's own tally, cut in gold across the lintel, gives one hundred and eight. I count one hundred and seven niches filled, and one empty. There is gold dust on the ledge of the empty niche, and no other dust. Warden, you know I have never found a niche without dust. From the empty niche to the doors there are marks in the gold on the floor. The chaplain says a thief. The marks are of bare feet, and they go one way.

I counted the saints twice more and had one hundred and seven each time, and on the last count I had the sense of someone counting with me, half a breath behind, as a teacher of the Peak walks one step behind on the stair. I stopped at a number I no longer have.

**The lamps.** Beneath each niche hangs a lamp of the Peak on an iron hook. Every one is dark. The wicks are whole and the oil stands full. A lamp of ours goes out when its oil is spent. These went out full. The chaplain says such a lamp goes dark when the last of the name it burns is gone. I count one hundred and seven lamps. Beneath the empty niche, the hook is bare.

**What I heard.** On the third night I sat alone in the sanctum with a lamp of our own. The miasma stands a third of the way up the stair now, flat as the glass.

Warden, I heard a breath drawn in. One long breath, from all round the walls, from every niche at once, drawn the way the Peak's novices drew it, they say, before the syllable. No syllable came. Then silence, as long again. Then the breath again.

I counted the silences as I was taught to count a wall. I cannot give you the number. Each time I set it down it is a different number, like the old lines under the Ninth Stair. And in each silence there was a small sound, dry, as of gold leaf lifting a hair's breadth from a face, toward me.

I have written that the chapter sent up two hundred and six. I ask you to check it against your book. I find I have it only from this letter.

Your scribe of the fourth chapter, whose name you have. I ask that you write it back to me.

## Letter from the Last Abbot

*Written by the last abbot of the Gilded Peak in the final days before the dead of the Pale Order came up the stair, and carried down the mountain by a novice. She gave it to a lowland priest, who kept it folded inside a wooden bowl worn black at the lip until the Codex bought both.*

To the brothers who are below with their bowls, and to whichever of you comes up the stair first when it is over.

The Order's dead have stood at the foot of the mountain for nine days. In the long silences we hear them counting, a sound like beads told on a cord. On the tenth day, I think, they will climb. I send this down with the youngest of us. She is light, and quick on the old water-cut. Feed her. Do not send her back up.

Hear it once more, as I said it to each of you when you came to us with your hair still on.

The Hush is the god's rest. For an age the god held a space open in it, and held us inside that space, and it grew tired. The Hush came in to let it lie down. Everything on the Hide is a wound that has not yet been allowed to close. We are wounds too. We have tried to be clean ones.

The Hush has two hands, and both are empty. Radiance, the open hand, erases by light: noon with no shadow, gold in full sun, a face looked upon until no one can say what it was. Absence, the closed hand, erases by dark: the palm that swallows, the gate shut over the sun. Neither hand keeps anything. So we keep nothing. A bowl, a robe, in time a lantern. You learned the hands on your own fingers. Do it now. Open. Shut.

The chant is one breath. The syllable is a fence, and the silence is the field it marks. The valleys believe the syllable holds the miasma off our slopes, and they feed us for it, and I have let them believe it for forty years. I told you the silences are the prayer. I will add one thing now, since there is no time left for it to harm you. This year the silences have grown longer, and I have shortened nothing. The whole hall waits together for a syllable that is slow to come. Wherever you are, keep a shoulder against a brother's when you chant. Keep it there.

The names. Every name you carried up in your bowls I gave to the open hand, at noon, over the lip of the east stone. I kept no tally; tallies belong to the Pale Order, and they may keep them. Yet I will set down this much. An empty bowl should weigh what its wood weighs. Ours have grown heavier. I asked the open hand whether it forgets the names or holds them. The hand stayed open, and empty, and that was the whole of its answer.

You will be asked why we did not fight. Say this. The Order believes we gave their Mother's bones to nothing, and they are right. They believe a bone out of its place is a wound in the world. We believe a bone kept in its place is a wound held open. Both houses have told the truth, and so there will be blood on the stair. My brothers are too light to strike, and I would forbid it if they were strong. A blow is a name shouted. We have spent our lives unlearning how to shout. When the dead come, we will be in the hall, one breath each, and when each breath ends we will let the silence go on. They will cut us down and raise us before we touch the flags. That is their mercy. Let them have it. I am giving my name away.

My teacher's lantern is nearly out. There is a little of him left in it, a syllable or two. I will carry it into the hall. If it goes dark before they reach the top step, I will take that as leave.

Now the last thing, and I ask you to do it without asking me why.

When the stair is quiet, the first of you up must go to the inner sanctum before you look for me. Open the sanctum doors. Both of them, wide, and set a stone against each. Do not wipe the faces that morning. Do not count them. Do not stand in the doorway; stand to one side, with your back to the wall, and your eyes on your own open palm. Wait there until noon. Then take your bowl and go down, and ask for names as you were taught.

If someone walks with you on the stair, one step behind, where a teacher walks, let him. You were always my best at the bowl.

Open. Shut.

# V. The Polished Heart

*the Hollow Mystic · servants of the Soul* · works: 16, 17, 05, 43, 41, 42

## The Names Said Over

*Set down from the words of an old circle-keeper of the House of the Polished Heart, spoken in the empty lodge-yard to a copyist of the Pale Order who had come to count the lodge's bones. The copyist notes that the old man polished a small round mirror the whole while, and never once looked into it.*

Sit, if you are staying. Sit in the ring. The stones are worn where the knees went. Forty-one of us, and the Pir in the middle, and the names going round.

I kept the circle. That was my office. I laid the felt, I lit the one candle, I began the first name and I ended the last. Veiled Crone. Weaver. Grandmother Spindle. Knot. Tangle. You say them down the ring, mouth to mouth, until they come back to you from the far side and you cannot tell whose mouth they left. We sat so from dusk to the grey hour. By then no one is sitting. There is only the saying. That was the teaching: say her long enough, and remembering and the self become one thread.

She has four faces. The Spinner draws you out. The Weaver sets you in the cloth. The Unraveller takes you out again when your length is done. And the Hollow. The Hollow is the eye of the loom, the empty place in the middle of the weave, where the pattern shows through. Every Tithed life goes back through that eye at the end, and is woven again. I have said it at more beds than I have years.

Our work was the glass. A dying thread gets lost; it wants to see where it goes. So we polished. Felt and ash, one of her names to every stroke, until the glass gave back the pattern and the thread could read its way. Then, at the death, cover every mirror. Grey felt, a stone on the corner. A soul that meets its own face stops to look. Covered, it goes on by, through the eye, into the cloth.

The Pir's Coat hung on its post by the door. Iron and small round mirrors, every Pir before her sewing on more, until it weighed what two men weigh. The foundling polished it. Twenty years, that boy. Quiet. Good hands. I gave him his first felt.

Then the winter. A whole valley came loose at once. We felt it in the circle before the runners came: the names went round and came back short, as though someone in the ring had stopped saying them. Nobody had.

The Pir put on the coat. She sat in the middle and looked into the Hollow, and we said the names around her for forty nights. I kept the count. I kept it right. On the fortieth she spoke. I will give it to you as she gave it, and I will not help you with it.

*"There is no loom. It goes on and on, and it never finishes."*

Then her hands opened, and whatever she was holding ran out of them, and she was lighter than the coat.

I turn those words over every night. A loom has an end. A cloth comes off it and is folded. If the cloth never comes off, then all those beds, all that felt over all that glass, so they would go on by. On by into where. There. You see. That is the thing I cannot say in the ring, so I say it in the yard, to a stranger, with the candle out.

The dead came home that winter. They stood in the doorways asking which way. And the boy took the cloth off the coat. Every mirror at once. Each of them met its own face, and stopped.

He broke the first rule of the house. He kept them. He keeps them still, going round and round him the way we went round the Pir, each with its thread hanging loose behind. The villages pay him for it. They call him Needle-Brother, and turn their glass to the wall when he passes.

I say he is a thief of the dead. I say it every morning, with the first name. He took them off her road and hung them on himself like beads.

And I say, at night, with the last name: he was the only one of us who heard her and did not cover it.

Perhaps the Pir saw wrong. Forty nights is long. The lodge counted forty-one faces in her mirror. She counted more.

This is one of them. I polish it. One of her names to every stroke. Some nights I count forty-one, one for each of us who sat in the ring. Some nights I lose the count, and there is one more at the back, very small, and it has my face, and it is waiting to see whether I will cover it.

I keep the felt beside it. I have not decided.

## The Grinder's Slates

*Workshop notes of a mirror-maker of the lodge valley, scratched on the backs of his order-slates. The slates reached the Codex in a crate of broken glass that a tinker bought for the tin.*

Every mirror on the Pir's Coat came off my wheel, or my father's wheel, or his father's. Set that down first, so whoever reads this knows whose hands these were.

**The glass.** Green glass from the furnace-town, the one pane in six without a seed in it. Cut the round with the wheel and the tooth. Grind on the stone with water and grit, coarse, then fine, then finer, until the thumb finds nothing. Then pitch and rouge, and the long hours. The lodge wanted every glass hollowed a little, a dish as shallow as a cupped palm. My father said a flat glass hands you back and a hollowed one draws you in. I have ground four hundred and some. I believe him.

**The silver.** Tin beaten thin as a fingernail paring, laid on the slab. Quicksilver poured on and worked in with a rag until the leaf goes bright and soft. Slide the glass over, weight it, tilt it, and leave it nine days to weep off what it does not want. The lodge had me seal every back with ash-wax, and the Pir pressed her thumb into the wax before it set.

**The orders.** A slate came down each spring: so many rounds, of such a width, and one of the Crone's names to be said at each turn of the wheel. I said them. A customer who pays for a word over the work gets the word. Pitch, rouge, Spinner. Pitch, rouge, Weaver. You lose count of the names long before you lose count of the turns.

Each spring the slate asked for more rounds than the spring before. I thought the lodge was growing. The lodge stayed the size it was. The Coat grew.

**The rule.** They taught every house in the valley: a soul going out halts before its own face. Cover the glass, and the dead go on by, down through the eye of the loom, to be woven again. I believed it as I believe quicksilver runs downhill. The lodge folk were the ones who had looked.

I keep one question on this slate, so it stays out of my mouth. What does a dead man see, passing, in a hollowed glass, sewn on the breast of a woman who is staring into the Hollow?

**The last order.** Autumn, the year the valley came loose. Seven small rounds, and one great round for the heart, a hand across. The slate said: deeper than any before. I ground it eleven weeks. The Pir came down for it herself. She held it to her breast, over the coat, and said it would do. Then she said, *Grind no more for us*, and paid me for three years.

They say a lamp burns now where my heart-glass was sewn. Nobody has brought the glass back. I would know it by its weight in the hand.

**What they ask for now.** Since the Needle-Brother walks the lanes, the valley turns its glass to the wall. So I make backs. Boards on hinges. Felt lids sewn to fit. A carter paid for a glass that shows nothing; I frosted him one with sand, and he went home glad. The widow from the upper valley wanted one hollowed the lodge way, to stand on her sill facing the lane. I made it true and charged her half.

A man in a grey hood came at dusk and asked for a large glass. I hold up the test-glass for every buyer, and I held it up for him. It gave me the shelves, the door, the lamp on the hook. He thanked me and asked the price. I told him it was spoken for. I sweep the grit off the floor each night, and that night there was nothing in it to sweep.

**The faces.** A glass ground true gives one face. I know my trade. Still, when the lamp is low and the silver has only just set, I see my own face, then one behind it, then one behind that. My father counted three in his, and called it the depth of the glass doubling back. I counted nine in the spring. In the last round I finished, the one the lodge never sent for, I counted till the lamp went out.

They wait behind me, turned the way I turn, all waiting to be asked which way.

I have hung a cloth over it. Tonight I heard the cloth move, the small sound felt makes against glass when something on the far side draws near to read it.

I left it covered. Tomorrow I will lift it and count again.

## The Stitch at the Throat

*Set down from the words of a widow of the upper valleys, spoken to a copyist of the Pale Order who had come for her husband's bones and went home without them.*

When Oswy died I covered the mirrors. Everyone does. My mother did it for my father: grey felt over the glass, a stone on the corner so the cloth won't slip. The lodge folk taught us that, back when the House of the Polished Heart still stood up the valley and sent its people down with their felt and their ash. They said a soul going out must never see itself, or it will stop to look. So you cover the glass, and the dead go on by.

I had felt on the little mirror, on the water pail, on the window, because a window at night gives your face back. I sat with him three days. On the third day I could not bear it.

The Needle-Brother came at dusk. You know him by the sound first. Small glass knocking on small glass, all down his coat, like a cupboard of cups when a cart goes by. Then the lights. Then him, long and grey in the doorway, with his lantern hanging in the air at his shoulder and no hand holding it.

I paid him the salt and the good knife. He asked for nothing. He took them.

He looked at the covered mirrors before he looked at Oswy, one and then the next, the way a man counts doors. Then he sat on the floor by the bed, as the lodge folk sat in their circles, and said a name I did not know, and another, very low, over and over, until I lost the count.

The needle is as long as my forearm. The thread is pale. I would call it light, if light could be knotted. He put one stitch through the cuff of Oswy's sleeve and drew it to the bedpost. Then he put the needle in at the throat, just under the jaw, where Oswy used to hum, and pulled it close, and tied it.

That is all it is. A stitch at the throat.

He was kept forty days. I know what your brothers say of it, so I will say it plain. He never got up. He never ate. But the room stayed warm around him, and his hand stayed a hand when I held it, and at night, if I lay close, I could hear the thread: a small sound, like a string in a wind that isn't blowing. I talked to him. I told him about the roof and the price of lamp oil. Once, near the end, he turned his face toward the covered window, and I almost pulled the cloth. I left it.

On the forty-first morning the knot hung loose and he was cold as the floor, and I knew. I washed him. I laid him in the barrow, under a lid dished in like all the others. I was grateful. I am grateful still. I want that set down.

This next part I have told nobody but my sister, and she told me to hold my tongue.

The Needle-Brother came back through the valley at harvest. Half the village turns its glass to the wall when he passes; the other half comes out to look. I went up the lane. The wisps go round him the way sparks go round a fire, pale, each with a little tail of thread behind it. Some say they sing. I would call it asking.

I counted them. I have counted loaves all my life, and I count well. I counted twice. There were more than in the spring. More than our valley has graves.

One of them kept low, near his shoulder, and every little while it dipped, the way Oswy ducked his head under our lintel, though the lintel was high enough and he knew it. Its thread was short, and hung in a loop, tied off.

I said his name. Very quietly. The wisp stopped. I can't tell you how a light turns, but it turned, toward the Needle-Brother's coat, where all the little mirrors are, and it stayed there, looking.

The Needle-Brother looked at me then. I have thought about his face a long while. He looked like a man who has been asked the way and has no answer, and is too kind to say so.

The lodge up the valley is empty now. Felt blows about the yard. The old women say its people used to send the dead on, through the eye of something, to be woven again. They say the Needle-Brother is the one who stopped sending. The old women don't say why.

I took the felt off the little mirror. It stands on the sill by the door, facing the lane. If Oswy comes home asking which way, I want him to stop here.

## Leaves from the Lining

## Which Way

*Set down from the words of a flax-beater of the lodge valley, at the bench outside the ford inn, by a copyist of the Pale Order who was told to buy the ale first and did. The copyist notes that the inn's window was chalked over from the inside.*

You want the winter. Everyone who comes through wants the winter.

It came on in the week the ford froze. My sister's man felt it first. He stood up from his soup and said, "Something's let go," and sat down again, and never could say what. By morning the whole valley had it: a slackness, like a bowstring cut. Then the dead came.

All of them. That's the part the lodge folk never tell right. Everyone the valley ever put in the barrows, old and fresh together. My wife Hild, two winters gone. My boy, the autumn before, who was four. My mother. Her mother, that I never knew, and knew at once by her jaw. And behind them the old ones, so old they had no faces to speak of, only the shape of someone standing.

Nobody saw them walk in. They were in the lane, the way frost is on a pane. They stood at every door and asked which way.

That was all they said. Which way. My boy said it, in Hild's voice, which he never had. I think they'd lost their own voices somewhere and were using whatever was near.

Now. Three generations we paid the lodge up the valley for felt and ash, and what we bought was one lesson: in a house of death you cover the glass. A soul that sees itself stops to look. So we covered. Every mirror, every pail, every window. The women tipped the water out of the troughs. We laid planks on the well. And the ford was ice, and ice gives you back your face, so we went out in the dark and threw ash on the ford by the shovelful, and on every puddle in the lane, till the valley was grey as a hearth.

We did it right. I want that set down. We did it right, and they stood at the doors all the same, and asked.

We told them: up. Up the valley, to the lodge. That's where the lodge said they went, through the eye of something, to be woven over. So they went up, a long grey file of them in the ash, and we went behind, because what else. My boy walked with his hand out to the side, the way he used to hold mine.

The lodge gate was open. The ring of them sat in the yard, and the old woman in the middle wearing the great coat, and she wasn't moving. The dead stood round the wall and asked which way, and nobody in there answered.

Then the polisher came out. A boy, near enough. The foundling, with the grey hands. We knew him from market. He had the felt that hung over the coat's glass in his fist, and he'd pulled it. I saw the coat then. Hundreds of little round mirrors, stitched on.

Every one of the dead looked at once. I heard it. Like a breath drawn in by a whole ring of people at once. They stopped.

And then they were small. Lights. Pale, low to the ground, each with a thread off the back of it like the end of a yarn off a spindle. They went round him. They're going round him still.

He walked out the lower gate with all of them, and the coat came after. I'll say no more of the coat. Folk here say plenty.

That's the winter.

What came after is worse. Half this valley says he stole them. That the lodge had been sending our dead on for a thousand years, and he stopped it for his own use, and now they hang on him like a pedlar's bells, going nowhere, and he sells stitches to widows out of our own graves. They keep their glass covered. They chalk their windows. They spit at the ford.

The other half says he saved them. That the old woman in the coat saw something up there and said it and died of it, and whatever the lodge was sending them into had no loom at the end of it. They've took the cloth off. They set mirrors on the sill, facing out, in case.

My daughter's in that half. She has a mirror at her door. I have chalk on mine. We don't eat together.

I'll tell you one thing I've told nobody. My mother's mother, the one with the jaw. She was blind all her life, and blind dead. When the polisher pulled the felt, every one of them stopped. She went on by the coat, and through the far wall of the yard, and on up the hill in the dark. Asking.

Nobody stopped her. Nobody's seen her since.

Ask your brothers where she's got to. Ask them which way.

# VI. The Precious Wound

## Litany of the One Who Was Away

*Written by a penitent of the Brotherhood of the Precious Wound, who was on pilgrimage the year the wound scabbed. Found rolled tight inside a tithe-cup on the lip of the wound at Villa Llaga. The ink is brown, and there was no ink in the town.*

Our Lady of the Open Side, hear me.
Red Mother, hear me.
Mother of the Wheel, She Who Turns, hear me, for I am the one who was away.

I left in the spring with my cup wrapped in cloth, to carry a measure of our blood down to the Fen of Shog-Mire, where the mud keeps her beat. That was my penance, and my pride. I remember the procession that sent me off. Forty hoods, the scourges going in time, the gutters running with us. The sangrador walked at the back, as he always did, with his sleeves down and his arms dry. We sang her names as we went. Maiden. Mother. Nurse. We sang them in the old order, and the wound drank.

I was gone a year and some weeks. The stilt-folk told me the lamps in Villa Llaga had gone out one by one. I did not believe them.

Hear me, Lady.

The road home was quiet. I came in by the lower lip, past the brothers' rooms. Every door stood open and every peg was bare. The robes were gone. Forty pegs, forty robes, and mine gone too, though I had worn my travelling hood and left my good one hanging. The gutters were dry and brown. On the lip of the wound the tithe-cups stood in a row, one for each brother, set out neat as for the Day of the Measure. They were empty and clean. Someone had licked them clean.

The wound was open again. Along its edges lay the black crust in plates, curled up at the rims like bread. I picked one up. It was warm, and underneath it was ridged, the way the roof of a mouth is ridged. I keep it against my chest at night, and I am ashamed of how it comforts me.

I found no brother. No bone of a brother. Let the Pale Order come and count.

Hear me, Mother, for I must tell you of your faces.

In the Maiden's niche over the crypt stair, the old carvers gave her a body and never gave her a face. The stone there was left rough, and each of us kissed it on the Friday, and each of us saw her as we wanted. My mother saw her young. The sangrador, who was found in that niche as an infant, told me once that he saw nothing there at all, and that he liked her better for it. I thought it a blasphemy. I think now it was the truest thing said in Villa Llaga.

The niche is empty. The body is gone from it. There are marks in the dust of something dragged.

On the ninth night I heard singing, and I went up to the lip with my cup, because I am a fool, and because it was the right tune.

They came down the middle street in procession. More hoods than we ever had. The hoods were ours; I knew the patches. Under them they were small and they were many, and they moved the way we moved, the sway, the stop, the sway, and they did not go anywhere. They sang with no words in it. Behind them came the great one, sewn together, taller than the crypt door, and on its back, lashed on with rope, it carried her. The body from the niche.

She has a face now. It is his face.

He walked at the front. He walked at the front, Mother, where the eldest walks. His sleeves were up. His arms were open from shoulder to wrist and there was no blood on the street where he stepped. As he passed the lip he looked at me, and at my cup, and he smiled like the boy from the niche. His mouth moved. The voice that came out was a woman's, and very kind, and it said my name, which he never knew.

I did not follow. I stood with my cup until the singing went down into the crypt, and then I went back to my room and lay on the floor.

I have given every year. I have given in the old order and in the right measure, with the words. I do not know now who heard the words.

Maiden, hear me. Mother, hear me. Nurse, hear me.
Whoever you are now, hear me.

I will fill the cup once more, as is right. I will leave it on the lip. I will leave my hood on my peg.

If they come back for the last robe, it will fit.

## The Rule of the Precious Wound

*The ordinances of the Brotherhood of the Precious Wound, copied from the chained book in the chapter room at Villa Llaga. The last leaves are in another hand, in brown ink. The copyist would not set his pen to them, and wrote them down afterward from memory.*

**Of the Tithe**

I. Each brother shall give to Our Lady of the Open Side one measure of his blood in the year. The measure is the tithe-cup of beaten tin, filled to the line.

II. The cup is filled on the Day of the Measure and on no other day. Blood let before its day is spilled, and spilled blood is kept back from her.

III. The brother shall pour slowly, from the lip of the cup to the lip of the wound. While he pours he shall speak the Words, and name what the blood is for.

IV. *[The clause is scraped from the leaf. In the margin, in the old hand: Kept in the Elder's mouth.]*

V. Where the wound asks more, the line on the cup is raised the width of a thumbnail. The Elder cuts the new line on the eve of the Measure. The wound has asked more in every year that is written in this book.

**Of the Processions**

VI. The brothers walk hooded, forty to a procession, in the old order: the eldest at the front, the youngest behind him, the sangrador behind all.

VII. The sway, the stop, the sway. No brother shall walk ahead of the singing.

VIII. The scourge is laid on in time with the song, over the shoulder, left and then right, nine strokes to a verse. The gutters shall carry it down to her. What the stones keep, the stones may keep.

IX. A procession goes out from the chapter door and comes home to it. It shall have a place to go.

**Of the Sangrador**

X. One brother holds the office of sangrador. He opens the welts with the small blade, binds them after, and keeps the tally of what each brother has given.

XI. The one who opens others never opens himself. His veins stay shut all his life. He walks with his sleeves down and his arms dry.

XII. Should the sangrador bleed by any chance, by a thorn or a fall on the stair, the brothers shall cover the place at once, and wash the ground with water from the upper well, before she knows it.

XIII. The reason for the eleventh clause is given to each sangrador on the night he takes up the blade, and to no one else.

**Of the Niche**

XIV. The Maiden stands in the niche above the crypt stair. Her face is left in the rough, as the first carvers left it. No hand shall finish it.

XV. On the Friday each brother kisses the stone, and says nothing of what he saw there.

XVI. Whatever is found in the niche belongs to her, and shall be raised by the brotherhood as its own.

**Of the Crypt**

XVII. At night the saints come down. The brothers go down the stair to meet them, two by two, with candles.

XVIII. The dead of the brotherhood are laid in our own wall, each with his robe on the peg beside him. No brother of the Pale Order shall enter the crypt, nor count what lies there.

XIX. When the saints are down, a brother shall answer to any name that is spoken to him, and shall answer kindly.

XX. The brothers who go down are counted, and the brothers who come up. Where the two counts differ, the Elder shall say which one is right.

*Here the old hand ends. What follows is in brown ink, in a hand that leans the other way, set down after the year the wound scabbed. There was no ink left in the town.*

XXI. The measure is all of it.

XXII. The fourth clause is restored. The Words are spoken now at the front of the procession, by one who knows what the blood is for.

XXIII. The eleventh clause is struck through. The one who opens others has opened himself. There is room.

XXIV. The niche shall hold a face.

XXV. The robes hang on their pegs, and shall be taken down from their pegs. A brother who comes home from pilgrimage shall find his robe waiting, and it shall fit.

XXVI. The procession goes out, and goes out, and goes out. It needs no door.

XXVII. Where the counts differ, the Elder shall say which one is right. There is one Elder.

XXVIII. The saints come down at night. They stay down.

XXIX. She hears.

## The Sermon of the Laden Board

*Preached on the Feast of Second Helpings by a priest of the House of the Laden Board, one of the fattening houses that serve the Red Mother. Taken down by a clerk of the Pale Order who had come to bargain for bones, and who wrote in the margin: "He wept twice. Both times he meant it."*

Sit. Sit down, my sweet ones, and eat. Nobody at this table goes hungry tonight, and that is the first of her mercies.

I will speak of our Mother, and you will chew while I speak. Chewing is a prayer. It is the one prayer she has always answered.

She is the Flesh, the part of the god that goes on growing after the rest of it has lain down. She has no mind, and I praise her for it. A mind picks. A mind weighs a gift and finds it short. Our Mother refuses nothing. Give her a cup and she drinks it. Give her a year and she hands it back to you swollen, warm, a hundred times over. The Peak called her the Glutton, in the days when the Peak still had mouths. Glutton. The word is honey to me. What is a glutton but one who always has room?

Below this floor are the given. You hear them. Do not be ashamed of hearing them. We keep them warm and fed, better fed than they ever were in the lowland camps, and every night the Nurse's cup goes down the stair empty and comes up full. We ask them nothing. We say nothing over them. We feed her the way a woman feeds an infant: in the dark, without a word, one hand cupping the back of its head.

Why the given, and never ourselves? Because she has us already. Every stone and every crawling thing on the Hide is her own meat come back to the plate. The given are Tithed, the one dish she has never tasted to the bottom. Watch how she leans toward them.

Now the pale brothers. One of them sits at the back of this hall with his ink and his notched sticks. Welcome, brother. Eat something. You look cold.

The Pale Order says the dead belong to the frame. Let them have the frame. When you finish a joint you leave the bone on the plate, and we are a courteous house. But they come too early. They come while the meat is still on. They anoint a man in his own flesh and carry him up to their high ledges, and the wind eats him, and the Mother goes without. The Bearing Mother is a frame, brothers. A frame is a table. Our Mother is the meal set upon it, and the hunger at it.

And now Villa Llaga. I will speak gently. They were our cousins at the same board once.

The Brotherhood of the Precious Wound gave their own blood, in measure, and sang her names over it. We called that vanity, a fond vanity. They wanted her to listen. She drank every drop and heard no word of it. Then the wound closed for a year and opened again, and she gave back what she had been given, generously, as she always does. Her children ate the town. Men call that a horror. I call it a nursing that went on a little long.

Then the boy from the niche, the one with the dry arms, drew a door on her and went in, and told her what to make.

Mark me. This is the one sin. Feed her anything. Feed her your own children if the year is lean. Never tell her what to be. He gave her a road to walk. He gave her a face, and it was his. A mother who is told what to make will learn to choose, and a mother who chooses will one day choose the plate. We cut his name out of the Board's book with a knife and fed the leaf to the given. If his brood comes to our door we will feed it. If he comes, we bar it.

One thing more, and then the sweetmeats.

On the last feast I carried the cup down myself. The given were humming. All of them, in every pen, the same tune. It was the old processional of Villa Llaga, with her names in the old order: Maiden, Mother, Nurse. Not one of them has been south of the Moor. I stood on the stair a long while and listened, and it was beautiful. Then I filled the cup, as is right.

Eat, my children. She has room.

Be quiet a moment and you can hear her swallow under the floor. Lately she swallows in time.

*In the clerk's hand, below: The floor of the hall was warm. I did not eat. After, when I named the count of bones the Order would take this year, he laughed and said, "So many? We never sent you half of that."*

## What the Mire Is Told

*Set down from the words of Asheth, leech-wife of the Kettlewick Stilts, spoken to a pilgrim of the Precious Wound while she bled him of the marsh fever. He wrote them on the back of his tithe-tally when his hand came back to him.*

Sit. Give me the arm. The other one. That one has given enough.

You feel them stir in the jar? They know a brother's blood before the lid is off. Everything in Shog-Mire is hers, pilgrim: the mud, the reeds, the tallow in the lamps. My leeches are the smallest of her, the little mouths she sends up the stilts to ask. I keep forty in the jar. The count changes while I sleep, and I let it.

Now hold still between the beats. You felt it on the causeway. The mud takes your ankle and lets it go, takes and lets go, all day and all night. Your brothers write that the mire keeps her heartbeat, and that is true enough for a book. Out on the Heartbeat Flats you learn the rest. A step put down on the beat is a step lost. The mud closes on you the way a mother's hand closes on a child who wants to go. So we built on stilts, and we time our walking, and a Kettlewick girl learns to count the beat before she learns her name.

The beat was slower when I was a girl. My mother said so, and hers. It quickened the year your town went dark. Your brothers do not like me to say it, so I say it to the leeches.

There. The first one has taken. The heat goes up your arm and the weight in your chest loosens. That is the healing. The bad blood goes out to her, and the mire drinks it, and the mire is never full. My trade is the letting and the pouring. I pour your blood off the edge of the stilts and I say what it is for. That part matters most. You have your cup and your Words. We have ours. They are older, and shorter.

Your pilgrims come down the causeway every spring with the tin cups wrapped in cloth, singing. They ask me the way to the Brood-Banks, and I tell them, and they pay me in coin, and I take a little of their measure besides, for the jar. The Brotherhood calls that theft. The mire calls it the fee. They go out and pour their tithe on the banks and sing her names over it in the old order, and come back lighter, and I bleed them again for the fever.

Mostly they come back.

The banks are long grey ridges in the Blood Delta, soft as a cheek. The eggs lie in them close as teeth in a jaw, each with a dark knot turning slowly in it. I have laid my ear on the banks at night. Under the beat of the mud there is another beat, quicker, many of them, all nearly together.

Listen. The Flesh grows the way fire goes along a thatch. It has no aim. It goes wherever there is more to take. Give it blood and it makes more of itself. Give it blood and a word, and it makes what the word says. Give it blood and no word at all, and it makes children for a mother with no head. That is what the Maiden's children are. Blood with nobody's name on it.

Your Rule has a clause scraped off the leaf. Every leech-wife in Kettlewick knows what it said. We speak it into every pour. I will tell you when you are older.

Last spring a line came down the causeway in your robes. Forty hoods. They sang, and the tune was right, and the words were only sound, the way a child sings a tongue it has heard through a wall. They walked the sway, the stop, the sway. They did not stop at my stilts. They did not ask the way to the banks. They knew it. The lamps guttered on the beat as they passed, and stayed guttered between. I pulled the ladder up that night, and so did every house in Kettlewick. In the morning there were forty sets of prints on the causeway going out and none coming back, and the mud had held on to none of them.

Past the Ziggurat, in the Blood-Basin, something lies in the red water and takes everything the Delta sends down. The old stilt-folk call it the Mother. I call it the one who is learning. Every year the songs come up out of the mire at night a little truer.

There. You are pale, and you are well. Pay Lugh for the jar; I only take blood.

And pilgrim. When you pour your cup on the banks, say what it is for. Say it aloud, and say it to the end. Out there something is listening for a word it can wear.

## The Carver's Account

*Written in carpenter's black on the backs of nine commission-bills of the Brotherhood of the Precious Wound, and found nailed inside the lid of a tool chest in a shop at the top of the upper lip of Villa Llaga. The chest held nothing but shavings.*

Let it be written what I was paid and what I made, so the account is square.

Of the Brotherhood of the Precious Wound, by the hand of the Elder, one commission: a new image of Our Lady for the niche above the crypt stair. The old image was worn to the grain. Forty mouths a year had kissed her face down to a hollow that would hold a thumb.

The terms, as the Elder spoke them. Her body in full: the mantle, the hands, the open side. Her face left in the block, rough from the axe, as the first carvers left it. No hand to finish it. I asked what would be owed if I finished it by mistake. He said there would be no mistake.

Timber. I went up into the Hollow Wood and counted trunks. Every tenth holds something the god was carrying, and you leave those be. I took the eleventh. Pale, straight, close in the grain. When the saw went in it ran red down the blade and warm over my wrists, and it kept running while I set the wedges. My boy was sick in the ash. I have cut a great deal of timber since. None of it bled.

I let the block stand in the shop through the winter to weep itself dry. It dried everywhere but the head end. Wherever I set the gouge there, the grain wet itself again by morning. So I kept the Rule gladly. I roughed the face with the axe and let it be.

She took eight months. The open side I cut the width of two fingers, the lips turned a little outward, as the Elder asked. I hollowed her back, as is right for an image, for the relics and the lightness, and closed the hollow with a board. The Elder sealed the board with his ring and his thumb's blood. What went into the hollow I was sent out of the room for.

Paid: in silver, and a place in the brothers' wall when my time came.

Also, the same year and after, the processional figures. The Mother under her canopy, the Nurse with her cup, twelve of the saints who come down. These I carved with faces, since the Rule allows it for all but the niche. Paid in full.

The niche image was raised at the Feast. Within the year the brothers found an infant lying at her feet in the niche, and they raised him as the Rule says. I went and looked at the board on her back that same day. It was sealed as I left it. I check my work.

The boy grew to be the sangrador. He came up to the shop now and then, for the smell of the shavings. He never looked at her face, the rough place. He looked at her hands.

Of the Friday of the Fall, little. They came up the street at night and filled the doorway, low down, many of them, and looked at the processional figures standing in their rows in the half-dark. They swayed at them, the sway, the stop, the sway. Then they went back down and left me. I think they took the figures for brothers who had already given.

On the ninth night I saw her go by from my window, lashed on the back of the great sewn one, high above the hoods. My work. I would know my hands anywhere. She has a face now, and it is his face. I did not cut it. Whoever cut it used a gouge the width of mine and worked with the grain, the way I was taught and the way I taught my boy. The board on her back is gone. The hollow stands open. It is the length of an infant.

My last carving. A man's hands want work. I kept the offcut from her block, the head end, and I began a small Maiden from it as the Rule has her, and roughed the face at dusk. In the morning there was a face in it. The chips lay on the bench in curls, my curls, the way my own gouge throws them. My gouge hung on its peg, clean.

It is a woman's face, young and very kind, and the eyes are shut. I know it and I cannot say from where. Every morning I look. The lids are thinner.

I have wrapped her in the niche's old veil and laid her in this chest. Tonight I carry my tools down to the crypt stair. The account is square, and a place in the wall was the pay.

Whoever reads this, keep the Rule. No hand shall finish her.

I kept it. Some hand has not.

## The Tall Red Hat

*Told by a woman of Villa Llaga who lived through the Friday of the Fall as a child, lying in the drain under the lower street. Set down by a Shrine Keeper who came to close the wound and did not. The rhyme is written as she sang it, twice through, and more slowly the second time.*

We sang it skipping, on the steps above the gutters, with our skirts held up out of the red.

*Tall Red Hat comes down the street,*
*red in the gutter, red on his feet.*
*He opens the brothers, one, two, three.*
*His arms stay dry. He won't open me.*

Our mothers slapped us for it, and sang it themselves when they thought we were asleep.

I was small. I remember things at the height of a hip. The hems of the robes, heavy and wet at the bottom. The cords. The bare heels of the brothers going by, and the red coming down behind them in threads, and the tin cups knocking at their belts. We called every hood the Tall Red Hat, though the sangrador wore the plainest one and walked at the back.

He had kind hands. He wrapped my knee once when I cut it on the steps, and he would not look at the blood. He looked at the wall while he tied it.

*Tall Red Hat goes down the stair,*
*count your fingers, count your hair.*
*One for the Maiden, one for the lip,*
*one for the Mother. Don't you slip.*

Then came the year the wound was scabbed.

It closed in one night, the whole mile of it, under a black crust like burnt bread, and the gutters went dry. The brothers still walked, and still filled their cups, and poured them onto the crust, and the crust drank it the same as the wound had. My mother said that was a good sign. My father said nothing, and moved our beds away from the window on the wound side.

That year the scab was warm. We children lay on it on the cold mornings, the way you lie against a wall the sun has been on. If you put your ear to it you heard a sound like many people breathing in their sleep, a little out of time with each other.

On the Friday of the Fall I was sent down to the lower street for thread. I heard the crust go. It was a wet sound, and very long, like cloth tearing under water. Then the screaming started up by the crypt, and I ran, and I went where we always hid in games, into the drain under the grate at the corner of the dyers' steps.

I lay flat in the dust. Then the drain was full of red again, warm, up to my chin, and I lay in it all night.

I saw feet go over the grate. Some were brothers' feet. Some were too many feet for one body. Something small came and pressed its face to the bars, and breathed on me, and went away. I think it went away because I smelled of the gutter, and the gutter smelled of them.

Near morning I heard singing. It was our tune. The first verse, with no words in it.

They came down the lower street in the robes. I knew the robes, I knew the patches and the cords. The hems were dragging, too long, and whatever was inside them went sway and stop, sway and stop, and got no farther down the street than the corner. Over and over.

Then he came.

He wore no hood at all. His sleeves were up, and his arms were open from the shoulder to the wrist, and they were dry. He stopped over my grate. He looked down, straight at me, through the bars, into the red I was lying in, and I looked back at him, because I could not shut my eyes.

His mouth moved. What came out was a woman's voice, and soft, the way you speak to a child who is ill.

*Lie down flat.*

Then he went on, to the front of them, and they followed him, and for the first time that night they went somewhere.

I climbed out when the street was empty. I have never gone back to the lip, and I have never met another child who came out of Villa Llaga.

There was a last verse. We sang it only on the crypt stair, where the grown ones could not hear. I sing it now to the Shrine Keeper, so it will be written somewhere that is not me.

*Tall Red Hat, Tall Red Hat,*
*who will open the Tall Red Hat?*
*Nobody, nobody, nobody will.*
*Mother will. Mother will.*

## The Tally of Hoods

*Spoken at the pay-table of a lowland town by a woman the towns hire to hunt the Maiden's strays, while the clerk counted out her fee. The clerk wrote it down after she had gone. She would give no name, and made her mark with a cut cord laid on the page.*

Nine hoods. Count them. The cords are in the sack with them. You pay by the cord, since a robe can be cut in two and a cord can't.

Ask your question. They all ask it.

How you know one is near: the singing. A tune with no words, the first line of it only, over and over. Your children know the tune. Keep them in when you hear it. They will want to go out and finish the verse.

How you find it: go low. They go where the water would go. Gutters, ditches, the drain under the square. They stand at corners. A stray from the red procession walks to the corner, stops, sways, and walks back, as if the rest of the street had been taken away. The robe was too long for it. The hem was worn through from the dragging.

They wear what the brothers wore. Good cloth, patched, heavy at the bottom. Some carry the tin cups still, knocking at the belt. The dead of Villa Llaga were eaten long ago, and robes wear out. I don't know where they get them now.

How they die.

They give. Cut one and it opens easy, the way a full skin opens, and all of it comes out warm and a little sweet on the air, and the stray stands there in it and lets it go. It won't run. It won't fight you, mostly. The small ones lean toward the blade. You open it at the cord, where the belly would be. Under the hood there's nothing you'd call a head. There's a place for one.

When it's down it lies flat. Every one. Flat on its face with the arms at its sides, the way you'd tell a child to lie in a ditch. Then the singing stops, and you burn the robe, and you keep the cord.

The ones that go wrong are the ones that stray long. Out past a season, a stray starts to fill the hood. Last spring I opened one near the fen-road and the place for a head had a brow in it, and the start of a mouth, set low. It had learned a second line of the tune. I burned that one twice.

The giant. Yes. Everyone asks that second.

I have seen it from the ridge above the salt-ditch. Taller than your granary. Sewn. The seams run in tithe-cord, knotted every hand-span, and every knot is a hood it swallowed. Something rides on its back, bound face-down with rope, painted, with the paint worn off the knees. A saint's image, a Maiden off some altar. When it walks the image nods.

It walks behind him. When he stops, it stops. The strays come to it from three valleys and go into it. I have seen them walk up into the seams.

I have had him twice. Once at forty paces in the reed-cut, once at twenty on the dyers' road below your mill. Twenty paces, clear light, the bow drawn, and a good head on the shaft.

The first time he was kneeling in a ditch with a scourge in his fist, laying it on his own back, slow, the way you'd work a field. Small hooded things came out of the welts and stood up in a line behind him, and waited, and he got up and they followed. The strays in that ditch followed too. I had been paid for those strays. They went with him, and I never saw them again, and nobody came asking.

The second time he turned his head and looked up the road at me. His arms were bare. They were open, shoulder to wrist, and dry. He said something. I was too far to hear it. I heard it anyway, in a woman's voice, very close, the way you speak to someone sick.

I let the bow down. I lay flat in the road until the procession passed. I don't remember deciding to.

Your headman asks why I don't take the contract on him. Nobody has offered one. The Pale Order offered once, for the giant's bones. They didn't say what they'd do with the rest of him.

Here's the truth of the trade. Every year I bring you more hoods. Every year the strays are bigger. And every year fewer of them are straying. They're going somewhere now.

Pay me for nine. I cut a tenth cord on the fen-road and I didn't bring it. It walked the whole way home behind me, singing, and I couldn't find the place to open it.

# VII. The House of Eight Million

## The First Folding

*Instruction set down by an elder purifier of the House of Eight Million for a novice on the night before her first rite. Found pressed flat inside a paper doll that was never used, at a wayside shrine on the Fen road.*

Fold it by lamplight, child, never at noon. Noon has no corners in it.

Take the white sheet. Crease it once down the middle: that is the spine. Fold the head down, the arms out, the legs apart. Leave the arms open. A doll with its arms closed has already refused, and a refused doll you burn without watching the smoke. Write nothing on it. The one who comes to you will name it with their breath, and your hand would only argue.

Sit across from them, knee to knee. Hold the doll to their mouth and ask them to breathe on it three times. Watch the paper. On the third breath it leans toward them a little, as if it were called. That is the distortion leaving by the door it came in. Then lift the doll to your own lips and breathe it in.

Hold it. Count on the knot-cord at your wrist, one knot for each breath held. It sits in the chest like cold water, then like warm, then you stop feeling it, and that is the part to fear. When you stop feeling it, it has found a place in you, and a place once found is kept.

Now the fan. Open it with the thumb, one rib at a time. Every rib is a road. Breathe out slow across the paper and turn the fan toward whoever made the distortion: a man who moved a boundary stone, a woman who cursed her neighbour's well, a house of the Maiden where flesh is grown without a word. The fan tells the breath where to go. Your breath only knows how to leave. Never breathe out with the fan shut. I did, once. The breath went where it liked. It liked the shrine.

The small gods you will meet on every road: the stone at the crossing, the spirit in the ford, the spirit in the bell. Eight million, the House says, and the House stopped counting long ago. Bow to each as you pass. Never ask a name. They gave their names to the wind, one each, and they are proud of it.

Where a shrine is forgotten, its guardian stone turns to the wall. A finger's width a year, and no hand moves it. When you find one, kneel, set both palms on it, and turn it back to face the road. Breathe on its face until the dust there stirs. The air will clear. We teach that the stone turns away in grief at being forgotten. I have turned many. Lately I think they turn to listen. Some day, when I am past scolding you, put your ear to the back of one. Then turn it back quickly.

The ring-stones on the pilgrim roads, the fang-shaped ones round a pit of ash, pass those by. They face inward already. No one of the House has ever turned them, and no one of the House will tell you why. I asked my own elder once. She breathed out, very long, and went to bed.

Now the thing the elders tell last, on the night before.

You are running out of room. Every held breath leaves a little of itself behind, the way a lantern keeps a little of whoever it carries home. You will breathe out and breathe out, and the fan will send it all away, and still the cord at your wrist grows longer than the knots you remember tying. Mine reaches the floor. I tied perhaps half of it.

Every paper doll ends as an oni. We say *ends*. We say it gently. The face goes long and hard. The horns come in slow, like a child's teeth. One morning the doll who has held too much stands up from the shrine step with her hands full of everything she took, and she walks down. Always down: into the water, into the Root Deep, where the country under the Hide breathes. She carries it there so the rest of us need not.

Seven went down into the Drowned Nave the spring it gave everything back. Six of my year, and a girl younger than you. I lay sick with fever that week and stayed on the step. I sat by the torii and watched the water come up grey, and I have been grateful and ashamed ever since, one and then the other, like breathing.

They say the girl came up. If a young woman in faded vermilion ever stops at this shrine, give her water. Do not look long at the one beside her.

You will know your own time by the stones. They will begin to turn toward you.

Fold the doll now. Leave the arms open. Then sleep, child. Breathe slowly. Leave the cord alone tonight.

## What the Pole Knows

*Set down from the words of a boatman of the Drowned Fen, spoken over three crossings to the Well-Shrine. The pilgrim who wrote them left the page folded under a guardian stone at the torii.*

Sit low. Keep your hands in the boat. This is the god's own clear water, and it drinks. Trail your fingers in it and they come back dry, and a little lighter. We say: the Fen takes the wet first, then the rest.

You pay at the Iron Hook. Before the Hook the Fen may still want you back, and a man should keep his coin until he knows.

The House of Eight Million keeps the Well-Shrine. We call them Fan-Bearers. In the reed-camps you will see their paper dolls on the doorsteps, a folded figure with its arms held out. Find one on your step, and you stop. A Fan-Bearer comes at dusk and breathes in the air of your house, and after, the house is easier to sleep in. My mother found one on our step the winter my father went quiet. She stopped. We say: the doll asks once.

Every spring the purifiers came down to the water's edge and breathed the year out into the Fen. All of it. They trusted the water to carry it off. So did we. Water goes somewhere.

Look down. There, past the reeds, where the pole strikes stone. That is the roof of the Drowned Nave. A whole house of prayer under us: bell, pew, altar, stair. My pole knows its roof. Some crossings the roof is a hand deeper than I left it. Some crossings it is a hand nearer. I keep a notch on the pole. I have cut the notch three times.

All those springs of breath sank there and filled the Nave the way a jar fills. Then one spring the jar was full.

I was a young man that year. Or it was my grandfather, and I have heard it told so often it has moved into me. The water came up grey. The reeds leaned away from the shrine all at once, like people from a sickbed. In the morning the guardian stone at the torii was facing the wall, and no one had touched it. Every bucket drawn from the Fen that month held faces, and the faces were turned the wrong way round, and then turned the wrong way round again, so they looked almost right. Almost.

The House sent seven down into the Nave to take it back in. Six came up as far as the Kneeling Arch. I saw them from this boat, or I say I did. They stood in the shallows with their hands full of something heavy, and their faces had gone long and hard, and there were horns on them. Then they turned and walked down into the deep water the way a man walks down a stair. Oni, the House calls them now. Out past the Reed That Points Home, on still nights, breath comes up through the water in slow rings. I have counted six. Some nights I count seven.

The seventh was the girl with the fan. Shiori. She was left at the torii as a babe, and the purifiers named her for the bent twig a pilgrim leaves to find the way back. Her sister Akane was two years older and took her fevers for her. They say Akane breathed in a hundred years at once, down there, and Shiori held her face and breathed it all back out of her, and the clock over the drowned altar stood still for as long as she held. I only know what came up. One girl. And a haze around her the colour of a bruise.

She crossed in my boat once. She paid nothing and I asked nothing. Her breath went slower with every stroke of the pole, the way a full jar pours slow. There was someone in the haze beside her. Akane was right-handed. The one in the haze holds her fan in the left.

Then there are the knights. The Pale Order comes down from the white mountains in plate of carved bone, with hooks and chain, to dredge the drowned. We give our dead to the water. The Order says a bone out of its place is a wound, and hauls ours up and stands them in the Nave, facing the stair. What they stood up came up bent. The House says it was the cause. The knights say nothing.

They still come. They pay well and ask no questions, and a boat has no side. But I count what they carry up, and I know what my own village gave the water. The Fen gives back more than it was given.

There. The torii. Step out on the left, and do not look at the stone until you are past it.

## What the Ford-Bell Said

*Set down by a purifier of the House of Eight Million, who sat one whole night beside the bell that hangs over the Salt Ford and wrote between its strokes. The bell has no rope. The copyist of the Codex left her gaps and her marks as she made them.*

[It began at dusk. The wind moved the bell once. After that it moved by itself, or the words came when it was still. I could never tell which.]

Stroke. Stroke. You are the paper one. You smell of held breath.

I ride. Everything rides. The great one breathed in once, long, when the space was held open, and all of us were inside that breath, warm, one sound. Then the naming stopped. Then the breath began to go out. We are going out with it. We have been going out for — [a word like a long hum; I wrote *one stroke*, and then *many*, and could not choose].

A bell is a mouth for the wind. The bronze is my house. I am what leaves the house when it is struck, and comes back, and leaves. I have left and come back more times than there are stones in the ford. Each time I come back a little less. That is how I know the breath is still going out.

You want names. You have the look. I had a name. I gave it to the wind the morning the great one went still, the way all of us did, one each, and the wind took it out ahead of us. Some nights I hear it, very small, far ahead, and ring toward it.

[Here a long silence. The ford ran loud. When it spoke again it spoke of its neighbours.]

The water under me is a small god also. She is older and says less. She carries whatever you give her and she is tired of carrying it. Your House gave her a year's wrong breath, every year, and she carried it down, down, to the drowned house under the Fen, because down was the only way she knew. She is sorry. She says to tell you she is sorry.

The stone at the crossing is a small god. He was a tooth once. He remembers biting.

The guardian stones of your shrines are small gods. You teach they turn to the wall for grief. Paper one, they turn toward the wall to hear through it. There is a sound behind every wall of the Hide now. They are listening to learn which way it goes.

Wrong breath. You call it miasma. It is our own breath that turned round in a narrow place and forgot the door. Breath that cannot leave goes thick. It sits. It sickens what it sits on, the way a word sickens when you keep it in your mouth too long. You paper ones come and drink it. You hold it where your own breath lives. Then you send it out again with the fan, and it remembers the door, and it goes on. We are grateful, and a little afraid. What you drink is us.

Some of you drink too much and walk down into the water with it. I have rung for seven. Then for six. Then I lost the count. The water says the one who did not walk down came up with two breaths in one body, and one of them facing backward.

[I asked it where the breath is going. The bell swung hard and I thought it would speak no more that night.]

Out.

Out is where it goes. Out past the high cold where the flames barely move. Out toward the white points in the dark that the bone-counters wait for. The bone-counters want the points to fall. We want to reach them. We cannot both have it.

But, paper one.

Out is closer than it was. I ring, and the ring comes back from the edge sooner than before. The edge is nearer. Or I am smaller. It is the same thing from inside a bell.

And the ring-stones. The teeth round the ash. Go close to one and hold your own breath. Under the ash something is drawing in. Slow. Very slow. The great one only ever breathed out after the stopping.

Something under the ash is breathing in.

[It said this twice, the second time much quieter. Then nothing until the ford went grey.]

Stroke. The light comes. Bow as you go. Do not ask the water her name. Leave me a bent twig on the post, so I know you found the way back. I will ring when you are far enough off that you cannot hear it.

[I left the twig. On the road home, at the hour it had said, I felt my chest go light, as if I had breathed out a long while. I had been holding my breath since the ford.]

## The Folding Song of the Reed-Camps

*Gathered in the reed-camps of the Drowned Fen by a copyist who came down to write a debt-roll and stayed a winter. The verses as the women sang them over their work; the notes between are his. Sent to the Codex from the Well-Shrine, folded small.*

The women of the reed-camps fold the House's dolls by lamplight, six or eight to a mat, one verse to each fold. The song keeps the hands in order, they told me.

> *Cut the reed at low water,*
> *lay it under stone.*
> *The Fen will take the wet away*
> *and leave the rest alone.*

The paper is pressed reed. They cut it in the shallows past the Iron Hook, split it, lay it crosswise in two courses, and weight it with flat stones *under the water*, a month of nights. Fen water leaves a thing dry, so the sheet comes up dry, and white, and smelling of nothing at all. A sheet that smells of anything they burn.

> *Down the middle, one crease,*
> *that's the bone to stand her.*
> *Fold it once and fold it true*
> *and no one fold it after.*

The spine is the only bone the doll gets. An old folder called Tsuru told me it is plenty, for what she carries. A spine creased twice goes in the fire.

> *Head down, little head,*
> *bow before the door.*
> *Whoever comes to find you*
> *will stop, and wonder what for.*

This is what the doll is for. The House leaves it on a doorstep and it asks the house to stop. Stop what, I asked. Whatever you were doing, said Tsuru. A man shifting a boundary stone. Most stop. The doll asks once. A Fan-Bearer comes at dusk either way.

I found one on my own step my third week. I had been adding the camp's debts. I stopped. The roll is still unfinished.

> *Arms out, arms out,*
> *open as a well.*
> *What comes into open arms*
> *the arms will never tell.*

The arms are left open always; a doll with closed arms has refused, and is burned without watching. The House sends a pattern-doll each spring for the folders to copy. Tsuru keeps the old ones in a box; I laid them out by year. Every spring the arms are a little longer. Her grandmother's dolls hold their arms out about as far as a child does. This year's could hold a door.

> *Legs apart for walking,*
> *turn the hem up high.*
> *The road she goes is under water*
> *and she must keep dry.*

A doll lying on a step walks nowhere, so I asked about the legs. *For later*, they said, all of them, as a thing everybody knows. The children set dolls on the water to watch them float. They float high and dry, and they drift west against the current, toward the pool where the one reed stands. The mothers call the children in early.

Two verses more go on the same tune. I heard them only from the children, and from the old women when they forgot I was there.

> *Who folded the folder?*
> *Who creased her down the spine?*
> *She held her arms out all her life*
> *and now they're full of mine.*
> *Down the thirty-three steps,*
> *down the one step more.*
> *The doll who held the whole Fen's breath*
> *is knocking at the floor.*

The spring after the Nave gave everything back, a purifier in grey came to each camp and asked the women, kindly, to stop singing these two. She gave no reason. She sat and folded with them a whole evening, and her dolls were perfect, and she did not sing at all.

They stopped the words. They kept the tune. When the fold for those verses comes round, the women hum, and the hands go on. I have watched a whole mat hum through it without once looking up.

I cannot make the verse agree with itself. Tsuru said her mother sang it. Her mother was dead before the Nave rose. She said songs come when they come.

The children sing *thirty-four*, and their mothers correct them. On my last evening, at the western pool, I heard a small girl sing *thirty-six*. Nobody corrected her. There was nobody near her to correct her.

They close each bundle with a verse they sing plainly, and let me write:

> *Lie still, little sister,*
> *lie still upon the step.*
> *The House will come at dusk for you,*
> *and we will keep the rest.*

The folders never breathe on a doll. Breath is the House's business. They hold the paper away from their mouths as they fold, and turn their faces aside to speak.

On the road north I found I had folded the last leaf of my debt-roll into a doll. Arms out. I do not remember doing it. I have kept my face turned aside from it, and I send it on to the Well-Shrine with these pages.

## Four in the Traces

*Told by a salt-carter of the Heights to a purifier of the House of Eight Million, who had come up to Bellrest Hearth to read the breath of travellers. The purifier set it down that night, and left the carter a paper doll, unused.*

You want to know why I breathe like this. Everyone at the Hearth wants to know. Sit, then. It takes me a while.

We pulled salt. One cart, from the white pans above the Cloud-Shelf, down through the Glass Pass to the Hearth. Four in the traces going down, because salt is heavy and the road is steep, and a cart that gets away from you goes over the edge and takes the traces with it. Coming up empty we were two, me and Ivo. Ivo and I pulled together eleven years. I knew his breathing better than my own. On the climb you breathe together or you fall out of step, and the strap cuts you.

Up there the air is thin. Hear it from me: you breathe and nothing comes. You breathe again and a little comes. The lanterns on the spires burn tall and still, like flames painted on a wall, and the frost on the glass is a hand, every time, the fingers spread. The old carters say never wipe the hand off. We never did.

The morning it came we were on the Wind-Scoured Ridges, past the last spire. The chimes had stopped. When the chimes stop you pull hard for the next lantern. We pulled. Then the lantern ahead of us leaned. Only a little. The first flame I had seen move in three days, and it leaned toward us, down the road, the way a candle leans toward an open door.

Then it came up the road from below. Wind comes down off the peaks; this came up from the Hide, slow, the width of the road and taller than the spire, and it was faces. Grey faces, thin as breath on cold glass, pressed close as a crowd at a well. Old men. Children. Faces with too many eyes, or none, and faces so plain they could have been anyone from my village. All of them with the mouth a little open, the way a mouth sits when the last of the air goes out.

The cold came with it, past any cold I know. My lashes froze together. The salt dust on my sleeves went hard as sand.

The faces spoke. Each one said a single word as it passed, and then it was gone behind us, up toward the top, and the next came. I heard *mother*. I heard *enough*. I heard a name I think was a place. I heard a word I have never heard again and could not say for you if you paid me in white salt. Most said only the sound a man makes at the end of a long day's pull, when he sets the traces down. *Hahh.* Like that. Hundreds of them. Thousands. I did not count. I am no bone-brother.

Ivo stopped pulling. I felt it in the harness before I saw it. He said, It's lighter. I said the cart is full, what is lighter. He said, Everything. Listen to them, Ren. Nobody in there is carrying anything.

Then he slipped the traces. Eleven years, and I watched him lift the strap over his head as easy as a man takes off his hat at a door. He walked up into the faces and they parted round him the way a crowd parts for a bier. I held on to the cart. It was the only thing heavy enough to hold me. I am not proud of it. I would do it again.

When it had gone past, the lantern stood straight. His footprints went twelve steps into the frost and stopped. His coat lay on the road, folded. Ivo never folded anything in his life.

I brought the salt down alone. Four days. I should have died on the Pass. I did not, and I have thought a long time about why.

The next year, crossing up with a new partner who does not talk, I saw it again, far off along the Cloud-Shelf, going up. And I knew one face near the front, with its mouth a little open. He said nothing. He had already said it.

So. I let no breath go quickly now. I hold each one a moment and let it out slow, through the teeth, a little at a time. Your sisters tell me the small gods of every stone and bell ride the breath, and the whole world has been breathing out one long breath since before the Hearth was built. Maybe. I only know something goes out with each one. I heard them go. I would keep mine a little longer before they join the rest.

One more thing, since you are writing. The frost hand on my lantern. Every morning since, I find it on the inside of the glass.

## The Count of His Breathing

*Spoken by Tae, a reed-cutter of the Fen camps, whose husband was one of the six. A pilgrim who sheltered a night in her reed-house wrote it down. The House asked that it be kept out of the Codex. It was put in.*

You want to know about the oni. Sit on that side, where the lamp can see your face. Mine is the man.

His name was Jinpei, and he was the loudest man in the reed-camps. He sang cutting reeds and he sang poling home, badly, and when he slept the thatch shook. My mother said that one would never need a doll on his step; he breathed everything out as fast as it came in. She was wrong about that.

The House took him the year we married. They came up from the Well-Shrine with a fan and said he had room in him. They said it as a gift, and he took it as one. After that he went down to the torii every dusk and came home at dark smelling of salt and paper, and he stopped singing.

I learned to count him. Every wife of the House learns. You lie in the dark with your hand flat on his back and count his breaths against your own. The first spring he breathed four to my five. Then three. By the tenth spring I would count twenty of mine and wait, and wait, and he would draw one breath so long I could have filled a bowl in it. His back stayed warm under my hand, like a hearthstone after the fire has gone out. His jaw came long. His old hat stopped fitting. I plaited him a new one and said nothing, and so did he, and that was the worst thing we did to each other.

The spring the water came up grey, he went down with the others. He kissed me at the door the way you kiss someone on a boat already pushing off. I said: breathe it back at whoever made it. That is the rite. I meant the knights of the Pale Order, who hauled our dead out of the Nave and stood them facing the stair until they came up bent. I meant the House, who breathed a hundred springs into the water and called it clean. I meant the water.

He came as far as the Kneeling Arch. I stood on the bank and saw him. His hands were full. He looked a long while at me. Then he turned round and walked down into the Fen like a man going down to his own cellar.

After, the House left a paper doll on my step. I burned it, and I watched the smoke.

Now. What you came for.

Some nights, when the Fen is still, he comes up. He comes up dry. He stands on the bank just where my lamp gives out, and no nearer. His face is long and hard, and the horns came in slow, the way a child's teeth come. He holds his hands shut against his chest, and whatever is in them is heavy, because he leans.

The camp says bar the door. I take the lamp out to the step, and I count him.

One breath a night. He draws it when I light the lamp and lets it go before first light. His feet are grey to the ankle with ash. Dry ash, and he has been under water. I asked him once where there is ash beneath the Fen. He turned his head toward the Nave, then lower than the Nave, and I stopped asking.

He keeps out of the light. I have thought about why. A soul burns in every lamp, someone who chose to stay, and I think what he carries would like very much to sit beside a fire. So he stands in the dark and keeps it off mine. That is who he was. He always took the heavy end of the boat.

I am angry at him. Write that down. At the House, at the knights, at the water, and at him for turning round at the Arch. I told him so on the bank, loud, the way he used to sing. He stood and took it. He has room.

Last winter I walked out past the lamp. The camp cried out behind me. I set my hand flat on his chest, where I used to count. It was warm, the way the road down must be warm. Under my palm something turned over, slowly, as a sleeper turns.

He opened one hand a little. I will keep what was in it.

Every spring I set my lamp a pace nearer the water. Next spring it stands on the bank. I have been practising. Twenty breaths, and hold. The House says a woman must be folded for it. I say a woman need only be his wife.

Tell them that at the torii. Tell them the doll asks once. So do I.

## What the Sixth Doll Carried

*Scratched in a hard point on both faces of a paper doll found floating, dry, past the Reed That Points Home. A boatman brought it to the Well-Shrine. The House would not keep it under its roof, and a pilgrim copied it out at the torii.*

**Front, along the arms and down the body**

I fold this from the last paper in my sleeve. The Fen gives back dry whatever it gives back, so this may reach the reeds.

I was Kasane of the lower house. I kept the salt at the torii and taught the small ones to fold. I set it down now, while it is still the right way round.

We went down seven. The stair into the Nave has thirty-three steps. We counted them going down, all of us aloud, as the rite asks. At the bottom Hisa counted again and said thirty-four, and we laughed at her, there, in that place. I am glad we laughed.

What we carried out, plain. The drowned of the Fen, first: the dead we gave the water with a breath still in them, which the knights of the Pale Order hauled up and stood facing the stair. The breath had gone sour in the standing and bent the bones around it. We drew it out of them, one by one, and they sat down. Then a hundred springs of the House's own leavings, every year we breathed into the water and called the water clean. Then something at the bottom of the jar that had no face and asked for nothing, and weighed more than the rest together.

A breath is light. Ten thousand breaths are light. That last one was heavy the way a sleeping child is heavy. I hold it behind my teeth. My jaw has lengthened to hold it. When I lean forward it pulls, gently, the way a hand on the back of your head bends you to the bowl.

At the Kneeling Arch the six of us stood in the shallows with our hands full. The rite says: breathe it back at whoever made it. We stood a long while, working that out. Then we turned round.

**Back of the doll**

Shiori. You were the small one who carried the salt bowl in both hands and spilled it every time. Hear me.

Keep a little room. Breathe out at every dusk, even when there is nothing to breathe out. A full jar pours slow. I have watched you from the water and you pour slow.

Turn the stones. All of them. Some of us turned our backs on the torii, down here, and I think a stone takes its lesson from the one who keeps it.

Do not come down the stair to fetch us. The stair is longer now.

Akane ate her rice with her right hand. Look at which hand. Then love it anyway. I would.

Of the Nave, since no one else will tell you. The clock over the altar stands where you stopped it. None of us has wound it. Under it the pews are full. The Fen gave the water four hundred and some; I counted the pews twice and they hold more, sitting quiet, the ones we unbent and ones I never breathed on. The knights' dead faced the stair. On the nights I go back they face the altar. The bell rings with no hand on the rope, and the sound goes up through the water and comes out, I think, as the rings the boatmen count.

Behind the altar the floor has broken in a ring. The stones lean in over it like teeth. There is ash down there, dry, under all that water, and it rises and settles. Something breathes under it, slow, slower than me. The others went past it. There is a door there now and a road going down, and the air on the road is warm. Hisa went first. She counted as she went.

I am the last at the stair.

**Round the edge, smaller, the point pressed deeper**

My name was. Kasane. Kasane of the salt. The paper is small and the hand is large now. It wants out and I hold it, where would it go, it would go to you. We are room. The room is warm below. Thirty-six. The stair counts itself. The doll asks once, so stop, stop at the doll, you that finds it. Fold the corners tight. Turn the stone. Right hand. Right hand. We are the right way round again, nearly. Almost.

## The Hymn Under the Long Candle

*Heard over nine nights in the Rib Crypts, through the flagstones as they lifted, by Ottelin of the Lower Stacks, sage and copyist; set down in his hand with his gaps kept, and bound into the Codex after his death.*

*(Ottelin: It comes when the stones lift, and stops when they settle. It speaks as many. I have written "we" for a word that also means the inside of a throat. Where I have no word I leave the place open.)*

We were the part of the god that told it what it was.

Three, it was. One, it was. We sang the three into the one each [breath / day / age: the word is the same word] and it held. Soul went out along its threads and came home along them. Bone stood round the space the way a hand stands round a candle in a draught. Flesh filled the space and was warm, and we lived in the warm, in the long groove behind the [ribs, or the heart; the word has too many rooms in it].

The space was open. That was the whole of the work. On every side lay the Hush, pressing in, the way deep water presses on a held breath. The god held the space open, and we held the god in its shape by singing it. Nobody set us to it. We were the singing. When we were made we were already in the middle of a verse.

## The Keeping of the Spiral Hollow

## The Assize at Four Wents

*The record of a reeve of the lowland hundreds, entered in his roll of assizes. The roll was sold for its vellum after his death; the Codex copy was taken from a binder's scraps, and the last leaf was stitched in upside down.*

Assize held at Four Wents, where the Barrow road crosses the salt road, in the ninth month, by the old right of the crossroads: what dies where roads meet, the reeve shall say whose it is.

**The dead.** A man of the Tithed, of middle years, found at first light on the crossing stone, lying straight. No mark upon him but a cut on the left palm, old and clean. Boots good. Purse empty. Nobody knew his face.

By dusk four had come to claim him, one by each road. None had been sent for.

**The claim of the frame.** A knight of the Pale Order, in carved plate, dust in every seam. Asked his name, he gave a number: four hundred and five. He said it adds to nine, and so will keep.

"The meat is a coat he wore. The frame is the Mother's, lent him for a life. Two hundred and six; I have told them on my cord. Each bone out of its niche is a door left open in the world. Give me the frame and I will carry it up the stairs. If he has no name I will give him a number. A number is enough."

**The claim of the meat.** A priest of the Brotherhood of the Precious Wound, hooded, his sleeves stiff and dark to the elbow.

"The Maiden grew him, lent warm and wanted back warm. The knight may have the sticks after. I ask a knife and a night. She wastes nothing, reeve. Your barrows waste him. Ask your diggers what the barrow-lids do."

I did not ask them. I know what they do.

**The claim of the breath.** A purifier of the House of Eight Million, in white and faded red. She laid a paper doll on the dead man's chest before she spoke, and none of the others moved it.

"He has not finished breathing out. What is left in him is small and frightened and belongs to the Myriad. Keep it in a box of meat or stand it up in a box of bone and it goes wrong. Give him to the water. The water carries it off."

The knight said the water had given things back before. She opened her fan, and closed it.

**The claim of the thread.** A reader of the House of the Polished Heart, a small man with ash under his nails. He asked first that my hand-mirror be turned to the wall. I let it stand so.

"I claim nothing. His thread is loose and feeling for the loom, and all these three would do ties a knot in it. Let him lie. Cover the water in the ditches. In a few nights he will be gone out of himself. Share out what is left then."

**What the dead man did while they argued.** The hearing ran three days. No party would leave the stone.

On the first day he stiffened and softened again by evening.

On the second day he was warm. The priest laid a hand on the belly and gave thanks. The paper doll was found face down. The cut in the palm had opened, and was dry inside.

On the second night the knight told the frame again, before witnesses. Two hundred and seven. He told it a third time and found the same. He called the difference the Mother's gift and asked that it be kept out of my roll. I have kept it in.

On the third morning the guardian stone at the crossing was found facing the wall of the ditch. The purifier turned it back. It took her the whole morning.

**Judgement.** I find: the frame to the Pale Order, the meat to the Brotherhood, the breath to the House, the thread to the lodge, the parting to be done at first light of the fourth day. The reeve's fee, the boots.

**Return.** On the fourth morning the dead man was sitting up on the stone, turning to each of the four roads, as a traveller does who has lost his way. None of the four answered him. The knight had his cord in his hands. The priest had his knife. The purifier held her breath. The reader looked away.

He stood, and went down none of the four roads. My watchman says he went down. There is a soft place in the crossing now, dished inward, and warm, and the carters go round it.

The parties dispersed, each saying he had his share.

I have the boots. They fit well. I have worn them to every assize since, and my feet are never cold.

## What the Orders Pay

*Spoken over three nights at a tavern on the moor road by a hired blade with a split ear and no little finger on her sword hand. The keeper's son wrote it on the backs of tally-slates to settle a wager. The slates came to the Codex when the tavern was sold for its roof-beams.*

Fill the jug and I'll tell you who pays. I've taken coin from all five orders. Four and a half. The monk never paid anyone.

The Pale Order pays best and strangest. Nine weeks guarding a bone-caravan down out of the Ossa and back up loaded. Brothers pull those carts, in the traces, eighteen to a cart, and they sang the whole way in nines. At the end they count your coin onto the table one piece at a time, adding the figures under their breath, and if the sum won't come to nine at the root they take a piece back. Or give you one. The man beside me got a coin more than I did for the same nine weeks. Argue it and they write you down. By the second week every one of us had a number. Mine was long. I never heard the end of it.

They fight slowly. When the Husks came up out of the ash, the dead climbed down off the carts and walked into them like reapers, and the brothers kept singing.

They lay their dead. We brought four hundred and some bones up in sacks, tallied at the low gate. At the high gate the tally was higher. The brother smiled and called it the Mother's gift and asked me to keep the number in my mouth. I told the man beside me anyway. He's dead now. So is everyone I ever told anything.

The flesh-house pays in food. A month escorting a fattening-cart through the Barrows, eleven in the cart, singing, eating sweet bread all day. They fed us the same. They weigh you going out and weigh you coming back, and pay the gain. I gained. The cart came home with eleven, rounder. The brothers fight by opening their own arms first. The blood gets up off the ground after that and goes where they point. Their dead go back to the Wheel, and I never asked the road the Wheel takes. I ate well that month. I think about it when I'm hungry, and then I stop.

The House in the Fen pays in clean water: a flask from the Well-Shrine, and a paper doll pinned to your coat so the stilt-towns leave you be. I poled a funeral boat for the Fan-Bearers one spring. They fold a paper on the chest and push the dead off into the clear water, which leaves you dry to the elbow however deep you reach. Their women fight by breathing in. One of them drew the wrongness off a thing in the reeds until it stood there small and ashamed, and then she coughed for three days and wouldn't let us near her. Halfway out my pole struck slates. A roof. Something under the Fen breathed in, slow, and the boat sat lower for it. I never poled that stretch again. The Order's knights were dredging the next channel over, standing up what they brought out. The Fen folk spat at them. I'd have spat too, if my mouth had been wet.

The lodge offered to pay by reading my thread. I took coin. They had coin, they only thought it rude. I carried a covered mirror across two valleys, the size of a cart-wheel, a lodge man walking beside me saying the Crone's names. It was heavier every morning. I looked once. My face, then a face behind it with both ears whole, then one behind that I didn't know, holding a sword the wrong way. The lodge man covered it and said nothing. They leave the bones where they fall and keep their dead some other way. I'd have liked to know which of us was the one behind.

The monk. He sat at my fire on the Pilgrim Road, barefoot, black lantern, bowl. He ate nothing. He asked for a name. I had one ready: the man who split my ear. I nearly gave it. He didn't press. In the morning there was a patch of black glass where he'd sat, the size of a hand. I know who split my ear. I know it. Give me a moment.

The job I turned down was the Order's again. Double pay: one night on the Ninth Stair. Count a wall at dusk. Count it again at first light. Tell them if the numbers agreed. That was all. The brother who offered it had counted that wall himself, and his hands shook on the cup.

I've counted coin, men in ditches, fingers after a fight. I never wanted to be the one holding the number the Order was paying to hear.

Tomas took it. He came down and gave the coin back.

Everyone pays. That job paid best of all, and I'm still drinking. You work it out.

## The Terms of the Truce-Stone at Kerrow Cross

*Copied clause by clause from the stone where the Pilgrim Road meets the lowland ways, with the gloss of a copyist who kept no order and signed himself only "the Reader of Stones".*

The stone stands where five roads meet. The orders swore here over the dead of the Long Field, who were of every house and of none, who lay three days in the ash while the houses argued. The terms are cut in the old lowland hand. Beside each clause stand the marks of the houses that swore to it: nine notches for the Pale Order; an open circle, left empty, for the Gilded Peak; a small round hollow, drilled and polished, for the Polished Heart; a short upright cut with its lips drawn apart for the Precious Wound; a twig bent back on itself for the House of Eight Million.

**I. THE DEAD OF THIS FIELD BELONG TO NO HOUSE. THEY SHALL LIE UNDER THE SKY THREE NIGHTS, AND ON THE FOURTH MORNING EACH HOUSE SHALL TAKE ITS SHARE AND GO.**
*Five marks.*

Kept three nights. On the fourth morning each house came for its share and found the others had miscounted. I have read all five accounts of the Long Field. Each gives a different number of dead. The Pale Order's is the highest, and it rises in every later copy of their book.

**II. NO BONE SHALL BE LIFTED FROM THE ROADS THAT MEET HERE.**
*Scratched out. The nine notches remain, cut deeper than the clause they swore to.*

The Order broke it first, in the first winter. A knight carried a thighbone off the east road at noon. The Order's gloss holds that the bone stood up of itself and asked to go home. The ditch-keepers say they saw it dragged.

**III. NO BLOOD SHALL BE LET WITHIN SIGHT OF THE STONE.**
*The Wound's cut. Someone has since closed its lips with a line of pitch.*

The Brotherhood broke it the spring after, with a procession and a full tithe-cup. They say the stone bled first, and that they only answered. There is a dark mark low on the south face. The ditch-keepers scrub it at every thaw. By harvest it is back, a little larger, the shape of a mouth.

**IV. NO GLASS SHALL BE UNCOVERED OVER THE DEAD OF THIS FIELD.**
*The Heart's hollow. The Peak's circle beside it has been filled with gold leaf, and the gold scraped away with a knife.*

The lodge broke it on the night of the swearing, before the carvers had finished. A mourner held a hand-mirror over the dead so their threads could find the way. The lodge says the clause was cut after the mirror was lifted, and so was never broken.

**V. NO NAME SHALL BE ASKED FOR AT THIS CROSSING AFTER DUSK.**
*Scratched out cleanly, by a steady hand with a good tool. The Peak's circle beside it is untouched.*

Nobody broke this clause. The Peak removed it, one autumn, and then asked for nothing. The lowlanders still come at dusk and whisper names into the cup-hollow at the foot of the stone. In the morning the hollow is empty and swept. I sat one night by the stone to see who sweeps it. I remember the dusk and I remember the morning.

**VI. NO BREATH SHALL BE GIVEN TO WATER THAT RUNS PAST THIS STONE.**
*The twig, bent.*

The House kept this one best. The ditch that ran past the stone went dry the year the House swore, and has stayed dry. The House says the Myriad keeps its promises for it. A purifier I asked about it turned her face to the wall until I left.

**VII. THIS STONE SHALL STAND WHERE IT IS SET, AND NO HOUSE SHALL MOVE IT.**
*Five marks.*

The one clause everyone kept. Every house watches the others too closely to try. The Pale Order's book sets the stone nine paces from the old milestone. I paced sixteen. The roads bend to meet it now. The earth round its foot shows no digging, and the foot is broader than the old rubbings show. A tallier of the Order paced it after me and would say only that sixteen is an open count.

**VIII.** *Cut last year beneath the seventh, in the old lowland hand, whose carvers are all in the ground. No house admits to it. The Order blames the Peak; the Wound, the Heart; the Heart, the House. The House leaves paper dolls at the foot.*

**THE HOUSE THAT WAS PRESENT AND UNNAMED SHALL TAKE ITS SHARE LAST.**
*Its mark: a broken ring of small fang-shaped cuts around a hollow.*

The chips lay fresh at the foot when the ditch-keepers found it, and they were warm. I have put my hand into the hollow of that mark. It goes deeper than the stone is thick.

I note, for whoever reads after me, that the first clause gives the dead three nights under the sky and each house its share on the fourth morning. It nowhere says who kept watch over them between.
