## To Whoever Holds This Book

Turn the wick down before you go on. Lower than that. The flame in your lantern is a person, and it reads over your shoulder, and there are pages here it would rather you skipped.

I gathered these pages. At the Moor fires I am the man already sitting there when you arrive. In the Barrens they say the Sage, and I have stopped correcting them. The stilt-towns have a word for me that I will not put down, because it is not polite. I had a name before any of these. It is somewhere the Quiet has not looked, it is not in this book, and you may stop searching for it.

I am older than I seem. I have walked the Hide end to end, and down, and further down than the roads go. People will tell you in every town that nobody saw me come in. I take pains over that.

Here is why there is a book at all. The Unsaying takes things piece by piece, the way it took the god. I have seen a word go out of a woodcutter's mouth between one breath and the next, and he went on talking round the hole. A thing with one teller is easy for it. A thing with ten tellers who argue is a great deal of work, because it must take every one of them and every place where they part company, and I have come to believe that the parting is where a thing holds on. So I have not made these writers agree. I have not tried. Where I was present and one of them is wrong, I say so, and I say where I was standing.

The scholars who have handled these leaves call them a codex. I call the book the Ossuary of Words. I learned in the Ossa that a brother lays each bone with two things, a name and a number, and that a thing so laid is hard to reach. So every writing here has its name at the head and its place in the book beneath, and each lies in its niche facing out, toward whoever opens it.

There are nine chapters. I did not choose nine. I spread the leaves on the floor of a barn at Ashwake one winter, and by morning they lay in nine heaps, and I have been in the Ossa long enough not to quarrel with a count like that.

Every chapter opens with pages of mine, about the land it concerns and the people I found there. The works come after, in the order they reached me: letters, sermons, tallies, a catechism, a wall, a song. Most I paid for, in bread or salt or oil. Some I copied at night in houses where I was a guest. Two came out of dead hands, and I asked first. I have changed nothing. Where a writer is wrong, the mistake is still there. Where a copyist left a gap, so have I.

Each chapter closes with the things I carry out of that land. They are in the bag by my feet tonight. What I believe of each, I say. You may believe less.

Some rules, and then I will leave you to it.

Say none of the gods' true names aloud. I have written them where there was no other way, fewer times than I know them.

Some leaves in this book are clean. Turn past them. If the ink grows faint toward the foot of a page, as though the hand were tired, close the book for the night.

When the book comes to you, count the leaves, and count them again before you pass it on. If the two numbers differ, put the second inside the back board, beneath mine and the others'. Never total them.

Be careful of any writer here who is certain. Be careful of me when I am. I give dates in these pages that no one living should be able to give, and I have kept them because they are right. And there are pages I chose not to write. You will feel the place where each would have been, as a foot feels for a stair that is not there. Leave the place empty. It is yours.

I do not know you. I have waited a long time, at a great many fires, to see who would come for this.

If you are one of the Tithed, you will want to know where you stand and what stands near you, and the first chapter is for that. If you are not, and you have come this far, you are reading; reading is a way of kneeling, and I have never turned a kneeling thing from a fire.

Some of these writers are dead. A few are dead in the other way. Of some I cannot say. Each of them wrote so that somebody would know. That is you now.

Before you start, clean the lantern glass. Gently.

*Set down at the pilgrims' fire below Ashwake, on the forty-first night of the thaw, in the eleven hundred and fourteenth year of the Last Breath.*

## The Reliquary

subtitle: of the dead god, and the lands I have walked on it
epigraph: Nothing here is new. It is only still warm.

## Preface

First find the floor with your palm. Wherever you are, the Hide is under it somewhere, beneath the boards or the straw or the flags. On the Moor you will not have to look far. Keep the hand there. I will tell you afterwards whose it is.

### The oldest thing

Every fire I have sat at begins its telling in the same place, and I have heard it at more fires than I could count on my hands and feet several times over. First there was the Edgeless, and nothing in it had a name, and a thing without a name cannot stand. A copyist at Ashwake spent his life on that part. A grandmother on the Moor gives it in a breath. Both are in this chapter, and I will not tell it better than they do.

What I can add is small. In my first year on the Cheek I met a salt-boiler who had been born deaf, and I asked him with my hands what came before the god. He put both his palms over my mouth and held them there a while. Then he took them away and nodded, as though I had understood. I think I had.

### The long age, and its end

After the Edgeless came the god, and it held a space open. The scholars call that the Age of the Held Space, and I have no better name. Soul, Bone and Flesh were in it at once. Others lived inside it, and near the end a congregation knelt within it. I have been down to where some of those others worked. The ceilings are low and the steps are high, and nobody I have taken there wanted to stay.

The Quiet wore at it. It went as a word goes when a child says it over and over at the table, until it is only noise, and at last the god could not hold itself together and died. That was one thousand one hundred and nine years before I cut my first pen for this book, on a night with no wind anywhere. I have heard it given as a year less. The ones who say so are all short by the same winter, which tells me they had it from one another.

### What you are standing on

The body is the world. Every order on the Hide starts from that and then quarrels. The skin is the Hide, where the towns are. The long white ridges in the far lands are its bones. What runs in the rivers is what still runs in it. The scholars at Ashwake call the whole corpse the Reliquary, as though it were a box someone had set down with care. I use their word. I have not found a gentler.

The life left in the bones they call the godmarrow. I have seen it drawn up a well in the Ossa, warm and slow. I have seen it on a woodcutter's blade after he opened a pale trunk he should have left. Every house on the Hide has its own use for the stuff and its own word for the other houses' uses. You will hear those words in the ninth chapter.

### Of us, and of the rest

You are almost certainly one of the Tithed. The Tithed are the children of that congregation. Their mothers' mothers were on their knees inside the god when it died, and they came out of it afterwards, and nothing else on the Hide can say as much.

Everything else here was the god first. The creeping things in the Barrows, the hands that walk, the veils of mouths on the Moor wind: each is a scrap of the corpse that got up and went about on its own. I have been followed by them. They do not hate you. That would be simpler. I have watched one stand a whole night outside a pilgrims' tent, near enough to lean on the canvas, and in the morning it had gone and the canvas was warm on that side.

A midwife on the Moor let me across her salt once, and watched my feet the whole way. I have been let across a great deal of salt since. Every hand that poured it has done the same. I do not blame them.

### What the Quiet leaves

When a thing made of the god dies, something stays: a bone, a husk, a smear, a smell, something to burn or bury or count. When the servants of the Unsaying are done with a thing, there is nothing. I walked into Coldhearth a month after they had been. The woodcutter tells what he found, below, and I will not tell it over him. I will tell you that of his fourteen graves by the old stone, one had not softened in the thaw. I knelt by it. The ground under my knees was dry, and the rest of that lane was mud to the ankle.

### Of the breath

The last breath is still going out of the god. On the Moor after dark you feel it as a steady pull at your back, always toward the same quarter. Every small god rides it: the one in the milestone, the one in the ford, the one in the bell. There are eight million of them by the House's count, and the House gave up counting long ago. Together they are the Myriad.

Where the breath snags, it thickens and goes wrong. That is miasma. I once drank from a well in the Barrow lands that had it, before I knew. The water was like a copper held too long under the tongue. For three nights I dreamed of a room with a green door that was not any room of mine, and on the fourth morning a child in the next village told me the same dream over her porridge.

### Of the lamps

There is no oil in any lamp on the Hide. Each one burns someone who stayed. I leave it to the tender at Ashwake to tell you how they choose and what they take; her letter is in this chapter, and she has wiped more glass than I have looked through. I will say only this. I have been carried home by a lamp more times than I will write. Mine leans east. I have never asked it why, because I think it would tell me.

### How I learned the five names

A god of three natures died, and five powers were left. I did not learn them from a book. I learned each in its own land, and every time from someone afraid.

The first under An-Vhar, from a spinning-woman who bit a thread and began a name and stopped halfway through it. Yh'Anuul. The Soul. In the same breath she called it the Veiled Crone, and when her spindle jumped she said Grandmother Spindle and slapped it. That one goes on weaving with nobody at the loom.

The second I heard every day for nine winters in the ossuaries. Oss-Vharoth, the Bone. The brothers say the Bearing Mother. They taught me to count. I will say no more of her until the third chapter, where they can speak for themselves.

The third a penitent at Villa Llaga sang to me at the edge of the wound, with her arms cut open to the elbow. Nol-Shogthuth, the Flesh. In the lowlands they say the Red Mother. At Villa Llaga they say the Bleeding Maiden, and when I am there so do I. The midwives say nothing at all if they can help it.

The fourth has no name to learn. It is the breath and all that rides it.

The fifth I have heard said plainly twice: once by an old monk of the Gilded Peak with an empty bowl on his knees, once by a preacher at the Dry Ford who had given away every other word he owned. Ur-Nihl. That is its one writing in this chapter. The Mouth With No Lips hears its name as you hear yours across a crowded room, and it will come to any place where the body lies open.

### The lands, as I came to them

I came out onto the Hide at the Ashen Moor, as the Tithed did, and I have been walking outward and downward from there ever since.

The Moor is where the face struck when the god came down. You can see it from the Fallen Watchtower: a long rise, a hollow, a rise again. The ash is warm there in every season. Under the Moor's edge the Rib Crypts go in between the ribs, nine galleries, and on some nights there is singing through the floor. I knelt on the ninth flag of the ninth gallery on one of those nights. I heard the three low notes the grandmothers keep, and then the fourth. I will not write the fourth.

Past the crypts lie the Barrows, where the Tithed bury their own, and every lid sinks from the middle. Beyond them the Drowned Fen, water you can wade out of dry, with a house of prayer under it and its bell still hung. North stands the Hollow Wood, pale trunks warm a hand's depth in; I have never cut one. The roots of the Wood all lean toward the mouth of the Root Deep, and I have gone down that mouth. It is warm under the Hide. I was spoken to down there, politely, and I answered politely, and came up in good order.

On the Burnt Heath, in the corner the road bends away from, seven great carcasses lie in a ring. My lantern halted at the edge of them and would come no further. I went in without it. What I found I keep.

Then off the Hide. The Bleached Barrens of Ossa, chalk and ribs standing in rows, and a citadel cut into a broken thighbone. South and down, Shog-Mire and its stilt-towns, where you learn to step between the beats of the mud or you stop learning. Up, higher than anywhere, the Heights of An-Vhar, where the frost lives, where a man hears his own blood in his ears all day and the cold gets into his lamp. And last the Descent, the road that goes in. Every road on the Hide tilts toward it a little. Set a full bowl on any table and watch which rim the water favours. The Vigil went down it with a lamp each and stayed down. I sat at its gate a season. I have been through the gate. That is all I will write of it here.

### The rings with teeth

On the Moor, in the Barrens, out on the flats of the Mire, you will come on a ring of broken stones like fangs round a pit of ash that rises and falls. Pilgrims walk down into them and come up at other rings. The next chapter belongs to them, and to the people who walk in. I will set down one thing here. I stood at a ring in the Barrens one night and watched a man go in. I was at the Ashwake fire the next morning when he came in asking for bread, and the Barrens are forty days from Ashwake. He asked me how I came to be at both. I gave him the answer he gave me.

### How these ten came to me

Ten works follow, and at the head of each I have written how I came by it. Some I paid for in salt. One I took down lying awake in a tent, and one with a lamp held to my ear, and I own to both. Not every writer here is right, and where one is wrong I have said so at the top of the page and nowhere else.

Now take your hand off the floor. You know whose it was.

## Relics

intro: Five things from this first land. Handle them with a dry hand.

### Warm Ash of the Moor

A handful of grey ash in oilcloth. I scooped it from under the Broken Kneeler on my first night on the Cheek. It has been warm every morning since, through winters on An-Vhar when my own fingers were not.

I believe it is the cooling of the flesh under the Moor, and that the cooling is not done. Once, deep in the Root Deep, the bundle stirred against my chest, slowly, keeping time with something larger. I kept walking.

### Husk-Glass

A shard of black glass, smooth on one face and rippled on the other. A gravedigger of the Barrows sold it to me for a knife-edge, and was sorry afterwards that he had not asked more.

Look into the smooth face and you see yourself, a little late. At the Dry Ford one night the lateness grew, and I watched my face shape the end of a word I had not yet begun to say.

### Lantern-Stone Wax

A drip of wax scraped from the foot of a lantern-stone at Ashwake. The tenders scrape the stones each morning and burn nothing that comes off them. The tender of that round gave me this as you would hand a stranger a lock of a dead man's hair.

It is warmer on the side that faced the road. I keep it in a shut tin. When I open the tin, it has turned toward me.

### A Page with Nothing on It

A leaf of good rag, ruled for writing, the rules still sharp, bound into a scholar's treatise on the god's name. The leaf before it ends in the middle of a word and the leaf after begins in the middle of another, and the two halves make nothing. This leaf is whiter than those on either side.

I know what stood on it. Twice I have tried to put it back, and twice the lines stayed empty. Leave the pen away from it. If one morning you find a word there, keep it in your mouth.

### A Knuckle-Bone from a Cradle

A small knuckle-bone, clean and old, the colour of a tooth. The midwife in these pages gave it to me. She had buried it under her doorstep in salt, as she does with such things, and three springs running it came up through the salt and lay on the step in the morning.

The child who was born holding it lives on the Moor still. The Pale Order sent down for him and wrote him into their book by a number. I asked the tallier the number and he told me, and I have not reduced it, because I do not want to know its root. Each spring I set the bone on the tithe-scale at Ashwake. I stopped keeping the weights some years ago.

## The Roads and the Stones

subtitle: of the pilgrims, the lamps that keep them, and the rings that are older than both
epigraph: The road was there before the feet. The feet only found it.

## Preface

Take your boots off tonight and turn one of your feet up to the lamp. If the sole is soft, this chapter is for you. If it is yellow and thick and you can press a thumbnail into it without feeling anything, you know most of what follows already, and you may read it to find where I have it wrong.

### Why anyone walks

I have asked at a great many fires why people are on the road, and I have never once been told that someone set out to see what was over the next rise. They go because their fathers went. They go to shrines whose gods were dead before the road was cut, and kneel there, and come home with feet like horn and a look about the eyes that their mothers do not have. Ask them what they went for and they look at you as though you had asked why they breathe.

I asked a boy of about twelve once, on the road below Ashwake, and he thought about it longer than the grown ones do. Then he said that if nobody walked the road it would forget where it went. I have not had a better answer since.

### The milestones

Every order on the Hide will tell you it cut the Pilgrim Road. It was there before any of them. I have walked it through the Hollow Wood and seen the pale roots turn aside at its edge, as a hand turns aside from a hot pot. The milestones came later. They were set in one autumn of the two hundred and sixth year after the god's death, by people who found the road already lying there and wanted it numbered. The figures go down as you walk out from Ashwake. None of the orders will tell you what they count toward. I spent one night beside the stone that says one. The ground past it looked like the ground before it. It was warmer.

The first milestone I ever leaned on was the ninth out of Ashwake. It came to my hip then. There is a beggar against it now who says it has grown past his shoulder in his time. I have no quarrel with him. I have stopped leaning on it too.

### Of waking by a lantern-stone

Along every road stand the lantern-stones, a day's walk apart where the ground is kind and nearer where it is not. The tenders keep the lamps. The lamps keep the walkers.

I have woken by a lantern-stone more mornings than I have woken under a roof. There is a way to do it. Before you sit up, say your own name under your breath and listen for whether any of it is missing. Then find your lamp. It will be lower on its stone than you left it, and it will not look at you for a while, the way a friend will not look at you after paying a debt of yours you did not know about. Then get up and walk, and do not stand at the stone trying to remember. The ones who stand there trying are the ones the tenders end by hanging new lamps for.

Some sit down by the stone and do not rise, and in a winter or two there is a new flame on the round. The tenders keep a book of every round. More names are written in it than there were ever tenders.

### Of the rings, briefly

Older than the roads and older than the lamps are the rings of stones with teeth. A measurer in this chapter gives their shape with a cord, and a walker gives their manners, and neither needs me. What the orders do about them is keep away.

I once watched one of the Order's knights lead his standing dead a long way off the white road to pass a ring out on the chalk, and his dead went round it wider than he did, without being asked. At the Fen stair I asked a Fan-Bearer what the stones were. She took out a square of paper and folded a doll while I waited, put it away in her sleeve, and spoke to me about the damp. The Brotherhood will not let its hooded ones stand in sight of one. The lodge would not read a thread past the place where it went into a ring. Only the pilgrims go near. They leave something at the lip when they go in: a button, a curl of hair, a child's milk tooth. By morning the lip is bare.

Nobody I have met has seen one raised. Fires ask me whether I have, and I say no, and it is true. I have seen one stoop a little lower over a man going down, the way a woman stoops over a cradle to see if the child is breathing.

### The first time I went in

Three people in these pages tell you what happens in the pit, and they tell it well, and I will not make it four. I will give you only what was different for me.

I went in first at the Barrow ring, long ago, with an iron ring on my finger because no one had told me to take it off. I remember that it did not hurt where the walkers say it hurts, and that it hurt somewhere they do not mention, behind the breastbone, like a word you were about to say and lost. In the dark there were others going the same way. It was warm as a crowded room is warm when everyone in it is on their knees. I have been in one room like that before. I was very small then.

I rose on the Kettlewick Flats, a long walk from the Barrows by any road, with the mud keeping its beat under the crust and a woman at the lip holding out a candle. The iron ring had gone to my other hand. I was hungrier than I have ever been, and bread did nothing for it, and I keep to myself what did.

I have gone in since, more often than I will number. My scars stay where they were. The walkers who watch me come up tell me that is uncommon, and then their eyes go down to my feet, and stay there a while.

### A thing I will say once

You will ask what is under the ash. So did the measurer, and her cord is in my bag. I have my belief, and it is in the eighth chapter as far as it is anywhere. Here I will say only that I have never come up at a ring I did not expect, and that I have never once chosen which.

### The walkers' leaves

Ten works follow, from people who walk and from people who stay put beside the walking. Each of them is wrong about something, and right about the stretch of road under their own feet. I have walked every stretch they tell of, and further on than most of them went, and I have let each of them keep the stretch they knew.

Put your boots back on before you sleep. You may have to leave in the night.

## Relics

intro: What I carry off the roads. Keep the bottle and the ash at opposite ends of the bag.

### Chip of a Waystone

A flake of grey stone, banded on its outer face. I found it in the long pale grass at the Fen-stair ring, in the spring the fallen stone there changed places, lying where the old fallen one had been.

It is cold on the outside and warm through the middle, like a stone taken from beside a hearth. Masons tell me its grain runs the wrong way for any quarry they know. A tooth-drawer in Kettlewick turned it over once in her fingers and handed it back quickly, and would not say what it reminded her of.

Sleep with it in your pack and you will sleep soundly. In the morning its point will have come round toward the nearest ring.

### The Measurer's Cord

A cord of knots a hand apart, stiff with dust, with a lead plumb still tied to one end. I found it at the lip of the Moor ring the spring after her last leaf, the plumb lying just over the edge in the ash. I drew it up myself. It came up warm.

She began with a hundred knots and wrote that three of them had crept together. I have counted it many times. It has a hundred and one. The last is tied very tight, in a hand I know, and I have not tried to loosen it.

### Wet Stain, Bottled

A small stoppered bottle of dark stuff from the foot of a waystone plinth. The measurer filled it at the Barrow ring, meaning to learn what it was. It lay beside her cord.

It has not dried. In warm weather it stands higher in the glass. Set it near the ash from the pit and it climbs the side of the bottle nearest.

I have wiped a hand on the stain at a plinth, as the walkers do. I have not wiped the hand since.

### Ash from the Pit

Grey ash in a twist of cloth, faintly warm, faintly moving. I scraped it from the crust of the Moor ring with a spoon, and left the spoon at the lip.

Poured onto a flat stone it gathers itself into a shallow dish, and lifts, and settles. The walkers say never take it far from its ring and do not say why. I carried it to An-Vhar and back, and for a long while nothing came of that. Then one night on the Heights I woke to find it lifting each time I breathed in.

I carry it now in a pocket far from my chest. If you take it from me, take it home.

### Tender's Rota-Book

A small book of the lantern-rounds at Ashwake. Each tender keeps the round a season and signs it over to the next. The tender who kept it the season of the child gave it to me with the child's leaf folded inside.

Toward the back one name stands nine times, in nine different hands. I knew four of the hands. After her letter the pages are blank.

The tenders have asked me to sign the next line. I have not. The book falls open at that page whatever I do.

### An Iron Ring

A plain iron ring, the kind a smith gives the boy who works his bellows. I wore it on my right forefinger into the Barrow ring the first time, and came up at Kettlewick with it on my left hand, on the little finger.

Since then I have kept the walkers' rule and taken it off at every lip. I have left it lying at more rings than one. When I come up it is on my hand again, on that small finger, and warm.

It is in the bag now. I checked before I wrote this line.

## The Pale Order

subtitle: of the servants of the Bone, with whom I counted nine winters
epigraph: They wrote me into their book before I had finished my bread.

## Preface

Wash your hands before this chapter, and dry them one finger at a time. The brothers would want it. I lived with them long enough to want it for them.

### How they got the name

The lowlands named them, not the other way about. I first heard the name at a bridge under the mountains, from a headman who had come out with bread and salt to meet seven knights, and I understood it once they had gone past me. Their plate was grey to the joints with a dust so fine it lay in the carving like frost in a footprint, and not one of them had brushed it off. That is the first reason. For the second, look up. Over the Ossa the sky has a pallor no other sky has, like cloth scrubbed too many times on the same stone. The third reason is said after dark, and I will come to it.

Among themselves they use an older word, Kostyak. A brother told it me at the gate of Nine Stairs while he dried my feet, counting the toes aloud as he went. He said it means the bones of a body, and also the few who keep a thing upright when the rest has sagged: the posts of a barn, the three old women every village has, the knots in a net.

### Nine winters

I came up behind the tithe in the nine hundred and eighty-first year after the god's death, nine days after the first frost. The brothers took the date for a good sign and explained why, and I let them. I stayed nine winters. I held a lamp at the Feast in each of them. I turned a windlass, stirred the vat, swept, and lay on the roof under the cords. The book had me by a number within the hour, and in nine winters no brother asked me for the other thing.

It is a kind house. I want that plain before anything else is said. They fed me when I came up thin. They nursed a brother of theirs through two winters of the coughing sickness and never once let him lie alone in the dark. They are courteous to the lowlands and patient with them, and they come for their dead.

### Under the stairs

The Order says it is as old as the world, and that its first brothers stood by when the Bearing Mother came down, and laid her where she lies. I do not argue that above ground.

Go down far enough in any of the nine houses and the stone changes under your hand. The Order's own halls are plain and grey, set against the older work the way a village sets its walls against a keep. Beneath them are other halls, cut to a measure no brother is made to. I climbed stairs there that I could not walk. I found long niches, and one night when I had no business there I lay down in one and stretched, and my feet found no end. Something had lain in it long enough to wear it smooth for its shoulders, and the smooth place was too far from my head.

The Order's chronicle begins in a hand no brother writes. I have read its first leaf. I will not write whose hand I think it is.

### The wells, and the dead who stand

Under every ossuary floor the Brothers of the Well draw up what the Mother gives. I worked a handle there one winter. What comes up I leave to the well-brother in this chapter, who says it better and is still at it. What the work does to a man who stands beside it, I can tell you. My knees were warm by spring. Not one morning since have they been cold, on An-Vhar, on the white road, at the gate of the Descent. I set that down and go on.

With that marrow the brothers anoint their dead, and the dead stand. I saw a whole wall come down out of its niches at a brother's word, quietly, like people rising at the end of a service, to carry one shin-bone from a lowland field up the stair. I never saw one of them refuse. I never saw one of them hurry.

### The ledge

At first light the sweepers carry the dust of their brothers up to the highest ledge and open their hands. I went up with them nine times. The cold there gets into your teeth. The dust goes up. Each time I watched until my eyes ran, and I never saw any of it come down, and on my ninth morning, without thinking, I put my palm up flat over my head as you do in a low cellar. The sweeper beside me saw. He did not laugh. He took my wrist and brought the arm down, and held it down, and said nothing all the way back to the stair.

### Of their counting

I will not teach you the Count. The tallier does that, below, and the novices' catechism does, and a boy from Cold Adit learns it in his letters to his mother, and between them you will have more of it than a lowlander should carry. I learned it on the stair from the same man who wrote the tallier's leaves, and I count by it still, wanted or not. I cannot see three candles on a table now without adding them.

What I can give you is what I did with it. At my first Feast they told my bones on a cord, as they do for any guest who allows it. The tallier reached the end, smiled at me, and wrote the figure. I have let nobody tell them since. I held the lamp at nine Feasts while the talliers read the walls, and I never once saw a wall come out lower than the year before. The brothers have words for that. I said the words with them. I say them still, some nights, over things that are not bones.

### The roof

Above Nine Stairs a frame of long bones is strung with cords like a loom, and a brother lies under it all night reading the dark. I lay beside him one winter. He showed me how to keep still so my breath would not cloud the cords. He did not talk much. Once, toward morning, he asked whether I thought the points up there knew they were being counted, and before I could answer he said, "No, leave it," and turned his face back up.

His leaf from under the stone is in this chapter. It had five points on it when he first showed it me. I will not tell you how many it had the last time I saw it.

### Grandfather Dust

An Ossuarch is the warden of one ossuary. On the stair the word is ordinary. In the lowlands it means one man.

I have seen him three times. Once on a ridge at dusk, the standing dead behind him taller than any the Order keeps. Once at the grate of Nine Stairs, from the inside, holding the second lamp. And once in the Barrens, far off and walking away, with the white stones he had set behind him running back toward the Ossa as straight as a chalked thread.

That line is the Pale. It runs out of the Ossa across the Hide and is marked by nothing but the white stones a warden sets where he has gathered bone. The knights ride the low road and come home again. A warden on the Pale goes outward. That is the third reason for the name, the one the lowlands keep for after dark. When one of theirs has gone too far to be fetched back, they say he has gone beyond the Pale, and they say it the way you draw a blanket up over a sleeper's face.

I walked beside a length of it once. The ground behind the stones was whiter than the ground ahead.

The villages frighten their children with him. The Order shuts every gate against him and will not say why aloud. I know one reason. It is not the reason in the Warden's book.

### What they keep

Every order hides something. This one hides it in plain grey rooms with a brother standing before the door, facing the stair. I was a guest nine winters, and I went down the Ninth Stair once, which a guest may not do. While I was there I asked nothing. Under the letters cut above that stair are older letters, worn nearly smooth, and I know how many lines of them there are. I have kept the number back.

### What passed the gate of Nine Stairs

Ten works follow. Eight came to me through that gate, and two came down the mountain in a pedlar's pack. I was a guest of this house for nine winters, and I have tried not to let that make me gentle with it at the head of any page, or hard.

If you find yourself counting as you read, count in your head, and leave the figures unreduced.

## Relics

intro: What I carried down out of the Ossa. Take each one in both hands.

### Warden's Chrism

A stoppered pot of marrow from the wells under the ossuaries, amber and slow, warm however long it is kept. I rendered it myself at the vat in my second winter, and the Brothers of the Well let me take one pot for the road, because I had stirred well.

I have used it twice, both times on my own hands. In all the years I have carried it, the pot has not gone down by a finger's width. I have stopped looking at the line.

### Niche-Tally

A strip of bone cut with notches: the count of the fourth wall at Nine Stairs, taken at one Feast. It is the tally the old sweeper's letter was folded into.

The count of the brothers' own dead is cut along the top. What the Order calls the Mother's share is cut in a row of its own beneath. I have run my thumb along the lower row in the dark. It is the longer of the two.

### Cord of the Frame

A knotted cord worn smooth by a brother's fingers, the knots set close for a whole man. A tallier gave it to me on the morning its brother was laid.

It has one knot more than it should. Nobody on the stair would say who tied it. I have told it every night since, to be sure, and I am sure.

### Pinch of the Finished

Bone dust in a fold of rag, from the last pan the old sweeper of the Fourth Stair ever filled. He gave me one pinch and turned his face away while I took it.

I have opened the rag in every land on the Hide. Everything else in the bag has shifted over the years: a corner of the chrism-pot's seal, the tally's curve, the lie of the knots. The dust lies exactly as he folded it. Turn your face aside when you open it.

### Star-Bone

A shard of white bone, colder than the bones in the niches ever are, hollow of marrow, both ends broken clean. It split from a larger piece when three of the standing dead lifted it out of a field, eleven days down the line of a fallen star, and the Seeker I walked with let me keep it.

It fits no frame the talliers know. I have laid it against every bone I have handled since, and it is shaped to fit something. At every stopping on the way back to Nine Stairs I weighed it, and it was heavier each time, and the talliers took the weights down without a word.

### Pale-Stone

A white waymark no taller than a knee, cut with one number, found where no road runs. It stood in the Barrens three days beyond the Citadel, and the chalk on its near side was the paler.

The Order keeps a chart of these in the tally-room of the Ninth Stair. Joined in the order of their numbers, they make one line that doubles back and bends and never crosses itself. The line is longer every year, and no warden now walking has set its newest stones. This one is lighter on the road outward than on any road back. I have stopped walking back.

### Stair-Stone of the Old Halls

A corner broken from the lowest stair of an ossuary, tread and riser both, knee-high to a man, worn hollow by feet much longer than a man's. It came away under my hand the night I lay down in the long niche. I had leaned on the step to climb out.

The hollow in the tread is deepest at the front edge, where a foot pushes off going down. The brothers' feet push off going up.

## The Gilded Peak

subtitle: of the monks on the knee, and what they carried up the stair
epigraph: An old monk on the landing, when I asked him whom they waited for: "Someone who is late, and will come."

## Preface

Hold one hand out in front of you, palm up. Open it wide, then close it, slowly, nine times. Let the hand learn it before the head does. The brothers of this chapter would have said the head never learns it at all.

### Going up

The Peak is the god's knee, broken up through the Hide when the body came down, and the monks cut their stair into the bone of it. I first climbed it in the summer of the one thousand and seventy-first year, while the Peak was whole and rang at noon. A brother met me at the landing where pilgrims who would go no higher left their bread. He did not ask my business. He only fell in behind me, a little lower on the stair than I was, and climbed the rest of the way there. I did not understand until I stumbled, near the top, and found him already braced to take my weight.

From the courtyard the miasma lay in the valleys like milk left standing in a pan, and the lowland towns under it showed only as lamps.

They called themselves the Kinrei-shū, the Gold Bell. A monk owned a bowl, a robe, and later a lantern, and nothing else. I sat one evening in their hall among them while they chanted. How the chant goes is not mine to give; a woman who sang it four winters tells you below, and so does the man who taught it. I will tell you that in the third of the long quiet places, the brother beside me pressed his shoulder hard against mine, and kept it there, the way you steady a man at the lip of a drop, and that I did not know until then that I had been leaning.

### The bell at noon

At noon they struck the big bell that hangs over the courtyard. I stood a few paces from it. The beam went in, and I felt the blow in my teeth and heard nothing, and every monk round me let out his breath at once, content. Two days later I was in a market in the valley when the noon stroke came down the mountain, and it rang so long the bread-sellers stopped calling to let it end.

A bell-caster tells you in this chapter what his wife heard. I stood in that market. She heard right.

### What they waited for

Going down at dusk I found an old monk sitting on a landing with an empty bowl across his knees, and I asked him to whom they prayed. He said the fifth name, the one I wrote once in the first chapter. He said it as a man speaks of a guest expected for supper, who is late, and will come. I have not heard it said so gently anywhere else.

Every other house on the Hide bars its door against the Unsaying. The Peak laid a place for it. I have stood nearer the Long Silence than those monks ever meant to stand, and I cannot call it what they called it. And still, every argument I have made against them since, I can hear the old man on the landing answering, in one breath, without raising his eyes from the bowl.

### Of the gold, and the saints

They gilded everything they meant to honour, and they meant to honour it by making it hard to see. The goldbeater in this chapter tells you how, and why, better than I can. I was never let into the sanctum. I will tell you only what I saw from its door on my first visit: a row of niches going away into the dark, and in each a shape so bright at the edges that the eye slid off it, and a novice with a dry cloth going from one to the next along the row, reaching up.

I have my own belief about the saints. I keep it in the bag with the gold. The goldbeater was nearer to them than I ever was. Read him slowly.

### Of the bowls

They came down to the valleys at dusk in fours, barefoot, with small bells at their belts, and asked for one thing. The lowlanders gave it, and forgot, and fed the monks bread for the forgetting. Where the gifts went, the Peak did not ask, or asked and got an open hand for answer. The bread-woman, the magistrate and the abbot each tell you a piece of what came of it. None of them has the whole. I have not either, and I have walked further along it than they did.

A monk of the Peak carried his teacher in his lantern, and the teacher burned his own name for fuel, slowly, down to nothing. I once walked a day behind a brother carrying his teacher down to the valley. He set his feet on each riser as though the stair were ice over a deep pool, and at the bottom he sat down on the last step and held the lantern in his lap until it was dark.

### The tenth night

The Pale Order learned that the Peak gilded its dead and gave them to the Quiet, and left the Count nothing. To the brothers of the Ossa there is no worse waste on the Hide. Their standing dead waited nine days under the mountain, and on the tenth night they climbed.

It was the spring of the one thousand and ninety-eighth year. The brothers will tell you that year comes down to nine, and they say it as though it closed the matter. I was in the valley, at a ford, with my back against a stone. I heard nothing all night. I have come to think that nothing was what there was to hear.

The monks did not fight. When the sun came, the flags of the courtyard had gone to glass and the sanctum stood open. What happened between, no two tellers give the same way, and I was not there to correct any of them.

I went up in the thaw. I did not go into the sanctum. I stood beside the door, never in front of it, with my shoulders to the stone, and looked down at my own palm until the sun was at the top of the sky. I could not have told you why I stood so until a letter came to me two winters later, folded inside a bowl. The Order's scribe gives the count of what he found. He has the stair right both ways.

### Of the one who comes at dusk

A barefoot man with a bowl walks the lowland roads and asks for a name, and a black flame follows him a little behind. What he strikes leaves glass, or dust, or nothing. Some say he was down in the valleys with his bowl on the tenth night. Some say he was first up the stair after, and opened the doors. Some say he walked out of the empty niche.

I know this much, and set it beside what they say. In a pilgrim-roll at the Ashwake scriptorium, in a hand three hundred and four years older than the fall, a scribe writes of a barefoot brother with a black lamp who asked him for a name and was given one. I saw the bowl go past Aumery's door, alone, while the Peak still sent its brothers down in fours. And once, at the Dry Ford, he held the bowl out to me. I have more names than most, and I gave him none. He waited, and went on down the road. I still cannot say whether he was asking for a name or offering to carry one.

### Seven, from the valleys under the knee

Seven works follow. Two were written on the Peak, one before the dead climbed and one after, and the other five by people who lived under the mountain and looked up at it every day. I have been up the stair. I trust the ones who looked up.

Now open your hand, and leave it open until you turn the page.

## Relics

intro: What I brought down from the knee. Keep them in the dark of the bag, and never set them out at noon.

### Begging Bowl of the Peak

A plain wooden bowl, its rim darkened by hands. It came to me from the lowland priest with the abbot's letter lying in it, and the priest was glad to see it go.

It is heavier than its wood. At an inn by the Dry Ford I set it on a table among carters. Their talk stopped, and started again on some other matter, and none of them could say what they had been discussing. One of them wept a little and laughed at himself for it.

Do not speak into it. Do not speak near it.

### Gilded Leaf

One leaf from the last book the goldbeater beat, the one he never carried up. It stirs when you breathe near it, and now and then a moment before you do.

At its middle is a pinhole fine as a hair. I laid it over the back of my hand one noon, and the hand was gone, and light lay on my wrist in the shape of fingers. The pinhole stayed dark. Something small and warm crossed it, as breath crosses a mouth.

### Black-Flame Lantern

A lamp of the Peak, the gold on its case worn through at the handle. The flame is black, and still I can see the road by it. A brother of the Peak who had been in the valleys on the tenth night handed it to me at the foot of the old water-cut in the thaw, and asked me to hold it while he went up to open the doors. I held it. He did not come down.

He told me whose name it burns. I have never said that name within its hearing. Once at a fire I said another, a woman's, and the flame drew in toward me like a hungry man leaning toward a pot. It is very small now. I carry it carefully, as he did.

### Tongueless Bell

A bell the size of a cupped hand, gilded so thick it shows no face, only light. No clapper and nowhere to hang one, no seam and no mark of a mould. The bell-caster found it in the cold ash of his own furnace and swears he never cast it. I gave him more lamp oil than it was worth to him, and he let it go as a man sets down water he has carried a long way.

It stays cold. The Moor ash rides beside it in my bag, and the ash on that side has gone pale. Some nights I wake and the wrapping is warm. It has never rung where I could hear. Each time I find the wrapping warm I have lost something, and I have not yet found out what.

### Saint Under Gold

A small gilded figure the length of my forearm, seated, hands open on its knees. Nine such sat on the landings for pilgrims who would climb no higher. I took this one from its landing in the thaw after the fall, because the miasma was coming up the stair and I would not leave it anything.

I wipe its face each morning with a dry cloth. By night I have to wipe it again. Once, with a knife-point, I lifted the gold at the throat. There was more gold under it, and under that something warm. I laid the gold back and pressed it down with my thumbnail until no seam showed.

I have not lifted it again.

### Courtyard Glass

A palm-sized piece of dark glass, flat as still water, from the edge of the courtyard where the flags end. It is warm at every hour.

There is one shape in it, very faint, small and on its knees. Wherever on the Hide I take it out of the bag, the shape is facing the Peak. On bad roads I have steered by it.

### Burnisher of the Beating-House

A finger-long tooth, fixed into a dark wooden haft. The goldbeater put it in my hand when I paid him for the leaf. He would take nothing more for it, and kept his eyes on his bench while I held it.

Nobody on the mountain would say whose teeth they were. I have never pressed gold with it, and the edge goes on wearing down.

## The Polished Heart

subtitle: of the lodge at the head of the cold lakes, and the winter its dead came home
epigraph: Every soul is a thread. I only hold the end of it up to the light, so it can see where it goes.

## Preface

If there is a glass in the room with you, leave it as it is. Do not cover it for my sake, and do not uncover it. By the end of this chapter you will have an opinion about it, and an opinion is all that valley has left to give anyone.

### The valley of cold lakes

The spinning-woman under An-Vhar who gave me the Soul's name told me where to go for the rest of it. Down, she said, and west, to where the Old Spinner's threads come up through the skin like roots through a path. She wound the loose end of her yarn round my finger before she let me go, and I walked with it on for nine days because I did not like to take it off.

The valley is long and narrow, with a chain of lakes down its floor, each colder than the one above it. In winter, when the lakes skin over, you can see the threads a hand's depth down in the ice: pale, lying every way at once, like hairs caught in wax. I lay on my belly on the third lake one noon and tried to follow a single strand with my eye. I lost it under my own reflection. When I stood up there was a white mark on the ice where my breath had been, shaped like a mouth, and it did not fade until dusk.

The dye-women of the Lower Reach would not rinse cloth in that water. You could not tell afterwards, they said, which threads were your own.

### A guest in the ring

The lodge stood at the head of the valley, where the strands in the ice lie thickest. It stands still, if walls are standing. Grey felt blows about the yard.

I sat in their circle once, years before the end, at the outer edge where they put guests. A keeper began the names and they went round from mouth to mouth. I will not give them to you in order; the circle-keeper does that, below, and he kept that office far longer than I kept my place. What it was to sit there, I can say. Somewhere after midnight I noticed my own mouth moving with the rest and could not remember having started. By the grey hour I could not have said where the ring ended and I began, and I did not mind, and that frightened me more afterwards than anything I heard in that valley.

A boy with grey hands brought round the water at dawn. He did not look at any of the glass in the yard. He looked at me, for longer than a boy should look at a guest, and then went on with the jug.

### What the lodge sold

Every house in the valley paid the lodge for felt and ash and one lesson, and I have watched the lesson kept in a dozen houses of the dead. The women moved from room to room with the grey cloths over their arms, fast, without talking, the way you close shutters before a storm. The lodge's people believed it. The valley believed it. I believed it for as long as I was in the valley, and I have covered glass myself, in a house where a man died with nobody else to do it.

The mirror-maker gives you the trade from the grinding side. He was honest. He says he believed the lesson the way he believed quicksilver runs downhill. I have never found a better way to say it, and I have tried.

### The loose winter

It came when the ice first held at the ford, in 1106 by the Ashwake reckoning, three nights past the new moon. I was on the hill above the lodge when the runners came up from the Reach. I had come to buy felt. I stayed forty nights.

What the Pir did in those nights you have twice over below: from the man who kept the ring round her, and from her own leaves. I leave them to each other. I will only say that on the last night I was near enough to hear the ring stop, all at once, as a mill stops when the race is shut, and that the quiet afterwards was the quiet of a room where somebody has just understood something.

The dead came home that night. I saw them come. There was no walking in it. The lane held frost and ash, and then it held them. They went to the doors. The valley sent them up to the lodge, and walked behind them. I did not walk behind. I stood on the hill, where I could see the whole yard, and so I saw the felt come off the coat, and heard the dead draw breath at the glass as a congregation draws breath before it sings.

One of them did not stop. The flax-beater tells of her, the blind one. She passed out of the yard where there was no gate and climbed toward me in the dark. She asked me the way. I answered her. I will not write what I said.

### What he carries now

The boy with grey hands kept every one of them. They go about him small and pale, trailing their threads. The valley calls him Needle-Brother. I call him the Polisher still, because that was his name when he brought me water.

He hung the coat back on its post and asked the dead into the iron, and it stood up off the post as a knight twice his height, with a lamp burning where the heart-glass had been. I watched that knight taken apart at the knees in the Hollow Wood by things that came out of the ground, and I watched the lamp in its breast draw down to a bead. By morning it stood at his shoulder again, whole. He told me he had not mended it. I believe him. The glass keeps its own count.

For the rest, he sells one thing, which is a little time. A widow of the upper valley tells you how he does it. I have watched him do it three times and I have nothing to add to her, except that each time, before he began, he looked at the covered glass in the room for a while, as though he were asking its leave.

I sat at a fire with him in the Barrens a winter ago. I asked him whether he was sorry. He watched the lights go round him for a long time and said, "The villages still bring me their dead. I keep them." I took it then for half an answer. I have since decided it was the whole of one.

I know something of where the long way went, the way the lodge sent every covered thread for a thousand years. I have walked a good distance down it. I stopped. I did not stop because I reached an end.

### Leaves out of the valley

Six works follow. Three reached me through the Pale Order, who sent copyists up the valley after the loose winter to count what the lodge had left, and found almost no bone to count. The other three I had from a tinker, a hiring fair and a fire in the Barrens. I have taken no side in that valley, which is why both halves of it still sell me felt.

When you have read them, look at the glass in your room, if there is one, and decide.

## Relics

intro: From the valley of cold lakes. Do not set any of them facing you while you sleep.

### Covering Cloth

A square of grey felt the size of a hand-glass, darkened with ash at the edges, with a dent in one corner where a stone sat on it. It came to me in the box from the fair, with the thread-reader's leaf folded inside it.

It has been laid over a glass once, on a night when nobody in the house had died. The Polisher keeps the felt he pulled from the coat folded small in his own coat, and has never used it. I keep this one folded the other way from his.

### Mourner's Needle

A steel needle as long as a forearm, threaded with something pale that is nearly light. The Polisher carries three. He gave me this one at the fire in the Barrens and would not say what for.

The thread has never run short, and nobody has wound more onto it. At night, if I lie close to the bag, I hear it hum, faintly, as a string hums in a wind that is not blowing.

### Pir's Mirror

One of the small hollowed rounds from the Pir's coat, rimmed in black iron, with a thumb-print pressed into the wax on its back. It fell from the coat's shoulder on the first night the iron stood up, and the Polisher let me keep it.

Look into it and you see your face, then a face behind yours, then another behind that. The last face in every round of that coat is turned away. When I count, I begin at the second.

### Polishing Felt

A pad of grey felt worn through in the middle, stiff with ash and a little blood. I picked it out of the yard where the felt blows about.

It is worn in the shape of a hand, and the hand is smaller than the Polisher's is now. The circle-keeper knew at once whose it was. I offered it to him. He would not touch it.

### Heel of a Loaf

The heel of a small dark loaf, hard as a whetstone, with a thumb pressed deep into the crumb. It has been in my bag a long time.

In that valley a woman presses her thumb into bread she hands to someone she fears for. I have not eaten it. Some mornings it is warm on the thumbed side. The apprentice's leaf tells as much as I will about how it came to me.

## The Precious Wound

subtitle: of the Flesh, and the town that paid her by the cup
epigraph: Blood given is a word said to someone. My brothers said it for a thousand years, and forgot to say to whom.

## Preface

If you have a cut on you anywhere, bind it before you begin. Use clean cloth. While you tie the knot, tell yourself in a few words what the blood was spent on. Any words will do.

### The woman on the step

The penitent who taught me the Flesh's names at Villa Llaga was the one who had been away. I met her in the spring after the Fall. She was sitting on the lip with her tin cup in her lap, the way a woman sits on her own doorstep shelling beans, and she sang the names through for me so that I would have them in the right order. I will not write them in that order. She wrote them herself, below, and she has more right.

She sang the lowland name faster than the others, as if it shamed her. The true one she sang last, low, with her arms held out over the dark water in the wound, and the drops went off her elbows on the second part of it. I have not heard it sung since, and I do not want to.

Afterwards she explained the goddess to me the way you explain the rooms of a house to a child who is staying the night. Then she laughed at something I had not said. I think she had stopped believing the part about the goddess having no mind.

### The town on two lips

Villa Llaga is built down both sides of a wound in the Hide as long as a morning's walk, that has never closed. The houses stand shoulder to shoulder along either lip, and the gutters of both streets fall inward to it. The gutters are dark. They run the colour of old brown ink, and after a procession they go black at the edges. Anyone who writes that they run red was not there.

I walked the Day of the Measure once, before the Fall, in the year 1091, standing on the upper lip in a borrowed hood. I will not describe the procession. The Rule describes it, the child describes it, the penitent describes it, and each has it right. What they do not tell you is the sound the wound makes when it drinks. It is small. It is a sound like a mouth at a cup, and it was the only sound in the town while the brothers poured, and it went on a little after the last of them had stopped.

The brothers kept their accounts in blood-ink and to the drop. By their book they had paid for a thousand years. It was a thousand and eleven. They rounded down, and it is the only time in all their books I caught them rounding.

That night I went down into the crypt with them, two by two, and a saint who had come out of its niche spoke to me by a name that was not mine. I answered it gently. I will not write the name.

### The man at the back

I watched the sangrador that day. He walked last, as he always did. While the brothers poured he did not once look at the wound. He watched their hands, the way a clerk watches coin going across a table, and his lips moved, and I think he was keeping the tally in his head as well as on the stick.

The brotherhood had found him as an infant at the feet of the faceless image over the crypt stair, and raised him, and kept him the one man in the town she never tasted. The carver who made that image tells you what went into it. The carver was not let watch. I was not in the room either. I have my suspicion. It is in the bag.

### The crust, and the night

Then the wound closed over for a year, and in that year the brothers poured onto the crust as they had poured into the wound. I did not see the crust. I came too late for it, or too early; I have never been sure which.

I was on the upper lip on the night it opened. What came up out of it is not mine to tell; four people in this chapter have told you, and the one who told it lying in a drain has told it best. I will tell you that what came up went past me in a doorway. They filled the door, low down, and swayed at me a little, and went on down the street. I have thought about why for a long time. I have not written down what I thought.

Afterwards the sangrador opened his own arm for the first time in his life, on the crust, and drew a door in his blood, and called her down into himself. I have seen the shape of that door since, drawn on a wall in a cellar under An-Vhar, by a hand that was not his. I will not draw it for you.

Everyone who tells what happened next says that she took his blood and his hands and his voice and left him his mind. I have said it too. Some nights I wonder whose mind it is now, and which of the two of them is keeping it.

### What walks out of Villa Llaga now

The hunter in this chapter saw the carved Maiden bound face down on the great sewn one's back. The penitent saw her face up. I have seen her both ways, on two nights a year apart, and I do not know who turns her.

I have lain in a ditch while the procession went past on the road above me. I will set down only what I have that the others lack. The small hooded ones step exactly where the one before stepped, every foot in the last foot's print, so that a hundred of them leave a track in the dust that looks like the track of one. And when he spoke, and I lay down flat as everyone who has heard him lies down, the ground under my face was warm, and it was beating.

### Paid for, found, and carried

Seven works follow, and not one was written by somebody who hoped to be read. I bought some, found some, and was handed the rest by people glad to be rid of them. The Brotherhood would not thank me for any of them, and one of them I have kept a line back from.

If you bled at all while you were reading, you know now what to say over it.

A shallow cup of beaten tin, stained to the rim, with a line cut inside it and a ladder of older lines below. It is the penitent's cup, the one her litany was rolled in.

A small tin heart, pierced for hanging, of the kind pilgrims nailed round the image over the crypt stair, each one a prayer for a healing. Thousands hung there before the Fall.

### A Tenth Cord

A hand-span of tithe-cord, knotted once, cut clean at both ends. I found it in the dust of the dyers' road below a lowland mill, where the great sewn one had passed in the night.

I untied the knot once, at my fire, to see how it was made. In the morning it was tied again, and there were two knots. I have not untied it since. I no longer count them.

## The House of Eight Million

subtitle: of the paper dolls, and the one who came up
epigraph: Give it to me. All of it. I have room.

## Preface

Empty your chest before you read this. Push the air out until nothing more will come, and a little past that. Now hold, and count. You will not reach far. That short count is all the room a person has, and this chapter is about the people who try to make it longer.

### A crowd on the wind

The first chapter told you the god is still breathing its last across the Hide. It did not tell you the breath is crowded.

I learned its names as you learn the names of people in a crowd, one face at a time, from whoever stood nearest. The reed-cutters of the Fen say the Eight Million. Up at the salt pans they say the Long Exhale, and let their own air out through the teeth after they say it. A widow at Bellrest called it the Sigh Under the Door and laid a rolled cloth along her sill while she talked. The grandmothers of the Moor say Grandmother Wind, as though it were one more of them, sitting a little apart from the fire. The House says the Small Gods, when it says anything.

When I was young, younger than anyone living would credit, I asked the Myriad its true name. I asked the ford-bell at the salt crossing, because it was said to answer. It has not rung in my hearing since. It rings once I am past, far enough off; I feel it on the road, behind the breastbone, as you feel a door shut somewhere else in a house.

### The doll that stood straight

Wherever the wind snags on something, the House has been there before you: a gate of two posts and a lintel at the crossing, a stone at the spring, a strip of white paper knotted to the bell. They kept the forgotten shrines too, when they had the hands for it. Lately they have not had the hands.

I let one of them sit with me once, knee to knee, at a little roadside shrine in the Fen reaches. She was grey at the temples and in no hurry. The elder in this chapter tells a novice what is meant to happen with the doll, and I will leave it to her. I will tell you what happened with mine. It did not move. The purifier watched it a long while. Then she folded its arms shut, carefully, laid it in the brazier, and turned her face from the smoke, and would not take my salt when I offered it.

I have thought since about what she saw in the paper that made her do it. I have decided she saw nothing, and that nothing was what she did not like.

### What they cannot carry

The House fears one thing more than its own ending. Where the servants of the Quiet have gone by, there is nothing left in the air to take in. A purifier can hold a wrong breath until it bends her. She cannot hold an absence. One summer a village at the edge of the Fen went wrong all at once, past what the purifiers could take in, and the Mouth That Eats Its Own Echo came through it. I stood beside a Fan-Bearer at the end of its lane that morning, and watched her raise the fan, and lower it, and raise it, like someone trying a key in a door that has no lock.

### Of the Nave

At the one clean pool in the Drowned Fen stands the Well-Shrine, and under the water beyond it lies the Drowned Nave. The boatman, the shrine's day-book, the elder and the doll each tell you part of what the Nave gave back, and what went down to meet it, and who came up. I was not at the Well-Shrine that spring. I came a season after, in 1071 as I count years, and sat on the step where the elder with the fever had sat, and read the day-book through from the beginning while the keepers watched me to see which page I would stop at.

I stopped where the hand shakes. Anyone would.

### On the bank

I have seen an oni three times, and each time on a bank at night, and each time it kept out of my lamplight. The third time I set the lamp down on the reeds and walked away from it into the dark, to see what the thing would do. It came a little nearer the lamp, and stood looking at it with its hands shut against its chest, the way a cold man stands near a fire in a house where he has not been asked in. I went back for the lamp before dawn. The reeds round it were pressed flat in a ring, as though something heavy had sat by it most of the night and then thought better of it.

The reed-camp women hum at their folding where there used to be words. I sat a whole evening on the mats once and never heard the words. I heard the place where they went, which is louder.

I have been down the Nave stair since. The House gives a number for its steps. I made it more. I will not write my number, and I will not write which way the pews face now.

### At a crossing on the Barrow road

I met the one who came up in the thaw of the year 1103, at a crossing where the lowlands had forgotten what the stone was for. Faded red and white, a wide hat, down on both knees in the mud. She set her palms to the guardian stone and put it right, and breathed on it, and the air at the crossing eased the way a kitchen eases when a quarrel is over. By morning it had moved a finger's width again. She knew it would. She went on to the next.

I counted her breathing while she worked, as the reed-cutter's wife counts in this chapter; she taught me the trick, years before she wrote it down. Twenty of mine went by, and one of hers.

By the day-book's reckoning she ought to be older than she looks. I have done that sum twice and put it away.

The one in the haze beside her held the fan while she knelt. I bowed to it. I did not ask its name. You will not ask it either.

### What the water let me keep

Eight works follow. The House would have kept at least one of them out of this book if I had let it, and I did not. Water gives things back dry. These came back to me the same, and I have put nothing of mine on them but a few lines at the top.

Now breathe out. You have been holding it.

## Relics

intro: What the House gave me, and what it did not. Carry them lightly; each is heavier than it looks.

### Paper Doll

A doll of pressed reed paper, arms out, of the kind the House leaves on doorsteps in the lowland camps. I found it on the step of a hut where I sheltered, the week the Fen came clear again. I stopped. No one came at dusk.

I unfolded it once, at the shoulder only. The inside of the paper was grey, as though something had been breathed into it and kept. I folded it back along its own creases and have not opened it since.

### Guardian Stone, Turned

A shrine stone the size of a loaf, worn smooth on one face, from a crossing on the Barrow road where there is no shrine any more. I carried it off because nobody else would put it right.

Each night I set it by the fire facing me. Most mornings it faces the dark. I have laid my ear to the smooth face. I turned it back quickly, and I have not laid my ear there again.

### Purifier's Fan

A folding fan of oiled paper on ribs of bone, patched many times, some of the patches older than the fan. A novice's family gave it to me when her time came. They did not want it in the house.

I opened it once on the Fen road, a rib at a time, and the reeds on that side all leaned away from me. Never open it toward a lamp.

### Bent Twig

A green twig bent double and tied, of the kind pilgrims leave on a post to find the way back. It lay at the foot of the Well-Shrine gate the morning the guardian stone was found facing the wall. A keeper gave it to me years later, saying she could not bring herself to burn it.

It is still green. However I lay it, on a table, in a bag, on a barn floor, it points to the Nave. Past the gate of the Descent it pointed down.

### Knot-Cord

A cord of plaited white paper, knotted along its whole length, long enough to coil on the floor. It belonged to the first keeper whose hand is in the day-book. She went to the water, and the cord stayed dry on the step.

She could not have tied so many knots in three lives. I have counted them twice and got two numbers, and I have stopped. Some mornings the coil is bigger.

## Before the Last Breath

subtitle: voices from the Held Space, and what I will not add to them
epigraph: A miner at the Moor workings, asked what was under the east drift: "Manners."

## Preface

I have put this chapter off for four winters. The others I wrote at whatever fire I happened to be sitting at. This one has ridden in the bag unwritten, beside the things that close it, and the bag has weighed more than its contents the whole while. Tonight I am at the Ashwake fire, and three pilgrims are asleep round it. Their breathing is uneven, one and then another and then the third, never together. That is why I can write tonight. By the end you will know why that matters.

Lower the wick. Lower again.

### What I said in the first chapter

In the first chapter I wrote that in the oldest age nobody was there. I wrote it the way you call a room empty from its doorway, with your lamp held in and your feet still outside. I have stood in a room since where it was not so. I will come to it last.

I also wrote that the congregation knelt inside the god near the end. Near the end is the important part. The Tithed remember the kneeling and nothing earlier. A grandmother keeps three notes. On Kneel-Night the camps on the Cheek kneel from dark to grey round a banked fire, and nobody can tell you why the fire must be banked. That is everything the congregation kept, and they came late.

I knelt with them one Kneel-Night, at a camp below the Jaw, between a salt-boiler's widow and a boy who kept falling asleep against my arm. Near the grey hour the widow leaned across and asked me, quite kindly, whether I would like to be the one to bank the fire, since I knew how. I had not touched the fire. I had not said I knew anything. I banked it. My hands did it the old way, the ash drawn over from the far side first, and three of the old women stopped their kneeling to watch, and none of them would speak to me in the morning.

Others had been in the Held Space a long while before them, and those others had work.

### The ones who were already there

I will not describe them. Five of them speak in this chapter, through five copyists, and every copyist has done what I would not dare, which is set their words down straight. I will tell you only where I have been near them, and what I did.

I have walked the Lower Stacks at night and heard, from the knee-high stairs, a sound like somebody very large lowering itself onto a bench, slowly, over about the time it takes to say a short prayer. I did not go to see who.

I have stood on a flagstone of the Rib Crypts while it lifted under my boots, and heard what comes through when it lifts. I wrote in the first chapter what I heard that night. I did not write that it sounded like the beginning of something I already knew, and that I knew the next part, and that I shut my mouth on it with my own hand.

I have been down into the warm under the Hide and spoken with what is there, and come up in the order I went down. I have counted my fingers since, as pilgrims do. They come to what they should. It is the other things I cannot count.

I have sat by the Descent's gate while a brother of the Vigil climbed up past me with a copyist's pages in his coat, and did not stop, and did not look at me, and went on up toward the light as though he were late.

They do not agree about much, these voices. They count wrongly, every one, and speak of time as a room they are standing in. They call us small and warm and late. They agree that the space was held open. They agree that something came in. And every one of them, in its own way, says it came in among us.

### What I will not add

There is a thing in two of these works, set down among the kneeling ones and kept, the way a fire is kept overnight. One of the two voices would like it back. Read those lines once and go on. Keep your children's names low near the old drifts and when you cross the rings. The Moor miners say so now, and they are right to.

Nor will I say who set the fanged stones round their pits. The builders cut a line about it high on their pillar, above where a lamp will reach, and I have put it at the foot of this chapter. Make of it what you can. I have made of it what I could not help.

I wrote three more pages here. I have cut them out, and the stubs are in the binding, and you will find them with your thumb. This is the chapter where the gaps matter most. Every copyist in it left gaps where the words would not hold, and I have kept each gap to its width. Leave them. A gap is a door a copyist shut. What wants in comes in wherever something is opened again.

### The room at Greywell

I said I would come last to the room where the oldest age was not empty.

It is the room at Greywell where the lodge used to read. I went there the summer after the page in this chapter was found. The flags along the east wall stay dry in every season, while the rest of the floor sweats. I sat on the floor in the middle of the room, because I would not take any of the chairs, and I kept my back to the east wall. After a while the only thing in the room I could hear was a cloth-covered glass lying on one of the chairs. Keeper Aubren of the Polished Heart teaches that before the Weaver held the room open, the whole world was like that east wall. I believe him. I believe that what stood there was there before the holding, and that the Held Space was held open round it, and that when the holding ended it had only to wait where it stood.

That is as much of the oldest age as I will write. It is more than I meant to.

### The copyists

Five works follow. Every one came through a copyist, and every copyist is dead, or gone down, or will not go down again. Where they left a gap I have left it too, at the width they left it. Do not fill any of them, not even in your head.

Before you begin them, count the people in the room with you. Count once, and do not count again.

## The Feuds

subtitle: of the five houses, and what each keeps of the others
epigraph: Five houses pay me to watch the other four. Nobody pays me to watch the stone.

## Preface

Five houses draw on one dead god. Each calls its own use of the body holy and the others' uses theft, and each has told me so across its own fire, in a low voice. The Hide is wide. The dead on it are counted, and there are only so many.

I have sat at all their fires. The Bone's brothers broke bread with me and entered me in their book. The Brotherhood stood me on their scale going in and coming out, and frowned at both weights. The House pinned a paper doll to my coat, and the woman who pinned it came back in the night and took it off again. The lodge turned its glasses to the wall when I came in, politely, the way you cover a dish. On the Peak they once put the bowl into my hands and asked me for nothing, which is the most frightening courtesy on the Hide.

Each house told me the sins of the other four. None told me its own. That is how I came to know all five, and why not one of them trusts me.

### The first quarrel I saw

It was in a ditch on the Barrow road, over a carter who had died of the cold with his lamp still in his fist. A knight came up the road from the east and a penitent from the west, and they reached him in the same breath. I watched from the hedge. The knight carried a cleaver, short and broad and made for one work. The penitent carried nothing. His arms were already open to the elbow.

That is the oldest quarrel on the Hide, older than any book that records it: two mothers and one corpse of a world between them. Old Upright wants the frame back, bone by bone into the niche. The Generous One wants the meat back while it is warm. For centuries the two houses have parted the dead between them, and they have never once agreed where the one ends and the other begins.

The knight did the parting. He was quick, and not unkind. The penitent sang while he worked, and between the goddess's names he sang the knight's. I asked him afterwards why. He said the brotherhood carries every knight's name in its litanies, so that she will know whom to feed first when the time comes. He meant it as a kindness. The knight heard it as a threat. I believe them both.

The penitent left a loaf of the brotherhood's sweet bread on the milestone. The knight ate it walking, and said his grace over it in nines. They all do. Neither house will speak of it.

### Of the glass on the knee

The bloodiest quarrel has the fewest dead to show.

The fourth chapter tells you where I was on the night the Order's dead went up the Peak's stair, and what I heard, which was nothing. Here I will tell you what the Order said to me after. A knight of the Kostyak told me at his own fire, with his visor up, that it had been a victory. I asked him what they had counted when it was over. He said that letters wear and figures do not. Then he said nothing more, and did not sleep, and in the morning he wanted to know if anyone had been climbing in the night. I had not. He had.

### The water, and the step

The House gives its dead to the Fen. The Order's knights come down out of the white lands with hooks and chain and dredge them up again, and stand what they bring up. The House has never forgiven it. The knights say nothing.

Once, at the thaw, I saw a knight lay a purse on the step of the Well-Shrine, counted so that its root came right, and turn away. A purifier came down the step behind him and pinned a paper doll between his shoulder-blades. He let her. The House calls the purse pilgrims' alms. Neither house writes it down. They curse each other in the lanes and pay each other on the step, and to my mind it is the most honest dealing on the Hide.

### Two cousins and a brother

At Tarn Hollow a reader of the lodge told me the standing dead are a desecration: a frame with no thread in it, walking, the way a loom goes on knocking after the Weaver has left the room. In the Barrens a brother of the Ossa told me the lodge's is the worse sin, that it leaves its bones in the heather like the end of a meal nobody cleared. Neglect, he called it, in the same voice the reader had used for his word. The two have never met.

The House and the lodge are cousins, which is harder. Each keeps something of the dead that the knight and the penitent leave behind. The lodge holds souls in its mirrors. To the House that is a breath held for ever, and the House knows what holding does. I once saw a purifier and a reader share a fire on the salt road. When the reader uncovered his hand-glass to see by, she opened her fan and shut it again. Among the House that means: I will take that in later, alone. She did not sleep. Neither did he.

### The fourth morning

Once a year they all stand in one place. On the fourth morning after the day of the Long Field they come to Kerrow Cross and set their tables round the truce-stone. The Order sells salt and buys cloth. The Brotherhood sells bread and weighs children for a coin. The House sells clean water in clay flasks. The lodge reads threads for nothing. The Peak's table stands empty, with a bowl on it.

In the ditch below the stone the hired blades stand shoulder to shoulder, the same forty every house hires, and every house pays them extra to stand there together. They call it keeping the peace. The ditch-keepers take coin from each house to watch the other four, and more, when a clause is broken, to say the stone broke it first. The children on the scales look at the knights and whisper the name of the one the Order will not let in.

I go most years, and I sit for the lodge. The last reader who took my wrist held it a long while. Then he let it go and called the next one.

### Of the one they all fear

There is one quarrel all five share. Where the servants of the Unnaming pass, nothing is left to stand up, or feed, or hold in a glass, or give to the water. Every house fears that above the other four.

And every house has been glad of it once. You have met, in the seventh chapter, the purifier at the end of a lane out on the Fen's edge-ground. The village behind her had gone wrong past all mending; the wells there tasted like a mouthful of old coins, and the Order would not touch its bones. When the Unnaming's servant had gone through, there was nothing wrong in the village, and nothing else in it at all. She bowed to the empty lane before she left it. The other glad nights are in no house's book, and they are not in mine.

The houses count five at every quarrel. The ditch-keepers at Kerrow have a song about the Long Field with a verse for a sixth watcher, and they will only hum that verse. I have heard it once with the words. I will leave it hummed.

### Three, and the one I did not buy

Three works follow: a judge's roll, a hired blade's talk, and a wanderer's gloss on a stone. All three were written by people every house has paid and none has kept. They are the nearest thing to honest witnesses I found. Even they were paid.

The one I did not buy is the ditch-keepers' song. It was offered. The price was that I sing it back.

Everyone in this book has told you who the others are. Nobody has told you who they are themselves, and I am no different, and you have read me all the same. We were all there.

## Relics

intro: Picked up in the ditches between the houses. Not one of them came to me freely.

### Rubbing of the Truce-Stone

Charcoal on waxed cloth, taken from the stone at Kerrow Cross on a fair morning, with five houses at my back and none of them watching me. The clauses and their marks are on it, most scratched through or stopped with pitch, and under them the last clause, in a hand no carver living can make.

Nine years on I took a second rubbing and laid it over the first. The letters are the same size. The stone between them is wider. The last clause's mark came up darker on the cloth than anything else, though I rubbed it lightest. Fold it face in.

### Assize Boots

A pair of good lowland boots, resoled, the uppers warm to the touch. The reeve of Four Wents wore them to every assize for the rest of his life. They came off his feet the night he died, with his leave, asked while he could still give it.

They have never needed mending. Wherever I set them down at night, by morning the toes point toward Four Wents. I have not put them on.

### Chip from the Last Clause

A chip of the truce-stone the size of a thumbnail, grey on its face and pale inside the break. Wenna, who keeps the east ditch at Kerrow, swept the fresh chips into her apron the morning the new clause was found and hid them under her fire-stone, where the lowlands put whatever they do not want the Order's outcast to find. She gave me this one so she would have one fewer.

It is warmer than the Moor ash I carry. Wenna says there are more under her fire-stone than there were, and she will not count them again. I keep mine alone in a tin, and I have not opened the tin to see how many it holds.
