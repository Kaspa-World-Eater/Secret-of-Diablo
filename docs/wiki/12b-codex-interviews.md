## Interviews: I. The Reliquary

### Aumery the Copyist, of the table by the Ashwake milestones

*Asked at his own table, in the last good hour before the lantern gutters, with both copies of the niche-tally lying closed between us.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Aumery. The pilgrims call me the Copyist, as if there were one. There are four of us on this road and I am the slowest. The children at the milestones call me Old Ink, and I answer to that one quickest, because it asks the least of me. The brothers of the Pale Order have a number for me. They will not say it. A brother once waited on his tally at this table and tapped the edge with his nail while he waited: eleven taps, a rest, then two. He saw me counting and stopped.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
A stone room built against the ninth milestone, so the milestone is my back wall, and warm in the mornings from the ash under it. North light through oiled paper. It smells of lamp-ash, gall, wet leaves and onions. Every copyist eats onions; they keep the eyes wet. Barley bread, onion broth, and on feast-days a cap of Wood fungus fried in a spoon of god-fat. I sleep on reeds under the table so the leaves are over me. Next door, a stone's throw off, the lantern-stones and the tender's hut. At night I hear her telling the lanterns the weather. Some nights I hear a page turn in the room. I keep no loose pages.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
It depends on the season. In winter I hold with Idrenne: the Hush takes us in order, smallest first, and my death will be one more small name gone. In summer I hold with Mahault: we are where the god kept its name, and when one of us dies a little of the name goes down into the ash, which is why the ash is warm. Hesk would say my bones go into the Count, and that is enough. I would like to be a number. A number keeps. I have written my name on the flyleaf of every book I own, and on four of them it has gone.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
First grey, I grind ash for the day's ink. Copy until noon. Eat. Write letters for pilgrims until the lantern gutters; most of them are to people who are dead, and I write them the same as the rest. My holiest day is the Clean Leaf, at the year's turn. The copyists of the Ashwake road lay blank leaves along the milestones at dusk, weighted with stones, and at first grey we go out together and read what is on them. It is nearly always nothing. Once, in my twentieth year, a comma. We argued about it for a year.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
The leaf between the two boards. You may look at the boards. I fear waking one morning and reading my own treatise and understanding less of it than I did the night before, and not knowing whether the fault is in me or in the leaves. I will not talk about the Beggar with the black lantern. Ask me about him and I will tell you about ink. There is a great deal to tell about ink.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The Pale Order are my best custom. They pay in marrow-ink and quarrel with every sum. A monk of the Gilded Peak once brought a leaf and asked me to write nothing on it, carefully, and paid. The House of Eight Million forbid me to name my inkwell's god; I have not tried again. The House of the Polished Heart buy my spoiled copies, where a line has doubled itself; they say a doubled line is a thread showing. The Precious Wound write in their own blood, faster than I do. As to the waystones: pilgrims come up out of the Ashwake ring and ask me for paper before they ask for water. They want what they remember set down while it is theirs. I keep a copy. No two of the copies agree. Each one agrees with itself.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
Ink on a letter to a dead man takes longer to dry. I have timed it against the lantern for twenty years, and it is always longer, by about the space of a short prayer. The pilgrims think I blow on the page from kindness.

**You said there is a pale place in the paper the size of your palm, where the candle shows through. Have you ever held that leaf up to the candle?**
Once. In the thirty-second year, at noon, which I thought was safe. The pale place is thin enough to read through. I laid it over the first page of this treatise to see my own hand through it, and through the pale place the line read differently. A line I never wrote, in my hand, a little older. I read four words and put the leaf back between the boards. I will not tell you the four words. I have been careful never to write them since, in case that is how it happens.

### The grandmother of the fire on the Cheek

*Asked at a pilgrims' camp on the Ashen Moor with the fire banked and three children asleep against her; she answered in a whisper and kept one hand flat on the ground throughout.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Nan, to the little ones. The camp calls me Old Belt, because when we move I walk at the front and the whole line holds on behind me, belt to belt, like the first ones did. My name is behind my teeth, where my mother put it. She said it aloud at the hearth once, when I took bread, and I have not heard it since. I will know it when I hear it. That is the point of it.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
On the Cheek. We move every nine nights and never pitch twice on ash we have scorched. Tents of grass-cloth, pegged low. It smells of wet ash, smoke and barley, and in the morning of the fat. We bake ash-bread under the fire bed, eat root mash, and a broth of pale caps from the Wood's edge. On hard days the pickers bring a pat of fat from where the Hide shows through, and we call it the good fat and thank nobody. We sleep on the ground, hands flat. At night you hear the Gasps sighing out past the Kneeler, and the Sighing Lantern, very far off, breathing. Sometimes somebody in the camp hums in their sleep, and I get up and find them.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
The god died and we walked out of its mouth. When you die you go down with the breath, and the breath goes on out past the Ridge, toward the dark, and it carries you like a leaf on a ford. That is all, and it is enough. Or you stay in a lantern, if you are fool enough to look back. I have buried two husbands and a daughter and I did not look back at any of them, and that is the kindest thing I know how to do. Some say you go back in. Some say that. I do not say it.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Count heads at first grey. Gather ash-crust for the fire. Mend belts, because a belt that breaks on the road is the worst luck there is. Count heads again at dark. The holiest night is Kneel-Night, once a year, when the ash is wettest. The whole camp kneels round the banked fire from dark to grey, with no singing, and nobody may stand. At first grey we all stand at once, and it hurts, and we take hold of each other's belts and walk one slow ring round the fire. The children love it until the knees start.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
Singing on the Moor at night. I have told you. And a child who wakes knowing a tune I did not teach it. I will not talk about what they ate, inside. I told you already: the singing filled them. That is the right way to tell it. If you have heard some other way, you heard it from somebody who tells it wrong, and I will not have it said at my fire.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The Pale Order would have my knees off me and number them. The red folk of the Wound can keep their Maiden. The bowl monks never ate at a fire of mine, and I never asked them. The House of Eight Million are the only ones I like; they bless a new fire and ask its pardon before they bank it, and that is manners. The mirror people I have no use for; there is no glass in my tent. The waystones are teeth, and we walk round them. My second husband went in at the ring by the Kneeler and came up at Ashwake the same evening. I made him say his name three times at the flap before I let him in. He said it right. He said it right every time after, too. Too quick.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
Tie your belt with the knot at the back, so whoever walks behind you has something to hold. Even alone. Even asleep. Everyone on the Cheek does it, and we do not know we do it until someone from the Barrens laughs at us.

**When you told the story, you hummed three notes and stopped where a fourth should have been. What is the fourth?**
You heard that, did you. There is one. My grandmother sang it once, the night she died, lying by the fire, and the fire lay down flat and every child in the camp sat up at once with its eyes open. We pulled the tents and moved before grey, and left her. The three go down, down, down. The fourth goes up. Like a question. That is all I will give you. I hear pilgrims whistle the three going down on the road sometimes, when they are frightened. None of them ever finishes it. Good.

### The preacher at the Dry Ford

*Asked at the Dry Ford on the third dusk, before he began to preach; the carter who kept his sermons sat near enough to hear, and wrote none of this down.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
I have three names and I will give you none of them; they are spoken for. The carters call me the Ford-Man. The children of the Turned Mound call me Quiet Uncle and run. A porter at the Gilded Peak called me too loud, and that is the truest name I was ever given, and the only one I would keep if I could keep one.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
Here. The Dry Ford on the Barrows road. A riverbed with no river, stones worn smooth by water nobody alive has seen. The old carters say the river's small god rode off on the breath one spring and took the water with it. I sleep in the lee of the ford-stone on a folded sack. I eat what is given: barley cake, a bowl from the carters' root pot, once an onion. At night the Barrows are close. You hear the lids settle, and the carts, and then nothing. A ford should be loud. This one is the quietest place I have found on the Hide. That is why I came.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
The god was eased, the way you ease a man who cannot let go. When you die, the breath takes you down and out, and you go on dying slowly with everything else, a little in the ash, a little in the lamp, a little in the brothers' niches. That is what they call death. It is a long leaving. The Hush lets a thing finish. If it comes for me, I will finish. I pray for that. Quietly.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
I walk the road to the next hamlet and beg bread. I sleep in the heat of the day. I come back to the ford by dusk and speak. That is all. I keep no feast. Every dusk is holy, because it is the hour the lanterns lean, and something at the latch of every wound stands a little closer. Once a year the carters keep the Drying, when they throw a cup of water in the empty ford for the river's god. I throw nothing. I stand in the ford where the water went and listen to where it went.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
Being called for after it is done. To give a name away and then hear it again in a mouth I cannot see, being kind to me. That frightens me more than the monk and the bowl. I will not talk about the woman who gave me my third name. Ask me about her and I will preach at you instead, and you will not like the sermon.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The Peak shut the gate on me and I love them still, the way you love a house that would not keep you. The Pale Order are sweepers of leftovers. The Precious Wound feed a wound that ought to close. The House of Eight Million beg pardon of stones and mean it, which I respect. The Polished Heart catch threads in glass, and glass is only what is left when a thing is gone. And the waystones. You step into the pit and come apart, and you are put back. Put back. Something under the ash wants you whole. Think on it. Nothing kind has ever wanted you whole.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
A name you have given away leaves a warm place at the back of the mouth, about the size of a barley grain. It lasts three days. People who have done it keep touching the place with their tongue, and do not know why, and you can see them do it at every ford and every fire.

**You said a name you gave away was spoken back to you, gently, in a dream. Whose voice was it?**
My own. Younger. Before the Peak. No. My mother's. I have told it both ways, at different fords, and meant it both times. By the end of the dream it was the same voice, and it was very patient, and it said the name the way you would say it to someone you were putting down to sleep. I woke with the warm place in my mouth. Three days. Then it was gone, and I was sorry.

### The reader of the sky at the Fallen Watchtower

*Asked on the roof of the Fallen Watchtower at last grey, beside the pole and its red thread, while he lit the first marked candle of the night from the stub of the last.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
My master called me Boy for thirty-one years. Then he died, and nobody has called me anything since, not properly. The Ashwake folk who bring my sack call me the Tower. The carters call me Candle-Ends, because I buy their ends by the bag. I have a name. I wrote it under my master's on the lintel, with the count, and I read it there when I want it.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
This tower. One room at the top, the stair winding under it with my charcoal on the wall. It smells of tallow and cold stone. The wind never changes, so the smell never changes. Every tenth day someone from Ashwake climbs up with a sack: barley, dried caps, a crock of god-fat, the candle-ends. I make gruel in one pot and eat it from the pot. I sleep on my master's pallet on the landing, where the stair turns. At night I hear the thread ticking against the pole. On the stillest nights, perhaps twice a year, from the east, a long sound like a great door being tried and not opened.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
I write what I see, and I have never seen the god. I have seen its day kept to the width of a thread for forty years, and I know a corpse keeps no hours. So the god is still somewhat alive, or something else keeps the day. I have no third guess. When I die I will go out on the Long Exhale, past the Sighing Ridge, toward the dark. I have watched everything light go that way. Nothing has come back. I think that is where it all piles up, out past the arches. Somewhere there is a great deal of ash.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
First grey: read the thread, light the first candle. Noon: note the white over the Ossa. Dusk: last candle. Night: the nail-heads, which I count once and do not recount. My holiest day is the one the thread lay down, in my eleventh year. Each year on that day I read nothing. I sit with my hand on the pole from grey to grey, and count heartbeats, and wait. My master kept no such day. He never saw it lie down. I have written the day on the lintel and not the date, since there are no seasons to date it by.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
The thread lying down again. That first of everything. After that, that it will lie down while I am asleep, and get up again, and I will never know it went. The candles would tell me nothing. I will not talk about the arches past twenty-four. You asked. I heard you. Ask me something else.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The Pale Order is whitening the sky over the Ossa and calls it giving back. The brothers say the stars are the god's bones, the Unfallen, and when one falls they go and fetch it. Some fall; I have seen the long white streak over the Barrens. Mine go out without falling, and the dark is smoother where they were. I told a brother so, and he wrote it down, and asked me not to tell the next brother. The Peak monk gave my master the lens. As to the waystones: the nine cold flashes in forty years. I found one ring by walking. I think the flash is someone going in. I think the sound after, which never comes, is someone coming up.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
A candle burns faster on the roof than in the room below, and by the same amount every day of the year, to the width of a fingernail. That is how I know the day is the same. A thousand learned men have told me days differ, and every one of them has been down the tower before noon.

**Your master had a lens from a monk of the Peak. What did the monk ask for it?**
Nothing, my master said. Only a promise: never to look west through it, over the Ossa. My master kept that promise thirty-one years. I have not kept it. I looked once, at noon, in the year the sky went whiter than the bone. The white over the Ossa has a shape at the top, where it thins. A long rise, and then a round, like a knuckle, or a knee. I put the lens back in its cloth and have not taken it out. I should like the next reader to keep the promise. I should like him to know why.

### The midwife of the Ashen Moor

*Asked at her door while she laid the salt line, and afterward inside by the cradle-lantern, where she would answer only while her hands were busy.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Midwife. The camp folk call me Salt-Wife. The children call me Mother Grain, because I carry barley in one apron pocket and salt in the other, and they always go for the wrong pocket and spit. I have a name. It is behind my teeth, same as I taught every mother on this Moor to keep them. You will not get it. You have a copyist's ears.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
A low house of ash-brick at the end of the track, half a morning out from Maren's camp, the last house before the hollow the pickers will not cross. It smells of salt, bread and old blood; you stop smelling the blood. Bread always. A birth-house keeps a loaf rising day and night, because every woman wants bread after, and so does everyone who helped. I sleep on the bench by the door so I hear the knocking. The ash-pickers live past the ridge. At night the salt ticks. Salt talks when something tries it. You learn which ticks to get up for.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
The god is dead and every scrap of it wants our young. That is all I need to believe to do my work. When I die I want to go down with the breath, no fuss, no lantern. I have salted my own bed so nothing wears me after. My name I will take down with me unsaid. What has never been said can never be unsaid. That is my whole faith, and it has served.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Salt the step, salt the sill. Bake. Walk the track to whoever is near their time and put a hand on them. Trade with the carters for salt; it comes up from the Ash Shore and it is dear, and I would sell the roof first. My holiest day is always someone else's: the Hearth-Naming, when a weaned child takes bread and its mother says the true name aloud, once, with the lantern lit. I stand at the back with the salt and listen for the name to land. You can hear it land. Mostly.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
The open-handed ones. I told your copyist of one, and that is the last I will say of it, so do not come round it sideways. I fear a wet line across a floor. I fear bread I did not bake. I will not talk about where the knuckle-bone child is now. The brothers took him up the mountain at seven years, with his mother's leave. She has his number on a strip of bone. She holds it at night.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The Precious Wound sent two penitents asking after the boy who does not bleed. I salted the step while they talked. The Pale Order wrote a child down by a number bigger than I can count, and I am glad I cannot. The House of Eight Million bless my salt, and say salt has no small god of its own, which is why the pieces will not cross it. The mirror people asked to hold a glass over a newborn once. The glass went in the trough. As for the waystones, no woman heavy with child goes through a ring. One did. She came up light at the other stone. The child came up six days after, at a third ring, well and hungry. No woman would nurse it. I did what I could with barley water.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
A newborn's first breath out is always longer than its first breath in. Always. I have counted every one for thirty years. We come in owing, and every one of us starts paying on the first breath. I have never told a mother. She has enough to carry.

**The boy the Wet Nurse knelt beside, whose cuts close without a drop. Does he know?**
He knows. He cuts reeds at the Fen now, and keeps his cuts to himself. Every harvest he walks up the track with a loaf he baked, and it is always too good, and he asks me if I remember his name. I say it for him. He listens as if it belonged to someone else and he were being told it for a message. Then he thanks me, very kindly, and goes. His mother does not ask him to stay the night. I would not either.

### The soul in the pilgrim's lantern, by way of the copyist

*Asked through the glass at the Ashwake lantern-stones with the pilgrim asleep; the copyist put each question to the lantern with his ear against it and set down what came back, and where nothing came, left the gap.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Half a name. *[The copyist asked for the half that was left. The flame drew in.]* The second sound. I keep it close. The tenders call us by our stone, and I have no stone now; I left mine to walk. He calls me nothing. He has never turned round. *[Then, quickly:]* You asked me that already. *[I had not.]*

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
Glass. Four sides and a lid, and the cold hook. The light is me, so I have never seen it. It smells of him: the cut on his thumb, his sweat, the rain he cannot remember. At the stones there were others close by. The nineteenth scratches at night, a small sound, always the same four marks. Now there is only him, a little ahead and to one side. What I eat. *[A long quiet.]* I told you what I eat.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
I did that. It was short. *[I asked again, of the god.]* Believe. I heard it. I was going down, the way water goes, and down there it was warm and very full, and it was singing, or breathing in the shape of singing, and every voice was a little behind the others, like people who have forgotten the words. *[Here a word, perhaps "room", perhaps "roof".]* Then the boot on the stones. I looked.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
He walks. I lean. He sleeps. I burn a little. *[It did not answer the second part. I asked it twice more. The third time:]* When he says Ettle in his sleep, and I have not burned her yet. Then I am very bright. The tender says that is the warmth of a feast-day. She is wrong about the feast. She is right about the warmth.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
The pits. The one at the bottom, breathing slower than him. And the day there is nothing of him left to burn, when I carry him home and nothing gathers up out of the ash, or something does. *[I asked about the first sound of its name. The flame went to a bead and stayed so for the length of a candle-mark. I did not ask again.]*

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
*[It asked what an order was. I said: a brotherhood, a house of people who keep the same rites. It said: a house with a low door? I let it be.]* The bone brothers come past the stones and count us. We lean away from them, all of us together, the way you turn your back to a cold door. The pits I have told you. They are warm. Everything warm wants him.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
Glass keeps breath. Every mouth that ever fogged it. The tender's cloth takes off the dust and leaves the breath, and at night I can read them all, layer on layer, in and out. Yours is on me now. *[It is. I wiped it before I wrote this, and the fog came back in the same place.]*

**You have carried him home eleven times. How many more times can you carry him?**
*[A long quiet, the flame low and steady, the way the tender says they burn at the Counting Wall.]* As many times as there is him. After that the other way. *[I asked what the other way was.]* Then he carries me. Like the grey woman in the Sighing one. Breathing together. *[A word here twice, which may have been "lighter", or "later".]* He whistles now, in his sleep. Three going down. *[It was the pilgrim whistling. I am nearly sure.]*

### The tender of the Ashwake round

*Asked walking the round from the Ashwake stones out to the Widow's Stumps and home by the milestones, while she wiped each lantern top to bottom and told it the weather.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
The tender. The camp calls me Tin, for the cloth-tin. My teacher called me Wick, when I was small enough to sit on her stool. The lanterns do not call me anything. I would hear it if they did; I listen for it. The pilgrims mostly call me nothing at all and step round me, which is proper. A tender is part of the stones.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
The hut at the Ashwake stones. One room: the tithe-scale on the table, the tin on the shelf, a chair that faces the window so I can see the round from it. The lanterns smell of a room someone has just gone out of. My hut smells of soot and wet cloth. I eat barley porridge with caps, and the pilgrims leave bread on the first stone as a toll, and I take it and say thank you to the stone. I sleep in the chair. At night I hear the nineteenth scratching from the inside, and far out past the Stumps, when the wind is low, the Sighing Lantern.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
My teacher said the Last Breath goes down and out, and everyone goes with it, and that is proper. I believe the lanterns, because they are what I can see and wipe. When I die I want to go down. Every morning forty-one someones ask me to stay, in their way, and I do not want to be wiped. I do not want to be heavier than I was. Though the sixth would lean for me. I know she would.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
The round, every morning. Forty-one lanterns, top to bottom, and the weather for each. Afternoons I mend the tin, sweep the stones, sit up with whatever came home in the night. My holiest is the Feast of Wicks, when the pilgrims crowd the stones and tie a strip of cloth on every lantern's hook, one strip for each soul they have lost on the road. By night the hooks are hung like washing. My teacher said the flames run warmer. I hold my palm to them. Same warmth. I tell no one.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
A lantern that comes home with less than it went out with. As though something drank through the glass. I sit up with those all night and I talk to them the whole time, so they have something to hold on to. I will not talk about the sum of the numbers in the tin lid. I stopped adding them. You add them, if you like. Do not tell me what they come to.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The House of Eight Million bow to the lanterns though they say there is no small god in them; they bow to be safe. The Pale Order walk past and count the flames, and the flames lean from them, all together. One brother asked to copy my tin lid, and did, and went pale, and paid me for it. The mirror people say they could see who is in each lantern with a glass. I will not let them. A Wound penitent sometimes leaves a drop of blood on the glass as a courtesy. I wipe it. The waystones: every flame shrinks to a bead near a ring and leans away. That is all I need to know about them.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
A lantern goes heavy in anyone's hand but its own traveller's. Honestly heavy, like carrying a sleeping child who does not want to be carried. I have lifted every one on this round, and every one of them has been heavy to me, even the ones with nobody to follow.

**You wrote that naming them is why you cannot leave. How does naming keep you here?**
Old Hesk leans at me when I walk down the milestones toward the camp. Leans hard, the way they lean for a traveller. Little Brand too, and my teacher. I get as far as the ninth milestone, by the Copyist's wall, and there is a pull at my back, like a coat caught on a nail. I wrote that I have never been chosen. That was a lie in a letter. I was chosen by three of them. Chosen to stay. When they lean I go back, and wipe them again, and tell them the weather again, and they are very content.

### The surveyor of Maren's camp

*Asked in the tithe-barn at Maren's camp, with the sheets of the chart laid edge to edge across the floor and the second lamp lit, as he had it that winter.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Surveyor. The pilgrims call me Cord, for the cord of a hundred knots, which is the only thing about me they notice. Maren calls me the Liar with the Ink, and means it kindly, mostly. A brother of the Pale Order asked my name in the Valley of Standing Ribs and wrote a number in its place. I have been trying to work out the number from the look of his hand. Nine figures, I think. Their root is nine. Of course it is.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
The loft of this barn. It smells of grain dust, gall-ink, and the wax on the cord. I eat what Maren sells, which is barley bread and root stew thick enough to stand a spoon in, and a cup of fen-water in the evening, which leaves your mouth dry. I sleep on sacks of tithe-grain. They give a little under you in the night, like everything here. Brannoc's hammer is my morning. Sister Ysolde's sick cough through the wall. At night the ash settles round the barn with one long sound, out, and I lie and count it.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
I believe the ground. The ground is a face, and the face breathes with the cold and the warm. More than that I will not swear to; I have been wrong about ground before, in ink. When I die, lay me somewhere that stays put. There is nowhere. The Fen, then. The shore goes back and back and always comes home in a night. I should like to be in something that comes home.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Pace against the cord at first grey, to be sure of my stride. Walk a road. Ink it by lamp. Correct yesterday's ink, most days. My holy day is Pacing Day, at the thaw. I pace from Maren's fire to the Sighing Lantern with the whole camp watching and the children counting aloud with me. When it comes to one thousand one hundred and forty, they cheer, and I thank whoever is listening, and we all eat. It has come to 1,140 eleven years running at the thaw.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
The eyes. Drawing them open. I will not talk about the last leaves of the book. Somebody cut them out. It may have been me. I have said as much as I am going to say, and I have said it twice now, which is once more than I meant to.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The Pale Order's count is always higher than mine, and they say mine will close in time. The Peak's monks walk out on roads I have not drawn and come back on roads that are not there; I asked one to show me, and he walked me in a ring round the barn and bowed. The House of Eight Million asked me to mark every small god's stone on the chart. I began. There was no room for the Moor. The waystones are folds. Fold the charts the way the pilgrims tell it and the folds all cross near the Jaw, at a pit with ash on it. The ash moves. I have set my pin at the place and not pressed it in.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
A road walked by many pilgrims is shorter than the same road walked by one. By a knot or two a mile, and more on feast-days. I think the ground makes way for a crowd, the way a room does when people come in singing.

**Somebody cut out the last leaves of your chart-book. What did they show?**
*[He was a long time answering.]* The place under the folds. I drew it on the last leaves because it would not go on the others; the ink would not sit on the old sheets there. I drew it the way the needle pointed, which is down. In the morning they were gone, cut clean at the binding. The knife was mine. It was back in its sheath the right way round, which I never manage. Whoever did it was careful of me. I have decided to be grateful.

### The gatherer of the children's rhymes

*Asked at the Ashwake waystone, where she sat on a fallen tooth of the ring with her book on her knees, on the evening before the book was found there without her.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
The children call me the Lady Who Asks, and then tell me nothing for three days. Their mothers call me the Rhyme-Wife. My name is Welt. Short, easy to keep. I chose it for that, on the road, the year I began. What my mother called me I gave away at a fire on the Pilgrim Road, in the Bowl game, playing with the children to learn it. They forgot it very well. So did I.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
The road, and whatever corner the children sleep in. In the barrow villages that is by the hearth on reed mats, three and four to a mat, and they whisper the counting-out in their sleep, all the way to nine. In the stilt-towns over Shog-Mire I sleep on planks that move with the mud, on the beat. I eat what the house eats: ash-cake on the Moor, barley everywhere, and over the Mire a grey cap pressed into cakes that tastes of the smell of rain. At night I hear children. Always. Someone's children, somewhere near.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
I believe the children. They say when you die you are laid: you stand still with your arms crossed till the game is over. I asked when the game is over. They said when the Mother has all her bones. I asked what then. Then everybody gets up and goes home, they said. I asked where home was. Every child I have asked has pointed down. Every one, on every road, the same way. I have not asked the brothers what they make of that.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Walk. Sit near where the children play and wait to be let in. Write, with the tally-marks down the margin so I know how many times I heard each verse. My holiest day is Last Stubble, after the harvest on the Barrens road, when the children lie on their backs in the stubble and sing the Unfallen, pointing. I lie down with them. The fathers stand at the field's edge with their sacks. Nobody says why they have brought the sacks. Everyone knows.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
The last line of Needle-Brother. Five words. I have seen it on their lips a hundred times and I have been careful never to read it there. I will not talk about the seventh rhyme. I count six. The brother at this ring counted seven and would not show me where, and I have looked, and there are six. If you find the seventh, keep it.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The Pale Order is in half the rhymes, as Grandfather Dust. The Wound is the Tall Red Hat. The Heart is Needle-Brother, and the children are more frightened of him than of anyone, and I have never met a mirror person who was not gentle. I think the Bowl came off the Peak. The waystones: *in at the tooth and out at the tooth.* The children know the rest better than I do. I have watched the ring game in eleven villages. The child in the middle always says her name wrong on purpose, the first time, to be let play someone else.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
Children skipping on the Moor never let the rope touch the ash. If it does, the game stops, and the rope is carried to the edge of the village and shaken out, and nobody skips again until dark. They do it gravely, the way the brothers sweep. Then they laugh, after.

**You never found the first child. Where did you stop looking?**
At Low Harrow, in the gap in the ring. I think the first child is whoever sits in the gap. The one nobody can name, that every child learned the rhyme from, and forgot. I think there is one in every ring, and the rhymes go from ring to ring the way pilgrims do. *[She looked at the pit a long time.]* In the morning I will go in at the tooth, and see which ring I come up at, and whether the children there already know me. Keep the book for me until I am back. If I do not come for it, give it to a brother. They will count it.

### The woodcutter of Coldhearth

*Asked on the step of his house at Coldhearth, at evening, after he had called once and nobody came; the second cup sat on the step between us, filled.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Garrow. Garrow the Cutter, at home. There's thirteen doors and I'm what's left behind them, so at the camp they've started calling me Coldhearth, like the place. I don't mind it. Somebody ought to be called it. I'm a plain man. I cut the pale wood. I said that to your other copyist, and I'll say it every time, because it's the one thing I'm sure of.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
Coldhearth. The last house on the lane, at the edge of the Hollow Wood. The pale trunks smell sweet where they're opened, like the crust of a loaf, and weep clear. The chips stay warm all night in the basket. Fungus on the trunks in three colours; the grey is good in broth, the other two you leave. I eat root-meal bread and the broth, and I make two bowls, and eat the second one cold in the morning. I sleep on my pallet. The other one's made up. At night the trunks tick in the cold like a pot cooling. And the houses make no sound at all.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
The Wood's the god's veins, stood up. My father said so. When you die, the ground has you, or the brothers come and count you, and either way there's something to put down. That's what I believed. Now I think there's two dyings. The one where there's something left for the spade. And the one I was digging for. I'd like the first. Bury me with a spoon. Mine's the one with the notch.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Up at grey. Barrow to the Wood. Count the trunks with my hand flat, leave every tenth. Cut till the barrow's heavy. Home before the candles. Split kindling, small. My holiest day was always Spoon Day. In Coldhearth, when a child could first hold a spoon, you carved it one, and the whole lane came in and watched it eat. Every house had its Spoon Days. I still keep them. I can't keep them all. There's one I keep that I've no date for, and no spoon.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
Finding out whose coat it is. I've said. And that the brothers will come back with their strip of bone and their little knife, and cut the count, and this time the count comes out right. I won't talk about the thirteenth name. I would if I could. There's a place at the back of my tongue. You can ask. Nothing comes.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The brothers came up and went down fast and none of them looked back, and a man remembers that. The mirror folk at the camp say they can follow a thread. The House came once and asked pardon of the well; I've not drawn from it since. The red folk I've never seen and don't want to. There's no ring near Coldhearth. Men I know have gone into one and come up fine, at the other end of the Moor, a bit hungry. I'd step into one now. There's nothing left here that'd want me whole.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
Pale wood burns with no smoke. The fire makes a sound like somebody breathing through their nose, asleep. In every house in Coldhearth we'd sit up of an evening listening to it, and nobody ever said it out loud. I only say it now because there's nobody to mind.

**You split the kindling small, for small hands. What else do you do now that you didn't used to?**
I leave the lowest shelf clear. I cut the loaf in three and eat two. I walk slower on the lane, the far side of the ruts, like there's short legs beside me that can't keep up. I stop at the tenth trunk and wait longer than I need. I sing. I never sang. Something about a harvest, with a second verse I don't know. I stop where the second verse would be and wait. *[He looked at the cup.]* It's a long wait. I'm not in a hurry.

### Notes for the lore bible

### Places

- The Copyists' Mile: four copyists keep tables along the Ashwake milestones; Aumery's stone room is built against the ninth milestone, a stone's throw from the lantern-stones and the tender's hut.

- The Dry Ford on the Barrows road: a riverbed whose small god "rode off on the breath" one spring, taking the water. The quietest place on the Hide; the preacher's glass-smooth mark lies in it.

- The midwife's house: last ash-brick house before the hollow past the Broken Kneeler, half a morning out from Maren's camp. Salt is traded up from the Ash Shore, dear.

- Coldhearth, at the Hollow Wood's edge: thirteen doors, fourteen graves by the old stone, one well nobody draws from.

### Beliefs and rites

- The Clean Leaf (year's turn): copyists lay blank leaves on the milestones at dusk and read them at first grey. Once, a comma.

- Kneel-Night: camps on the Cheek kneel round a banked fire from dark to grey without singing, then stand as one and walk a ring holding belts.

- The Drying: carters throw a cup of water into the Dry Ford for its departed river god.

- The Feast of Wicks: pilgrims tie a cloth strip on a lantern's hook for each soul lost on the road; the hooks hang "like washing".

- The Hearth-Naming: a weaned child's hidden name is said aloud once at the hearth; the midwife "listens for it to land".

- Pacing Day (thaw): the surveyor paces Maren's fire to the Sighing Lantern with the camp counting aloud; 1,140 is cause to feast.

- Spoon Day (Coldhearth): a child's first spoon is carved and the lane watches it eat; the dead are buried with their spoon.

- Last Stubble (Barrens road): children lie in the harvest stubble singing the Unfallen while fathers wait at the edge with sacks.

- Children everywhere say the dead are "laid" until the Mother has her bones, then "everybody goes home", and all point down.

### Daily life (food, dress, work, sayings)

- "The good fat": fat cut where the Hide shows through; eaten on hard days, thanking nobody.

- Food on the Moor: ash-bread baked under the fire bed, root mash, pale-cap broth. The Hollow Wood's grey fungus is good in broth, the other two colours are left alone. Shog-Mire presses a grey cap into cakes that "taste of the smell of rain".

- On the Cheek, belts are knotted at the back so the one behind has something to hold; Barrens folk laugh at it.

- Moor children never let a skipping rope touch ash; a touched rope is shaken out at the village edge and the game ends.

- Sayings: "Somebody always sits up." "We come in owing" (a newborn's first breath out is longer than its first breath in). "Salt talks when something tries it."

### Things they disagree about

- The grandmother says the dead go down with the breath and out, and "some say you go back in"; the lantern-soul heard the down-place singing, warm and full; the sky-reader thinks it all piles up past the arches.

- The Pale Order says stars fall and are fetched; the sky-reader says his "nail-heads" go out without falling. A brother asked him not to tell the next brother.

- The tender says feast-day flames are no warmer; the lantern-soul says she is right about the warmth and wrong about the cause.

- The preacher says "nothing kind wants you whole" of the waystones; the woodcutter would step in now because nothing wants him whole.

### Threads worth pulling

- Aumery's pale place in the leaf shows through it a line he never wrote, in his own older hand (Copyist). His Pale Order number may be "eleven, then two".

- The fourth note of the congregation's tune goes up, "like a question". Frightened pilgrims whistle the three going down and never finish it (Grandmother; Lantern-soul).

- Through the Peak monk's lens, the white over the Ossa has a shape like a knuckle or a knee. The monk made his master promise never to look west (Sky-reader).

- A woman went through a waystone heavy with child and came up light; the child rose six days later at a third ring, and no one would nurse it (Midwife).

- A lantern's soul fears the day there is nothing left of its traveller, and he "carries me the other way", as the grey woman does in the Sighing Lantern (Lantern-soul).

- The surveyor's last leaves drew the place under the waystone folds, as the needle points, down. They were cut out with his own knife, sheathed more neatly than he ever manages (Cartographer).

- Maybe one "first child" sits in the gap of every ring, and the rhymes travel ring to ring the way pilgrims do. The collector stepped into the Ashwake pit the next morning (Rhymes).

- Garrow has started humming a harvest song and stops at the second verse, the same place as the lantern-soul's pilgrim. His coat-child may be on the road inside someone else's lantern (Woodcutter; Lantern-soul).

## Interviews: II. The Roads and the Stones

### The beggar at the Ninth Mile

*Spoken on the warm ash at the ninth Ashwake milestone, one copper to each question; the scribe brought a full purse this time.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Ninth-Mile. You know that already, you've paid for it. The brothers who ride by call me "the stone's man", as though the stone owned me. The children from Ashwake call me Uncle Cold-Coin, because I showed one of them the knight's coin once and she dropped it and cried. Before that I had a name and a trade. I think the trade was barrels. My hands know how to hold a stave. My tongue doesn't know what they were called.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
Here. This is the house. The stone is the wall and the ash is the bed and the sky is a roof with holes in it. The ash smells like a hearth the morning after, always. I eat what the pilgrims leave: grey bread, ash-root, a cake of white caps now and then, a mouthful of fat from the Kettlewick carriers. Rain comes sideways off the Sighing Ridge. At night I hear the knights' bones going by, and the Lantern at the crossroad ticking in its glass as it cools, and the ring below the Kneeler. That last one I try to not hear.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
The god's dead, and we're sitting on its face. That's the Moor. It's its cheek, they say, where it struck first. Warm because it's still going cold. When I die I want a lantern. I've spent forty years wiping one, and I'd like somebody to wipe me. The brothers say I ought to go up their mountain to be counted. I've been counted. A knight held a cord to my stone, and it's been getting taller since, so whatever he counted, it wasn't finished.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Wake. Wipe the Lantern. Sit. Hold out the left hand. Sleep. The holiest day is the Day of the Wiped Glass, first thaw, when every pilgrim on the road stops at every lantern and wipes it with their own sleeve. The whole road smells of wet cloth. I get more copper that day than the rest of the year, and more bread, and people tell me their names so I'll say them to the Lantern after they've gone. I do. I say them all. I say them in the order I got them, because it matters to someone.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
The monk. I've told that once and it cost me something, so no. And the ninety paces. I'll tell you this and then no more: last spring I counted them again, and it came to eighty-nine. I have not moved. I've sat on this ash forty years and there's the dent to prove it. Eighty-nine. I count every spring now. I'm afraid of the spring I get to nothing.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The knights pay cold. The red brothers from Villa Llaga pay in blood-pennies, little red clots, dried hard, that go soft in your palm, and I don't take them. The paper-folk from the Eight Million are kind and tie a strip to my stone every year and say it's for my safety. The mirror-lodge sent a man to show me my face in a polished plate. I told him I'd rather not know. The stones? They're a mouth. Everybody knows that, nobody says it. Anybody would, looking.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
Pilgrims who've come up out of a ring more than twice give short weight. I mean the copper. They'll count me five into my hand and there's four. They don't do it to cheat. They count five and believe five. I've stopped telling them. I just hold out the hand again, and they count another five, and there's four again, and they look at me very sorry and walk on. I'm owed a great deal of copper by pilgrims who can't count. Or by the stones.

**You said the Lantern leans your way in a wind. Do you know who is in it?**
Her name's on the glass, scratched small, down at the foot: "Aveth. She was kind about the road." That's all. I've wiped round it forty years. Some mornings I think I knew an Aveth. My wife, maybe, the one whose name I gave away. That would be a neat thing, wouldn't it, and I don't trust neat things. But the Lantern leans. And when I give her the pilgrims' names in the evening, she goes low at some of them, and I don't know why, and I don't give those names twice.

### The old walker of the rings

*Spoken at the lip of the Moor ring, where she sat with her feet over the edge and would not come to the hearth; she answered with her face turned to the pit.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Walkers call me Old Thirty. It was a count of my crossings once and it has fallen behind. I had a name from my mother. I spoke it aloud every morning for a long time, as you're told to, and one morning I spoke it and it was a word for a kind of spoon. So I stopped. Thirty will do. Thirty doesn't go anywhere.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
At the lips. Whichever ring I'm near. You sleep on the warm side of a plinth, curled up, with your back to the stain. It's the best bed on the Hide. The smell is ash and something under the ash, like a kettle that had broth in it last week. I eat hearth-bread from the camps when they give it and I carry a twist of salt. Neighbours: other walkers, now and then. We don't talk much. We look each other over. At night I hear it breathing, and the stones settling, a little noise, like old wood in a cold house.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
The god is down there somewhere, and it's big, and it's still breathing, whatever the brothers say about dead. When I die I'd like to go in one more time and not come up. Walkers say that happens to the lucky ones. They say you go on down with the pull and you're kept whole, all of you, every little thing it took, given back. I've heard a walker say it. I said it myself once, to a girl, to make her brave.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Walk. Find the threads. Follow them. Kneel at the lip and watch the crust. Step in or don't. Most days I don't. Most days I only sit. The holy day for walkers is Stepping Eve, the longest night, when all the walkers who can reach a ring go in together, one after another, so each one's alone and none of them is. We hold the lip in turns. The last one in has no one to hold the lip. That's an honour. I've been last four times.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
I'm afraid of a ring where the crust lies still. It means the stone isn't keeping the road. I've seen two. I stood at one a whole day, and nothing lifted, and the stones leaned out, away from me, the way you lean from a sick man. And I won't talk about the man who asked where we were. Don't ask me his name. I have it. It's one of the things I've kept, and I'm keeping it.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The knights won't cross. They go round by the long road with their dead behind them, weeks of it. They say a bone out of its place is a wound, and the rings take every bone out of its place and put it somewhere else. I think they're jealous. The Villa Llaga brothers love the rings, they come and stand at the lip smelling it, and I don't like them there. The waystones are the road's own hearths. You're warm there. What else should a pilgrim want?

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
When you come up, your teeth are clean. All of them. Every walker's teeth are white as the stones. You'll see an old walker grin and think she's rich. And some of us come up with our back teeth sharper than we went in, just a little, just at the tip. Nobody minds. Bread's easier.

**You said the stones lean nearer to look at you. What do you think they see?**
Thirty-four of me. Thirty-six. Every time I've gone down, a me went in, and a me came out, and they weren't the same weight. The stones have seen them all. I think they know me better than my mother did. My mother saw one of me. When I stand at the lip with my mouth open and the warm comes up, I think: it's glad. It's like going home and finding the door ajar. You'd go in, wouldn't you? You would. Everybody does, in the end. Close your mouth, you're letting the warm out.

### The measurer of the rings

*Taken down at the Barrow ring on a wet morning, between two soundings; she answered with the cord in her lap and counted the knots whenever she was not speaking.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Idra Vell. Road-measurer, licensed by the milestone-wardens of Ashwake to set the miles on the Pilgrim Road, which I did for nineteen years, until I found the miles did not need me. Pilgrims call me the Plumb. Children call me the Cord-Woman and run off. I am seventeen hands and three fingers tall. I was seventeen and four. I measure myself against a doorpost at the Ashwake inn every winter. The doorpost is the same. I have asked.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
On the road, and in the back room of the Ashwake inn when the road is mud. The room has a slant floor and I sleep on the high side on a pallet of heath-straw. The inn smells of wet ash and hot grain. The innwife makes a mash of ash-root and cap-meal with a dab of Kettlewick fat on it, and I eat it every night I am there, because it is always the same. Through the wall I hear drovers from the Heath who pull their carts themselves and snore like saws.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
I believe what the cord says, and the cord says nothing about the god. My father was a lay brother of the Pale Order. He taught me the knots and he taught me that a thing counted is a thing kept. So I suppose I believe that. When I die I want to be measured. Head to heel, by someone careful, and the number written on something that will not burn. After that they may do as they like.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Wake before light. Check the cord against the doorpost marks. Walk to a ring. Measure the stones, the stains, the pit, in that order, always that order. Write. Walk back. My holiest day is the day I finish a table and every column adds. That has happened three times. The first time I lit a candle. The second I bought the whole inn a round. The third time was the Moor ring, and when I checked it the next morning it did not add any more, and I put the candle out.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
I fear a short cord. You know I have one. I will not talk about the Moor ring's last number. It is in my book, the last page, and I have folded that page under so it will not touch the others. Some people fear the dark and some fear the dead. I fear a number that is right and should not be.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The Pale Order counts better than anyone and tells you nothing. The paper-folk of the Eight Million once asked me to measure a shrine so they could purify it by the width, and paid fairly. The mirror-lodge of the Polished Heart asked to see my tables and read them with a hand over one eye, and said I had written down a face. I had written down forty-three rings. The waystones are the only honest things on the road. They change, and they let you watch them change.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
Every stone has eight bands on the root side of the plinth, under the turf, that nobody sees. I dug at one, a hand's depth, where the ground was soft. Eight bands, close together, very old, tight as the rings on a bitten nail. At every ring I tried. Eight. Under those, the turf went warm and wet, and I stopped. So the bands above the ground are the new ones. That is what the digging told me. I have not written it in the tables.

**You said the threads meet at a place no map shows. Have you gone to look?**
I have gone toward it. The bearings meet somewhere south of the Burnt Heath and east of the Drowned Fen, where there is no road and the ground is flat and dark and warm. I walked half a day. The threads grew thick as fingers, then thick as wrists, all running the one way. Then I found I had been walking beside my own footprints for some time, fresh ones, going the other way, and they were a little longer than mine. I came back. I told myself it was to fetch more cord.

### Wenna, the child at the Fen stair

*Spoken at the ring by the Fen stair, some weeks after the Ashwake tender set her down; she sat at the lip on her own and said her father had gone for water, and nobody came while we talked.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Wenna. That's my name. Papa calls me Wenna. He used to call me Pip, and I told him, and he said Pip, that's a nice name, is that your sister? I haven't got a sister. The old lady walkers call me Little Warm, because I sleep curled up like them now. I like it. You can call me Wenna. Everybody should.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
We lived at Cinderholt, by the forge, where it smelled like hot nails and the bread was always burnt at one end. Now we live at the rings. This one smells like wet reeds and the ash smells like when you blow out a candle. We eat bread and it doesn't help. Papa gets pot-cakes from the Fen people, made of reed-seed, and those don't help either. At night I hear the ring breathe. It's nicer than Papa snoring. He doesn't snore now. He sleeps on his side with his eyes open a bit.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
Mama said the god was a big person who died and we live inside it, like in a house that somebody left. When you die you go in a lantern if you want, or you go down if you don't. I think down is where the somebody is. The glad one. I think when you die you just go down and it's glad and it counts you and you stay. I asked Papa and he said don't talk about that at the lip. We're always at the lip.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
We wake up and Papa counts my fingers, then his, then mine. Then we eat bread standing up. Then we watch the ring. Sometimes we go in. Sometimes we walk to the next one. My holiest day was Hearth-Night, at home, when everybody put their bread by the forge and the smith said words and it was all one big loaf in the morning, and you broke a piece for everybody you loved. I broke one for Mama. Papa ate it. I don't know what the holiest day is now. Maybe going in.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
I'm not scared of the dark any more. I'm scared of candles a bit. And I'm scared Papa will go in without me, but he always holds my hand. I won't talk about the tooth. Papa told me not to. He said people would look. And I won't tell you which hand. I told the lantern lady and she wrote it down, and then Papa read it and he was quiet all day.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
A bone knight stopped here and looked at me for a long time and asked how many times I went in. I said I lost count. He said that was a pity. He gave me a knotted string and said to tie one knot every time. I've got nine knots. I don't remember tying the last two. The waystones are nice. They're like big teeth, but friendly ones, like Mama's teeth when she laughed. They lean in to listen when I talk. Look. They're doing it now.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
In the dark, when you go through, everybody's going the same way, and if you turn round, just a little bit, you can feel them all turn round too. Like a whole field of people looking at you. And then the pull gets stronger, like it's cross. So you don't turn round. I turned round once. When I came up I had ash in my mouth and I didn't spit it back. I swallowed it. It tasted like the bread from Hearth-Night.

**Where is your father now?**
Getting water. He'll be back. He always comes back. Last time we went in he was holding my hand, and when we came up I was holding a hand. It was his. It was warm and wet, and I held it the whole way up the stair and it was his. And then he said I'll get water, Pip, and he went down the Fen path. He called me Pip. So that was him. I'm waiting here so he knows where. Can you count my fingers? Just to see. I'll hold them up.

### The digger of Widow's Lane

*Spoken beside the one flat lid in the Empty Ninth, where he sat on his own spade; he would not stand while he talked, and said standing up was for the ones he'd buried.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Coll. Coll Harrow, same as my father, who had the spade before me, and his father, who was named Harrow because he was the first to dig here and that's all anybody knew about him. The lane calls me Digger. The knights call me the Keeper of the Low Count, which is their way of saying I kept my bones from them twenty years. The children on the lane call me Face-Down, because of how I put the risers back.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
In the lodge at the top of Widow's Lane, a stone room with a turf roof and the tool-rack and a bed of barrow-straw. It smells of wet clay and lamp-smoke from the crypt mouth. I eat what the widows bring: black bread, cap-broth, a bowl of groats on a burial day with a thumb of fat stirred in for the dead to smell. They say the dead like the smell and stay down for it. Doesn't work. Next door is nobody. At night you hear the lids. A little creak. Like a floor when someone's standing still on it.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
I believe what I've put in the ground stays there, mostly, and what doesn't, walks home. I used to think the dead went down into the god and were done. Now I think the ground takes a breath in, every dawn, and the dead go down with it a finger deeper, and once in a long while it breathes out, and one comes up. When I die I want to be in that flat lid. The one place round here where the breath goes out.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Walk the rows. Look at the dishes, see which ones sank. Put a stone on any that are trying. Dig if there's a death. Rest. On Low Day, the dark of the year, the whole lane comes down with lanterns and stands one at the foot of every barrow, and we sing the Lying Song, which is only a list: lie down, lie still, lie low, lie long, till the Kneeler finishes. We sing it nine times. Nine barrows. The knights stole the nines from us, I always say. We had them first.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
The Kneeler finishing. That's the fear on Widow's Lane, and don't let a widow tell you different. Whatever it's praying, it's nearly got it. I'll not talk about the Bellwether with the name on it. I put that man down whole, I told you. I dressed him myself. I'll not talk about what the bell was made of either. It wasn't bronze. That's all.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The Pale Order carry up what I give them now, and it doesn't come home, so I suppose they're good for something. The mirror-folk want our dead to look at their own faces before we bury them, so they'll stop. I let them, once, and the dead man's eyes moved. The stones: there's the Barrow ring, east of the lane, and the lids on this side dish toward it. Every lid. Take a line from the deepest part of each dish, like a surveyor would, and they all run to the ring.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
Dirt from a grave doesn't go back in. You dig out a barrow, you lay the dead, you shovel it all back, and there's always a little heap left over. Always. Should be the other way, shouldn't it, with a body in there taking up room. I used to spread the extra on the lane. Now I keep it in a sack in the lodge. It's getting to be a lot of sacks. They're warm.

**Why have you started sleeping near the flat lid?**
Because the breath goes out there. You lie by any other grave on the Barrows, you wake with your chest going in, in, in, and your mouth dry and full of ash. By the flat lid you breathe like a man. And the long bones that were in the Empty Ninth, the ones I gave the knights, I think they were lying there to hold that lid flat. A wall set by someone careful. Now they're gone up the mountain, and the lid's still flat, and I'm lying next to it. Somebody's got to be the weight.

### The hunter of the Hollow Wood

*Spoken on the flat stone at the Wood's edge, in a thin rain; the hunter kept one palm on a trunk the whole time, and gave no name.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
I told the last one. The Wood has my name. My father gave it to a trunk when I was born, the way it's done here. You speak it into the bark and the bark takes it and you don't use it again. Pilgrims call me the Hunter. The fungus-pickers from Cap Hollow call me Tenth, because I'm always at the tenth trunks. That'll do.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
A hollow under the Ribcage Bough, dry, with a floor of old bark. It smells of cut sap, which smells like a sweat that isn't yours. The light's green-white, from the caps, and at night it's white caps only, a little light on the floor like a banked coal. I eat white-cap stew and root-bread and a sour red sap I tap off the dead trunks, the ones that stopped. Next hollow over is the pickers, four families. At night you hear the trees. Slow. Slower than you. You try not to breathe in time.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
The god fell here. This is its blood gone up into trees. Everything I hunt came off it and got up. So when I kill a thing I'm putting a bit of the god back down, and I bury a finger's width to say sorry, or to say done. When I die the Wood takes me the way it takes the finger's width. In a night. And there'll be a mark in the bark somewhere, at the height of someone's heart.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Walk the lines at dawn while the caps are white. Hunt what's got up in the night. Bury the width. Go home before the Moth-Saints. The holy days are the Flat Days, if they come, when the ash at the ring lies still and the Wood leans east. Nobody hunts. The pickers put white caps on every flat stone. We sit in the doorways and wait for the lift. Only once in my life. The old hunters say the Flat Days come round the way the thaw does, only longer.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
I fear the lantern trunk. It was a ninth. I'll not talk about my sister's room. She's older than me and she talks less every year, and more in her sleep. She knows things about the room now. How many doors. What's on the table. She says the table's set.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The brothers from the Villa came in once to bleed a trunk into a bowl. They drank it and one of them laughed for an hour and the others carried him out. Pale Order knights ride round the Wood, never through. The paper-folk tie strips to the tenth trunks, and the bark grows over the strips. The stones are where the roots go. Roots go to water, in the old stories. These go to the ring. So I suppose the ring's what they drink.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
You can tell how long a thing's been dead in the Wood by which way it's lying. Fresh, it lies any way it fell. A day on, it lies with its head toward the ring. Three days, it's moved a hand toward it. I've marked them with sticks. Nobody drags them. The ground takes them the way a sleeper pulls the blanket up. Pickers won't eat caps that grew on a trail like that.

**You said the old ones say the roads were there before the Wood. Who laid them?**
Nobody laid them. That's what the old ones say, and I don't like it. They say the roads were already there, flat and hard, when the Wood got up, and the Wood grew back from them. My grandfather said, look at a road from the top of the Ribcage. It runs from ring to ring. It always has. Like a line drawn from one mouth to another by somebody's finger. He said the roads are where the god was going to be touched. I didn't ask by what. I hunted.

### The keeper of the reed hut at the Drowned Village

*Spoken at the cold hearth, as before; the cloth was still over the bowl, and she asked twice whether the Fan-Bearer had kept the doll.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
The boatmen call me Dry Nan, because the water never wets me, same as it never wets them, and they think it's funny. I was a child on the chapel roof. I was the one who climbed down. My name is on the table below, with the spoons. My mother wrote all our names on the table's edge with a burnt stick, so we'd know our places. I'll not use it up here. It's needed there.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
A reed hut on stilts of old door-posts from the village. Every post was a door. It smells of wet reed, though nothing's wet, and of the smoke from cold fires, which is a smoke you only get here. I eat reed-root, pounded, and fen-bread, and I drink with my eyes shut. The boatmen tie up to the Kneeling Arch and sleep there and sing badly. At dusk the bells go under the square. At night I hear the loom. I hear the shuttle go across, and back.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
The god's water is dry. That's all I know about the god. What happens when you die? You stay. You set the table. You leave the door ajar. My sister's still ringing, my mother's still laying spoons. They're dead or they're not, and the water's a lid over them. When I die I'll go under, I suppose. Down through the square, and I'll knock, and they'll say, who's that? And I'll have to tell them.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
I sweep the hut. I walk the edge of the square and look at the doors and count them open. I sit. In the evening I say the bell-names while they ring, the old names of the bells, Hark and Mercy and Little Brass. My holy day was Lych-Eve, before the water. We'd carry a candle to the Lych-Gate for every child the mill-race took, and float bread on the race. Now I carry one candle to the edge of the square for the whole village, and I don't float anything. Nothing floats.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
My face. You know that. I'll not talk about what my father brought up in the dry summer and put back. He did not eat that night and he did not eat the next, and he went out to the well at night and talked down it. He said please a great deal. He said a number, too. I'll not say the number.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
Your House hangs a doll on the hook and says the water is clean after. It was always clean. That's the trouble with it. The mirror-folk come to stare at the water and it shows them nothing and they go away pleased. Knights don't come. The ring by the Fen stair: it's the one thing out here the water never went over. It rose round it and stopped, like a hand that won't touch a hot pot. The boatmen won't pole within fifty strokes of it.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
The dry water tastes of the last thing you said. You drink it after a quarrel and it's sour. After a kind word, it's sweet as fen-bread. I drank some after I said my sister's name once, the day I came down, and it tasted of iron, like the hook, like blood in your mouth. I haven't said her name since. I say "my sister". It's thinner, but it's safe.

**You saw your face once, on the Pilgrim Road, and it was surprised. What did it do after?**
It looked at me a long time. I looked at it. Then it lifted a hand, its left hand, and parted its hair, like it was checking. Then a pilgrim stepped in the puddle and it was gone. I've been thinking about it for sixty years. It was surprised to see me because it thought I'd stayed on the roof. Or it thought it was the one who climbed down. One of us is on the right side of the water. Don't look at me like that. Drink your tea. Shut your eyes first.

### The keeper of the Heath fire

*Spoken across the banked fire on the Burnt Heath, the pilgrims asleep around it with their feet toward the road; she kept one eye on their feet.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Aud. My mother was Aud too, and hers. Pilgrims call me Keeper, and half the old walkers think I'm my mother, and I let them, because it saves telling them she's gone. She's in the lantern at the fork now, if you want her. The one that burns low and a bit to the left. That's her. She always did lean.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
The tent's reed-cloth, sewn from bast and tallowed, patched every thaw. It smells of brush smoke and ash-root broth and wet boots. I sleep on a heath-straw tick with my feet to the road like everyone. The broth is ash-root, a fist of barley, salt, a spoon of Kettlewick fat on a feast day. Neighbours are whoever's walking. The cages creak on the edge. At night you hear the road. It makes a sound when it's moving. Like a sheet being pulled slow off a bed.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
I believe it's under the Heath, and it's hot, and we mustn't feed the fire. When I die I'll go in the fire. Keepers do. We bank ourselves. My mother went in on a Turning Morning and the fire burned a little brighter for a year, then settled, and then she was at the fork in the lantern. I don't know how she got there. The fire gives you where you're needed.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Feed the fire. Make the broth. Say the rules. Pace the road to the saint when nobody's looking. Count the cages. Sleep. My holy day is Turning Morning, midsummer, when every sleeper at the fire lies with feet toward the corner, the far corner, the herd's corner, and I let them, the one day of the year. In the morning we see whose feet turned back toward the road in the night. Those are the ones who'll go on walking. The ones whose feet stay, stay.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
I'm afraid of a big fire. You heard. I'll not talk about the second number the knight wouldn't tell me. I found out, you see. He dropped his book in the ash going out, and I picked it up. I read it before I gave it back. It was the same number as the first, only with one more figure on the end.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The knights sit where you're sitting and eat my broth and count my cages. The Villa brothers won't sit at a banked fire; they say it's a wound kept closed. The Gilded Peak monks walk by at night and never stop, and the pilgrims' feet all point at them as they pass. The stones make the folk in my cages. So I've no love for them. But pilgrims need them, and I need pilgrims, so I keep my mouth shut, like the saint.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
The ones in the cages are always hungry, and bread doesn't help, so we give them ash. A pinch in a cup, like the saint's lap. They eat it gratefully and say thank you and they say it very nicely. The one with the extra hand says it twice, once for each side. I keep a crock of ash by the tent just for the cages. It's never gone down. I fill it, and I feed them, and it's always full.

**Old Garrow said there was a seat under the herd, with someone patient in it. Did you believe him?**
Garrow was a liar his whole life, and a good one, and he was never patient himself, so he wouldn't have made that bit up. He said the someone was sitting like a man waits for his name at a hearing. Hands on knees. He said the seat was the same stuff as the waystone plinths, grey, and stained at the foot. He said the someone had seven places at its feet, all empty, and they were the shape of the herd lying down. I don't believe him. I've paced the herd. It's seven. It's always been seven.

### Mother Brisk of the Kettlewick Stilts

*Spoken over the rendering kettle on Knot Night, when the stilt-folk go out along the causeway and tie a knot on every lamp at once; she did not go, and said the lamps knew she was busy.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Brisk. Tallow-wife. My mother was Mother Slow, because she took three days to render what I do in one, and her fat was the better for it. Everybody's Mother something on the Stilts once they've had a kettle long enough. The children call me Mother Stink. The priestess calls me Sister Brisk, meaning she wants a discount. My husband called me Bris, no k. Nobody's called me that since the Salt Ladder.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
Six legs up, second terrace, the house with the black kettle-mouth and the roof full of knot-coils. It smells like my hands, which smell like the Mire, which is fat and sweet and a bit like a baby's head. We eat mire-cake, which is reed-meal fried in fat, and bog-crust, and on a good day a pot of the clear broth off the top of the kettle. Neighbours are the Salt Ladder folk, who talk too loud. At night you hear her beat under everything, and the mud trying the ladders.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
She's the Mother, the one under the Mire, and she's warm and she's fat and she gives. That's the whole of Kettlewick belief and anyone who tells you more is selling candles. When you die you go over the rail or into a lamp. Over the rail, she keeps you. Into a lamp, the town keeps you. I've told my granddaughter lamp. I want to see how tall she builds.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Dig on the lift, walk on the beat, render all afternoon, skim, give back what won't melt, tallow the stilts at dusk. Tonight's the holiest: Knot Night, first dark of the wet season, when the whole town goes out and ties on every lamp, and they're counted back in, and the ones that come back are sung up the ladders. You can hear it from here. The ones who don't come back get a lamp of their own, if they want one.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
I fear the Flats. The ones walking out there. I fear the night one of them steps onto the causeway, only it won't be a step for it, it'll be a lean. And I won't tell you the word my mother said over the tooth. She never told me. But I've given back a tooth myself since, flat and wide as hers, and when I held it I knew the word. It came into my mouth. I didn't say it. I'll never say it.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The Villa Llaga brothers come to buy tallow and never haggle, which I don't trust. They call the Mire "the Lady's Table". The bone knights come through on their way to the Barrens and won't eat anything fried. The paper-folk want to hang dolls on our lamps and we let them, and the lamps burn the dolls, politely. The waystone: it's the only thing out here that doesn't reach. Everything on the Mire reaches. That keeps still. Makes you wonder what it's already got hold of.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
The fat from the Mire has a grain in it, like wood, if you cut it cold. All of it runs the same way. You dig it out and look at the grain and it points to the waystone, every spadeful. Tallow-wives know. We cut across the grain because the candles burn steadier. Cut with it, and the flame leans.

**You said the pilgrims all name the same place. What do they call it?**
They say it quick, like it's a word everybody knows. It sounds like the old stilt-word for the space behind your teeth, where the tongue sits when you're quiet. Grandmother used it for the back of the kettle, where it's hottest. I asked one, where's that, then? He pointed down, between his own feet, and then he looked surprised he'd done it, and asked me the way to the Salt Ladder again. I gave him a candle. It leaned.

### The gourd-writer on the White Road

*Spoken in the shade of the ninth standing rib, on the third night; she held her hands up against the stars and counted ten while she answered.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Essa, from Low Ashwake. I dyed cloth, grey mostly, the only colour anyone buys. On the road they call me the Dyer, because my hands are still grey to the wrist. The brother calls me Pilgrim, because he calls everyone Pilgrim. The girl from the stilt-towns calls me Auntie, and I haven't the heart to ask her why.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
Under the rib. For now. It's the coolest place on the Hide at noon and the warmest at midnight. It smells of dust and our own breath and something like an oven cooling. We eat the edge-well brothers' bread, which is baked hard as the ground, and a paste of bitter root and salt. We drink little. I sleep in a row of a hundred, on the white, head to foot, no blanket. At night the rib ticks. Someone always prays. Someone always counts.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
The brothers say the white is her bone and we walk on the Mother. I believe that now. I didn't in Ashwake. When I die I'd like to lie down out here and be taken up like the saints, and sit, and breathe once an hour, and have the dust move on my lip. I think that would be restful. The brother says it's the holiest thing a Tithed body can do: become a little less soft.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Walk before the sun, lie up at noon, walk at dusk, count at night. Drink a cup a day and leave a mouthful for the Caravan. My holiest day will be the Citadel, when we see the Femur standing and all kneel on the white together and say the Nine Tellings, which I have learned, all nine, by the brother's knuckles. Before this road, my holiest day was Grey Market, and I sold a whole bolt to a knight, and was proud of it for a year.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
I'm afraid of eleven. Everyone out here is. We don't say it. We say "the one after". I won't talk about the ribs' count and what I heard when I put my ear to it. I told you the brother wrote the number in his book. I didn't tell you I saw the page after. He had written "twenty-three" at the top in a steady hand. Below it were other numbers. They weren't in his hand.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The brothers are hard and kind. The Villa folk came to the edge-well to buy water with blood-pennies and were sent off. A Gilded Peak monk passed us barefoot on the white at noon without shade, and none of us saw him drink. The waystone out here breathes deep and slow, one breath to seventy of your heart, the brother says. Pilgrims don't use it. The brothers won't let them. He says the Barrens ring goes somewhere the Mother would rather we didn't.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
The dust at the saints' feet is softer than anywhere else, like flour, and the pilgrims take a pinch and put it in their mouths, and it tastes of nothing at all. Nothing. Everything out here tastes of salt and bone. That dust tastes like a word you've forgotten. I keep a pinch in my cheek while I walk. The brother does too. He thinks I haven't noticed.

**Why did you want to come out here at all?**
My mother came back from a waystone missing a word. Only one. The word for the colour of the sky at dusk. She'd stand at the window and try for it with her hands. I thought, if I walk this road on my feet, all the way, no stones, then when I get to the Femur I'll be all of me. Everything I brought, I'll bring. The brother says that's a proud thought. He says the Mother will teach me. Ten. There. See? Ten.

### Notes for the lore bible

### Places

- **The Aveth Lantern:** the Sighing Lantern at the Ashwake crossroad has a name scratched small at the foot of its glass: "Aveth. She was kind about the road." (Ninth-Mile)

- **Cinderholt**, a forge village near Ashwake; Wenna's home. Its Hearth-Night loaf is a local rite.

- **Cap Hollow**, a hamlet of four fungus-picking families in the Hollow Wood, next to the hunter's hollow under the Ribcage Bough.

- **The Ashwake inn** with a slant floor and a measuring doorpost; the surveyor's winter home. The Salt Ladder in Kettlewick has a lamp at its foot and loud neighbours.

- **The thread-meeting place:** south of the Burnt Heath, east of the Drowned Fen, flat, dark, warm ground with no road; threads there grow thick as wrists. Candidate secret zone.

### Beliefs and rites

- **Day of the Wiped Glass** (first thaw): every pilgrim wipes every lantern with their own sleeve and gives beggars their names to say to the lanterns.

- **Stepping Eve** (longest night): walkers cross a ring one after another; the last one in, with no one to hold the lip, is honoured.

- **Low Day** at the Barrows: lanterns at every barrow foot and the **Lying Song** sung nine times ("lie down, lie still, lie low, lie long, till the Kneeler finishes").

- **Flat Days** in the Hollow Wood: when the ash lies still, nobody hunts, white caps are laid on every flat stone.

- **Turning Morning** (midsummer, Burnt Heath): sleepers lie feet toward the herd's corner; those whose feet turn back toward the road by dawn will walk on.

- **Knot Night** (Kettlewick): the whole town ties a knot on every lamp at once, is counted back in and sung up the ladders.

- In the Hollow Wood, a newborn's name is spoken into a trunk and never used again.

### Daily life (food, dress, work, sayings)

- Food: grey bread, ash-root broth with barley, white-cap stew, root-bread, red tapped sap from dead trunks, reed-seed pot-cakes, fen-bread, mire-cake (reed-meal fried in Mire fat), bog-crust, groats with a thumb of fat "for the dead to smell", Barrens hard-bread with bitter root paste.

- Kettlewick tallow has a grain that points to the waystone; candles are cut across the grain, because a candle cut with it leans.

- Crossers come up with clean white teeth, sometimes with sharper back teeth; old walkers "grin rich".

- Villa Llaga brothers pay in **blood-pennies**, dried red clots that go soft in the palm; nobody on the road will take them.

- Barrens pilgrims avoid the word eleven and say "the one after".

### Things they disagree about

- Whether the waystones are a mouth (Ninth-Mile), the road's own hearths (Old Thirty), the only honest thing on the road (the measurer), or friendly teeth (Wenna).

- Whether the dead go down (Wenna, the digger), stay and set the table (Dry Nan), or go into fire and lantern (Aud).

- Old Thirty claims walkers who never come up are "kept whole"; she admits she said it to make a girl brave.

- Kettlewick: the priestess says the Mother is fed; tallow-wives say only "bring longer timber".

### Threads worth pulling

- **The rings are coming nearer:** Ninth-Mile's ninety paces are now eighty-nine (compare the measurer's shrinking distance to the fallen stone).

- **Eight hidden bands** under the turf at every stone, tight and old; the visible bands are the new ones (the measurer).

- **Wenna's father:** he came up after the last crossing, called her Pip again, and went for water and did not return. Which one came up? (Wenna)

- **The roads were never laid**, and run ring to ring "where the god was going to be touched" (the hunter's grandfather).

- **The seat under the herd** is carved of plinth-stone and has seven empty places at its feet (Garrow, via Aud); the knight's herd count ends in an extra figure.

- **The word over the tooth** in Kettlewick comes unbidden into the mouth of whoever gives a tooth back; the place risen pilgrims name sounds like "the space behind the teeth" (Mother Brisk).

- **Graves leave leftover earth**, kept warm in sacks; lids on the Barrows all dish toward the Barrow ring (the digger).

- **Numbers in another hand** below the brother's rib count in his book (Essa).

- Dry Nan's reflection **parted its hair with the left hand**, as if checking which of them had climbed down.

### The sweeper of the Nine Stairs

*Heard at the foot of the Sixth Stair, where he sat down in the dust to rest his knees; he kept a cloth over his mouth and lowered it only for the names.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Brother Sweeper. The book has a number for me, three thousand and something; the tallier would scold me for the something. The novices call me Old Broom, and I answer to the broom. Before I came up I was a carrier's boy on the salt road, with a name my mother gave me. It was taken a long time ago, and I let it go without a fight. Fifty years on the Nine Stairs. I have swept every niche of them. Some twice in a day. The lowest ones once, and never again.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
Cold. Dry. It smells of lamp and stone, and low down it smells of nothing at all, which is the dust. We sleep in niches cut like the niches of the laid, a hand shorter, on a tick of barley husk, so the body learns the shape early. Stair-bread in the morning, scored in nines. A broth of roots and niche-cap at night. On my left sleeps Brother Tomo, who snores in nines. On my right, a wall of the laid. At night the house settles. Small ticks. A rib moving a nail's width. You learn to sleep through it, or you get up and sweep.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
The Mother bears everything, and when I am done she will bear me, in a niche on the Sixth, facing the stair. Then brittle, then dust, then up into the pale. What lies past the pale I leave to the Wardens. My first master said the dust goes to her and lies on her like frost on a shoulder, and she is glad of the weight. I liked that. I have thought since that she has weight enough.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Up before the lamps are trimmed. Down the stair, always down. Kneel, sweep toward you, say the name, hold your breath over the pan. Bread at midday, which I eat standing, because if I sit I will not rise till evening. The Weighing, if the pan is full. The holiest day is the Feast of the Laying, when I hold the lamp. The holiest hour is the morning after, on the Sky Pogost, when the wind is still and you open your hands. Forty-nine times I have opened them.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
Dropping the pan. As a novice I spilled a pinch on the Third Stair and swept it up holding my breath so long I fell over. The Warden gave me that stair for a year. I will not talk about the lowest stair. You climb it on your hands; that is the whole of what I say. And the risers. Ask the tallier how many. I gave up counting them long ago.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The flesh-houses take the meat and leave the frame, and they keep the old terms. I hold nothing against them except the smell of their baskets. The lodges leave bone lying in the field for the frost to split: neglect. The Shrine Keepers throw it in the water, and our knights wade after it. The Peak we do not speak of. The waystones: a pilgrim steps into the ring and comes apart, blood and bone and all, and stands up somewhere else. Somebody sweeps that pit, I suppose. I hope he says the names.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
The laid face the stair with their mouths shut, and in the night some jaw always drops open. Every morning, somewhere on the Nine Stairs, one jaw is down. We push it to with one finger and say "hush, brother," which is a joke, and a bad one, and very old. Also, a pan swept at dusk and set by your bed sits lower in your wrist at lamp-trim. The brothers say my wrist is old. My wrist is old.

**You wrote of a room behind the long wall with your own broom-marks in it. Have you been back?**
Twice. The door was on the Sixth, where I left it. The second time there were forty-one niches. The tallier carries forty; do not tell him. Three of the long skulls had come round to face the door. I swept with the same short stroke, left foot leading. Going out, I looked back at the floor. My new marks lay over my old ones exactly, stroke on stroke, as if I had swept that room in that order many times. I have given the Sixth to the tithe-boy now. He does it well. He never asks.

### The novice who learned the Lesser Catechism

*Heard in the novice gallery above the Gate Stair, from a boy with a fresh welt across the back of his hand, who spoke kneeling out of habit.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Vasko. A novice, so the book has no number for me yet, only my cord. Since the penance the other novices call me Twenty-Eight. Brother Tallier started it to shame me, and they took it up. I let them. Twenty-eight is ten, and ten is one, and the fewer is the holier. Brother Radko of the knights calls me Little Lamp, because I am to hold one at the Feast.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
The gallery over the Gate Stair. Twelve sleeping-niches and eleven of us, since Pavel went up to the Pogost at the thaw with a cough. We keep his niche swept. It is cold enough that your breath stands in front of the lamp. Stair-bread, pale broth, and on feast days a barley cake baked on a hot stone. At night you hear the windlass far down, and the novices telling their cords under the blankets. Click, click. Two hundred and six, and then somebody starts again.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
She bears the world, as a frame holds a hide, and she has never once set it down. I believe it. I think she is tired, too. You may say that of a mother. When I die I will be washed and anointed and laid, facing the stair, and I will watch. What for is the twenty-fourth question. I expect I will know the answer then, and have nobody to tell.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
The bell. Tell the cord. Sweep the gallery. Carry water up from the snow-cistern. The catechism with Brother Tallier, kneeling, until my knees go. Then lamp practice on the Gate Stair, holding a lamp at arm's length and never letting it dip. The holiest day is the Feast. I have never held a lamp at one. This year. Brother Radko promised to write to me for it and tell me the low road. He is due home before the Feast.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
The cord, on the back of the hand. And the fifteenth and twenty-eighth questions, the scraped ones. I asked the old sweeper what was under the scraping and he said, "A better answer." I asked him better for whom and he went on sweeping. I will not talk about the stair count. I was told to leave it off, and I am leaving it off. And I fear the lowest stair, which novices are sent to look at once and never taken down.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
I have seen none of them. The catechism has nothing on them, so Brother Tallier says they stand outside the Count. The knights say the Shrine Keepers pray to water and everything in it, eight million of them, and none has a number. I laughed and was struck. The waystones I know from pilgrims at the grate. You come apart, they say. I asked a woman how many bones she came back with. She put her hands in her sleeves.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
Tell your cord fast in the dark and you lose the end, and the cord seems to go on. Every novice finds it in his first month and nobody warns you. The trick is to tell it slowly, with the first knot held between your teeth, so you feel it come round. Also, the Gate Stair is the only stair where the laid are allowed to be touched for luck, and the finger-bone on the ninth step is worn smooth as a pebble.

**What did you count on the stair, for your penance?**
Every riser of every flight, top to bottom. I counted twice, because the first count made no sense. The second made the same sense. I will say this: it closed. Its root was nine, which is good. Brother Tallier told us his own novice count once, to shame us for slowness, and it was smaller, and its root was nine too. Every novice's penance comes to nine. They all close. They are never the same nine.

### The tallier of the Ninth Stair

*Heard at the tallier's desk beside the scale under the Eighth Stair; he added across a leaf the whole time and never once looked up.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Seven thousand two hundred and nine. Root nine. I had a name. The book has blank lines where names were written, and mine is one of them by now. I am a tallier of the Ninth Stair: I count walls, I carry the open counts, and I go down as far as the Weighing and no further. Eleven counts behind my teeth. Twelve, since this winter; a novice's penance I was fool enough to hear.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
Here. The Eighth Stair, under the lamp, beside the scale. It smells of soot-ink and chrism; we set the ink with chrism. I sleep in a niche behind the desk so the book is never alone. I eat when a sweeper brings bread and remember to. My neighbour is the scale. It keeps odd hours. A pan of dust set on it at evening, and the beam a hair lower by morning. The Rule says to write the evening figure. I write the evening figure.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
The Mother bears, and she is a sum. When I am laid, my number is cut beside me and my count closes, and that is a small sum, soon done. Hers is the one I lie awake on. At the Last Number every bone of her is counted, and she rests, and nothing anywhere will be open again. I believe that. I weep to think of it. I have wept at this desk more often than a man of my root should.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Count. A wall a day out of season, with a sweeper holding the lamp. Reduce. Carry. Before sleep, tell my cord, then tell the open counts after it, nine times each. The Feast is the holiest day and the worst: every tally in the ossuary comes to this desk in one night, and I reduce the Great Count by dawn with the Wardens standing behind my chair. Then I say the root aloud. One. They say, "The One Bone," and go to bed.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
A count that closes by itself. It happens. You carry a wall at root seven for a year, and one morning on the stair you find it has closed, with no bone laid. I will not talk about the column. I wrote about the column once for the novices, which was one time too many. The leaf is in the book. Add across.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The Shrine Keepers count to eight million and stop, a round number and a coward's one. The flesh-houses weigh everything and count nothing. The lodges count souls, which have no figures. The waystones I have thought on. A pilgrim at the grate told his frame for me, before he stepped into the ring at the foot of the Ossa and after he came back. Two hundred and six, both times. The second time he reached the end with cord still in his hand. He said he had tied it loose.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
On the oldest leaves, where the names went blank, the numbers were written in chrism ink and the names in plain soot. The Hush took the soot. So we set our ink with chrism. The Order teaches that it thought of this. The Order found it done so, in the oldest pages, in a hand older than ours, and copied it, and forgot it had copied.

**The gift column is written in a hand you have never seen at work. Whose do you think it is?**
The Warden says an old tallier's, and that the office keeps it. Then the old tallier lives long: the hand is the same from the first row to the last. The strokes lean wrong for a man who writes with his right, and wrong for a man who writes with his left. Once I left the leaf on the desk overnight with the ink uncapped. By morning there was a new row. That was a week after the Feast, when no gift is given. I sat with my hands under my thighs until the Warden came down.

### The Brother of the Well

*Heard in the vat-room of the Ossuary of Nine Stairs; he would not let the paddle rest while he spoke, and set his questioner to stir between answers.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Four Thousand and Fifty, Brother of the Well. The novices call me the Warm Brother, on account of my hands. Stir. Thirty-one years at the windlass of the Third Well and the vats. I render the chrism for the laid, the tallow for the lamps, and the pots the knights carry down to the villages. Before my vows I was a Barrens lad who could lift a sack of salt one-handed. The Order taught me to use two.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
Down here, the warmest room in the ossuary, and the brothers upstairs envy it. It smells of tallow and hot stone and the tar from the splices. The light is vat-lamps, low and amber. I sleep on a shelf over the stove so I hear if a vat skins. The bread comes down cold and we warm it on the vat lids. At night the vats tick as they cool, and the windlass creaks when nobody is at the handle. That is the rope settling. Stir.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
She gives freely. That is everything I know of her and all I need. When I die they will anoint me with my own rendering, an honour kept for well brothers. Then I stand, and step down when I am asked, and carry. My master said a well brother's bones are the last in the ossuary to go brittle. Warm to the end. He is on the Fifth Stair, thirty years laid, hard as a new step. I lay my hand on him when I pass.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Before light, the splice-check. Then the windlass: nine hundred turns and on. Pour, stir, skim, pour. At midday, mould the tallow. At evening, fill the knights' flasks. The holiest day is Splice Day, the eve of the Feast, when we let down nine fathoms of new rope, tarred, and the Warden blesses the knot, and every well brother lays a hand on the splice as it goes over the lip. It is the one day the rope goes down on purpose.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
A vat left standing. A cold bucket, which I have never once drawn. I will not talk about the Fifth Well. I talked about it once, to a novice, who wrote it down, and I took his leaf and set it in a cake of chrism so it would keep and be out of reach. It is in the store with three hundred cakes. I have lost which. That seems right to me.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The flesh-houses buy our chrism from the pedlars and pour it in their wounds, and the wounds close hard, the way ours do, and they come up to the grate holding out their knotted hands and calling it a curse. I tell them they have a good hand now. Their Maiden bleeds and ours gives, and there is a use for both. The waystones: I am told the pit breathes, warm, like the shaft past the four hundredth turn. I have wondered whether there is a rope down there too. I walk.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
Chrism takes a thumbprint and keeps it. Press a cake on the day it sets, and in a year the print is sharp to the last whorl. Only a little deeper. The cakes at the back of the old store have prints you could lay a whole finger in, and none of us pressed that hard. The oldest have prints that go in further than a thumb is long. We sell the new cakes first.

**You wrote that the well said your number, then a smaller one, then smaller. What were they?**
Four thousand and fifty. Then four hundred and five. Then forty-five. Then nine. You see? My own count carried down, and the root never once changing. It was telling me toward the fewer, the way a tallier tells a frame, toward the One. It stopped at nine and said, *Kneel.* I have given those numbers to no Warden. If it had gone on and said one, I think I would have followed the rope down. Stir. You've let it skin. Stir.

### The knight on the low road

*Heard by a barrow-fire eleven days below the Barrens, with twelve of the standing dead in a row just past the light; he kept his left gauntlet on throughout.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Radko, knight of the Kostyak, of the Ossuary of Nine Stairs, twenty-two years under plate. The book has me as six thousand three hundred and ninety, root nine, which was thought lucky at my vows. The lowlanders call us white knights, or bone-takers, or worse. Their children shout "Grandfather Dust" at any of us until we lift our visors. Then they see a man's face and run faster.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
On the road. The chest rides on a frame of long bones between my dead, and I sleep sitting against it, in plate, because the barrow country has hands in it. It smells of turf-smoke and wet ground and chrism. I eat stair-bread from the Ossa while it lasts, then what the villages sell: root-cakes, fen-cap broth, bread with ash baked into the crust. They fry in god-fat down here; I refuse it. At night my dead creak at the joints as the cold settles, and the barrows breathe.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
She bears the world, and I carry home what has fallen off her. When I die the flesh-houses may want my meat, and my brothers will argue them out of it politely. Flesh to the high ledges; bones to my niche on the Fourth Stair, next to Dobrin, whose pauldron I wear. I would like to be asked to carry. I would rather carry than stand. A standing brother sees the same stair for a very long time.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Walk. Kneel where the ground is wrong. Say the place's name. Lift in two hands. Anoint, thumb and two fingers. Write it in the day-book, in few words. Walk. The holiest day on the road is the day you turn for home with the chest full and see the Ossa again and the pale above it. At home it is the Feast, as for everyone. I have missed four Feasts in twenty-two years. This will be the fifth.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
Ground that keeps its bone. It is rare. Say the name and wait, and it lets go. When it will not, the knights' book has a rite called the Third Asking. I have seen it done once, by an old knight, and I will not describe it, except that we moved camp a mile after. I will not talk about the Peak. Nobody under forty in plate knows what our dead did in that courtyard, and nobody over forty will say.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The flesh-houses keep the old terms. I have shared bread with their elders on a barrow-lid while we sorted a grave between us, meat to them, frame to us. Courteous people with wet hands. The lodges are worse: they leave the bone lying and sit with the soul, which needs no company. The Shrine Keepers I respect and cannot forgive; we dredge their rivers every spring. My dead will not go within a stone's throw of a waystone ring. They stop, and turn their skulls to the pit, and stand. I go round.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
The standing dead keep step even when they stand still. Put your ear near their ankles on a quiet night and you hear it: a small tick, all of them together, slow, like a man tapping time on a table. When I was new I took it for the cold. It keeps time for them. Twice this season I have heard it change its time, all at once, and I have looked up and found the ridge.

**Why do you keep the left gauntlet on?**
It fits close. Close is good in the barrow country. You want the true answer. At the Shallows I took it off to file the spur, and the hand came out of it slowly, the way a hand comes out of clay that has set round it. The back of my hand carried the same ridge the gauntlet did. Fainter. I put it back on. I would rather file one of them. Nobody who reads the Codex could send me home, so there: I have said it.

### The headman of Cold Adit

*Heard at the bridge of Cold Adit on the ninth morning after first frost, with the bread set out on the parapet under a cloth, waiting for the knights.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Wat Orrin, headman of Cold Adit, as my father was, and his. Wat to most, Headman when they want something. The knights call me Keeper of the Bridge, which is a title they made up, and I have come to like it. I keep the village book, the pit accounts, the lot-pot and the bridge. Forty-some winters. My wife says forty-some is what men say who have stopped counting, and she is right.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
Forty-one households in a cleft under the Ossa: stone houses with turf roofs, all leaning a little toward the mountain, the way men lean toward a fire. It smells of smelt-smoke and wet slate. The light comes late now. We eat oat-bread, turnips, pit-cap from the old drifts, and in winter a porridge with god-fat stirred in, if the pedlar came. I sleep on a straw tick with the book under the bolster. At night you hear the drifts closing: a long low sound, like somebody upstairs turning over in his sleep.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
The god is dead and we live on it, like moss on a stone. The Order's Mother holds up the mountain; I thank her at the bridge, since it costs nothing. When we die the knights come and take us up, and our flesh goes to the cold, and our bones stand in a hall none of us will see. My mother said the smoke off the peak was us going home. I say we are buried, and then borrowed. Where we end up I couldn't tell you. Up. Everybody says up.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Before light I walk the drifts with a lamp and look at yesterday's pick-marks, to see how much the rock took back. Then quarrels, tallies, a smelt now and then. The holiest day is this one: Bridge Bread, when the Order comes. The women bake from midnight: round loaves scored in nines, because the knights like it so, with salt on the crust. The children stand on the wall and count the dead going by. We tell them not to. They count.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
The lot. I pull it from the pot myself, every third year. I will not talk about my sister's boy; he went, and it is in the book. And I fear the drifts will close with men in them. Nobody sleeps in a drift now. We had a lad, Corm, who did, the winter I was twelve, and in the morning we found him, and the rock had— No. That goes in the book, and stays there.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
We see the Order and the pedlars and nobody else. Once a Shrine Keeper came up the valley looking for a spring to give our dead to. I showed him the tailings stream. He looked at it a long while and went down again. There is a waystone two days down the valley at Harrow Pit, three of its teeth fallen. The pedlars use it. Anse the pedlar came out of it last year and sold me a pot of chrism for a sack of oats, and his left hand had a knuckle more than I remembered. He says it always had.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
A pot of chrism in a cold house keeps it warm by a hair. You set it by the cradle. Every mother in Cold Adit does. The children who had chrism by the cradle are the sturdy ones, heavy in the bone. They walk late, and they sit down hard, and they never break anything when they fall. We think nothing of it. I have written it here so somebody might.

**Why do you think the eldest knight asked how many of you there are?**
I've turned it over every night since. Maybe the Order wanted to know how much bread to expect. Maybe how many to come back for. They count everything, and they have counted our dead in their books for longer than ours go back, so why not the living? Only they never asked before. My wife says he was being kind. "Bury deep this winter." We buried the smith's girl at the thaw, deep as we could dig, and the spade went easy. Next morning the grave stood a hand higher than the path. Kind ground.

### The tithed son of Cold Adit, now a sweeper

*Heard on the Sixth Stair, behind the long wall, from a young sweeper who would answer only to a number and asked that the lamp be kept low.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Nine thousand one hundred and seven. It was nine thousand one hundred and six; the Tallier corrected it. Seventeen; eight. Eight is a man's root; it wants one. I sweep the Sixth. The brothers still call me the Tithe-Boy, though I have been here six Feasts. Some say Oskol's Boy, for the yoke. The name my mother used is at the top of her letters, and the brother who reads them to me reads round it.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
Here. Behind the long wall. The rooms go on further than the lamp does. It smells of nothing, which is the dust, and the dust keeps still. I sleep in the novice gallery still, because nobody moved me, beside Pavel's swept niche. I take my bread down here. At Cold Adit there was oat-bread, and porridge in winter; I remember the porridge better than the house. At night, down here, I hear the knee-high stairs. They make a sound like someone large sitting down, very slowly.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
She bears. The fewer is the holier. When I die I will close, and be laid, near Oskol. Oskol faces up. I asked the Warden why Oskol faces the roof when all the others face the stair. He said Oskol was set so. I set him. I set him facing the stair, with my own two hands. I think about that, and then I sweep.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Sweep toward me. Say the names. Down here I know none of them, so I say the bones, and when I run out of names for the bones I say numbers. The Tallier said I may. My holiest day was the Feast when the Warden gave me my number. Now it is any day I close a room. It happens: I sweep, I count the niches, and the root comes to nine, and I sit in the dust, and it is very quiet.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
Being given my name back. I will not talk about Cold Adit. You are going to ask about Cold Adit. Bury deep this winter. The ground at Cold Adit is kind. That is all of Cold Adit.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
In the village we called the knights the white men and hid behind the wall when they came. Mam said the waystone at Harrow Pit took a boy once and gave back a boy. The same size, the same hair. Everyone was glad. His mother was glad in front of people. The other orders I never saw. The Tallier says they are open counts, and that open counts are to be carried, and pitied, and never argued with.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
A frame you carry on a yoke for four years learns you. Oskol's skull turns further for me than for anyone. And my shoulders: the brothers said they would stay as if the yoke were on them, and they have. Feel. A ridge along each, where the yoke sat. Hard, and warm. The Tallier says a yoke-ridge is common among the tithed. He was looking at my hands when he said it.

**You counted the holes in your collar: four, wanting five. Has it closed?**
Last night I looked, and there were five. The fifth is sewn round with grey thread, our thread, small stitches, better than mine. I do not know who. Four and five is nine. It has closed. I sat a long time with it. I cannot remember what the letters said, when there was thread in them. I remember there were four. Now it is a good root, and I am glad, and I would like you to write that I am glad.

### The Warden of the Ossuary of Nine Stairs

*Heard in the Warden's press above the Gate Stair, the book shut on the table between us; he wore his right gauntlet and kept the left hand bare.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
The Warden of the Ossuary of Nine Stairs. The Rule writes a warden's number and never says it aloud, so I will not. My brothers say "Warden". The lowlanders at the gate say "the Old Man of the Grate", and they mean well. I was tithed out of Low Hask, a village the Barrens have since covered, so I may say its name freely. Nobody is left to mind. I was a knight twelve years before the book.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
Above the gate. The press is cold; the Rule forbids fire near the book. I sleep in a niche behind it, as the tallier sleeps behind his desk: we are the two who never leave a book alone. The window looks up the Ossa to the Pogost, and on a still morning I watch the dust go up. At night I hear the Gate Stair. Since he came, the laid there hold up their hands, and bone held so a whole year makes a sound. A creak. Like a roof under snow.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
The Mother bears; the Order keeps; at the Last Number she rests. That is the whole faith, and a warden's work is to keep it whole. When I die I go into the Wardens' Wall, and the next Warden says my name once and my number after, and from then on only the number. It is said that laid wardens are asked to step down more often than other brothers. It is a slander. It is true.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
The book at dawn. The grate at noon, where lowlanders ask for their grandmothers back and I give them the Order's words. The stair at evening, every flight, with a chrism lamp. The holiest is the Feast. I read the Count aloud at the wall, the tallier gives the root, and I say, "The Mother's gift," and nine hundred voices say it after me. Thirty years I have said it. I mean it every time. Write that I mean it.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
That the book will need a second book. I do not talk about the second hand in my entry. I do not talk about the Seventh Ossuary. I do not talk about my gauntlet. There: three things a warden does not talk about. Three is more than a warden should admit to, and a poor root.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The Order is courteous to every order and yields to none. The lodges and the Shrine Keepers I pity. The flesh-houses I trade with, through the grate. Of the Peak, the Wardens in agreement say nothing. The waystones were here when she fell, as were we. A pilgrim who comes to my grate by way of a stone smells of the pit for a day, warm and close, like the room over the Fifth Well. I have them wait on the road until it passes.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
The book weighs more than its leaves. Weigh them one by one and add, then weigh the book whole. Wardens have done it for as long as there have been wardens. I have done it. The binding is board and cord, and I weighed those too. The difference is small, a pinch of dust. The Wardens call it the ink.

**The second hand in your entry corrects you. Whose is it?**
Mine, some say. It is a fair likeness of my hand, a little older. I have no memory of writing it, and I would remember writing that the Warden has the week wrong. I have the week right. He came after the Feast. The sweepers remember before. The talliers remember before. I asked the laid on the Gate Stair, as a warden may. They keep their hands up and face the stair. I let the lines stand, because the book must hold everything. It also holds that I disagree with it.

### The keeper of the night-roof

*Heard in the roof-house above the Ossuary of Nine Stairs after dark, lying beside him on the cold stone with the lamp put out, both of us facing up.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Five Thousand and Thirteen. Root nine. Keeper of the night-roof. The brothers downstairs call me Up-There, or the Roof, or nothing, since they seldom see me awake. In my youth I was a Seeker and walked two lines to their ends. After the second, the Warden sent me up here to watch instead of walk. I have counted the sky thirty-three times.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
A stone hut on the lip of the ossuary, and over it the frame of long bones, strung nine by nine. No lamp at night; the eye must go dark. By day I sleep under hemp blankets doubled nine times, because the cold up here goes into the joints. Bread comes up in a basket on a cord. Broth comes up in a covered pot and arrives cold. At night the cords hum in the wind, nine notes. And some nights, far off, the dry sound of a floor of bones settling.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
Most of her is here under us. Some of her is still up there. When all of her is down she rests, and the dark is empty, and I may sleep. When I die I want to be laid on the Wall of the Come Down with the star-bones. The Rule forbids it; a man belongs on the men's wall. I ask the Warden every year. He says the same every year. We are both very patient.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Sleep through the day. Wake at dusk, eat the cold broth. Lie down. Count one square, reduce, write. If one falls, read the angle and go down and wake the Seekers. Sleep at grey. The holiest night is the Night of the Come Down at midwinter, when more fall than on any other. Brothers come up from every stair and lie on their backs along the lip in rows, and we say nine prayers for every line. The novices love it. It is the one night they may lie down on duty.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
A clear night with nothing left to count. I dream it: the cords above me, the dark empty, and I am glad, and then I look down and the dark is below me as well. I will not talk about the man on the low ledge. I wrote it. Written is enough.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The lodges say the stars are souls. The Shrine Keepers say they are spirits riding the breath. The flesh-houses say nothing and look at the ground. All wrong, and courteous about it. The waystones: once, on a very still night, I saw a ring far down in the Barrens, a small black mouth, and a white line drawn out of it up into the dark. Going up. The Rule has no leaf for a thing that rises, so I kept it on mine.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
Star-bone comes home cold and stays cold in the niche. You can find the Wall of the Come Down in the dark by walking toward the cold. The novices dare one another to lay a cheek on it for the count of nine. The mark stays pale on the cheek a whole day, and the Tallier knows who did it, and says nothing, because he did it too.

**What is on the leaf under the stone?**
The points that went and did not fall. Their squares, their rows, their nights. Eight, now. I tried to reduce them and no root came out right. Then one night I laid all eight on the frame at once, with pebbles on the cords, and saw that they made a line. Straight. Coming down the sky toward the Ossa, square by square, far apart, as if something slow were walking. The next square on that line is the first of the ninth row. I have not counted it since the ledge. I will count it tonight.

### The Tithed woman at the grate

*Heard at the grate of the Ossuary of Nine Stairs on her second day of waiting; she sat on the step with her left hand open on her knee, palm up, for anyone who cared to count it.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Two hundred and seven. Write that. I had a name at the thighbone citadel, and a man, and a mending stall on the market stair. Those are all well where they are. Leave them. The salt-carriers I walked with called me the Mender, because I mended their bowls on the road for bread. I would like the number, please.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
The citadel, under the great femur, a room over my stall. Dry. Everything tastes of chalk. We eat millet cakes and a sour root soup, and when the salt caravans come in, flat bread fried in god-fat. At night the femur creaks in the wind like a mast, and the pilgrims sing on the market stair. Now I sleep at your grate. It is cold. Your brothers pass bread through the bars. Good bread, scored in nines. They pass it in two hands, which I like.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
I believed what the citadel believes: you go into the Last Breath with everyone, and the spirits carry you. Now I believe she holds, and we were carried in her, and one of us knelt. When I die I want to stand in your wall, facing the stair. I will keep watch. I know what for, now. Do not ask me. The answer is warm, and I have no words for it that are ours.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Mending. Crack, root-glue, bind, set it in the sun. My holiest day was the Festival of Bowls at the citadel, when every pilgrim's bowl is set out on the market stair and the menders go down the rows ringing each one with a knuckle to hear whether it holds. Now my holiest day is the day at the well. And the next will be the day your Warden writes me down.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
That he will not write me. That I will sit here until my knees knit altogether and I cannot rise to go in when the gate opens. I will not talk about the sound it made, the one your tallier left a gap for. He gave it three words' width. It is longer than three words. I think it is still going, somewhere, very low.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The orders are kind at the citadel. The Shrine Keepers' women gave me water on the road. The lodge men would not look at my finger. I used a waystone once, years ago, from the Barrens' edge to the citadel. You come apart. You feel every bone let go of the next, like a crowd leaving a hall, and then they come back in, and they sit down in some other order, and you stand up. That is all I will say of the stones.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
A mended bowl rings lower than a whole one. Every mender knows it; you can tell a mended bowl in the dark. The old women of the citadel say that is why old pilgrims have low voices: they have been mended on the road. I have been mended. Listen to me. Lower, yes?

**It said you knelt once, inside, when she held. Do you remember it?**
No. Yes. I remember a floor, warm, very wide, sloping a little toward the middle, and a great many people kneeling on it all facing one way, and above us a sound like a held breath. I remember my knees on that floor. I was small, or everyone else was very tall. I have never been there. My mother said I dreamed it as a child, and cried when I woke because I had stood up. Since the well I cannot stand without thinking of it.

*Afterward the Warden came down to the grate and answered two questions standing, his hands behind his back.*

**What did you count in her hand?**
The Rule tells the frame of a whole man at two hundred and six. I counted her left hand, and the rest of her through the bars, joint by joint, by the grate lamp. Two hundred and seven. I counted again. Then I went in and counted my own, in the dark, which a Warden need never do. Write that I counted correctly. Write nothing more about my own.

**Will you write her in the book?**
The book holds the laid and the brothers. She is neither. I have written her on the second leaf, the one we add across. It was the only place in the book with room. I put the lamp out before I did it. Tomorrow we open the grate a hand's width and pass her bread, as the Rule allows. The Rule says nothing of a closed count at the gate. I have asked the tallier to add across, and he has asked me why, and I have given him no answer.

### Notes for the lore bible

### Places

- **Sleeping-niches:** brothers sleep in wall-niches cut like the niches of the laid, a hand shorter, on barley-husk ticks, "so the body learns the shape early."

- **The tallier's desk** sits beside the scale at the Weighing under the Eighth Stair; the tallier sleeps behind it and never leaves the book alone.

- **The roof-house:** a stone hut on the ossuary's lip under the 81-square frame; bread and broth are hauled up on a cord. The **Wall of the Come Down** is cold enough to find in the dark.

- **The Wardens' Wall:** laid wardens, whose names are said once by their successor and never again.

- **Harrow Pit:** a waystone two days below Cold Adit, three of its teeth fallen; the pedlars use it.

### Beliefs and rites

- **Splice Day** (eve of the Feast): nine fathoms of tarred rope let down the wells; every well brother lays a hand on the splice. "The one day the rope goes down on purpose."

- **The Night of the Come Down** (midwinter): the whole ossuary lies on its back along the lip in rows, nine prayers per falling line.

- **Bridge Bread** (Cold Adit, the ninth day after first frost): round loaves scored in nines, salted, set on the bridge parapet for the knights.

- **The Third Asking:** the knights' rite for ground that will not give up its bone. Seen, never described; "we moved camp a mile after."

- **Chrism ink:** the Order writes numbers in soot-ink set with chrism, because on the oldest leaves the soot names went blank and the chrism numbers stayed. The Order found this in older pages and forgot it had copied it.

### Daily life (food, dress, work, sayings)

- **Food:** stair-bread scored in nines, pale broth of roots and niche-cap, barley cakes baked on a hot stone for feasts. Lowlanders fry in god-fat; brothers refuse it.

- **Novice lore:** tell your cord slowly, with the first knot held in your teeth, or the cord seems to go on.

- **Jaws:** every morning some laid brother's jaw has dropped open; sweepers push it shut with one finger and say "hush, brother," a very old bad joke.

- **Chrism by the cradle:** Cold Adit mothers set a pot of chrism by the cradle for warmth. Those children are heavy-boned, walk late, and never break anything when they fall.

- **"A mended bowl rings lower":** the citadel saying for why old pilgrims have low voices.

### Things they disagree about

- **What the skulls turn for:** the lamp (sweeper), chrism lamps and no other flame (well brother), "us" (the novice's master), and the Ungated, who carried no lamp (Warden).

- **The Pogost:** to the village it is smoke, "the brothers going home"; to the Order it is dust.

- **When the Ungated came:** the Warden says after the Feast; the sweepers, the talliers and the second hand all say before.

- **What the stars are:** the Order says the Mother's bones, the lodges souls, the Shrine Keepers spirits of the breath.

- **Oskol:** Benn set him facing the stair; he now faces the roof, and the Warden says he "was set so."

### Threads worth pulling

- **Novice penances:** every novice's count of the Nine Stairs' risers closes at root nine, and no two are the same nine. *(the novice, Vasko)*

- **The gift leaf:** left out overnight with the ink uncapped, it gained a new row in a week when no gift is given. *(the tallier)*

- **Weights:** the Weighing scale sits lower by morning, a swept pan sits heavier at lamp-trim, and the book weighs "a pinch of dust" more than its leaves. *(tallier, sweeper, Warden)*

- **Chrism thumbprints** deepen with the years; the oldest cakes have prints deeper than a thumb is long. *(well brother)*

- **The Fifth Well** counted him down 4050, 405, 45, 9, "toward the One," then said *Kneel*. The novice's leaf is sealed in one of three hundred chrism cakes. *(well brother)*

- **The vanished stars** make a straight line walking down the sky toward the Ossa; the next square is the one where he saw the man on the low ledge. He means to count it tonight. *(star-watcher)*

- **The Tithed woman's memory:** a warm, wide floor sloping toward the middle, the congregation kneeling all one way. It matches the old halls beneath the ossuaries. *(the Tithed woman)*

- **Radko's hand** carries the gauntlet's ridge. He expects to miss his fifth Feast; the letter he owes Vasko is the one that lay unread. *(knight, novice)*

- **Benn's collar** closed to five holes overnight, sewn round with grey Order thread in a hand better than his. *(the tithed son)*

- **The Warden counted his own frame** in the dark after counting the woman's, and will not give the figure. He wrote her on the gift leaf. *(Warden)*

## Interviews: IV. The Gilded Peak

### The lamp-seller of Lower Stair

*Taken at her counter by the Well of Two Steps, at dusk, with the shutters half closed and the copyist on the buying side of the board.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Wick, they call me, because I sell them. The name I had on the Peak I left on the stair. They shave your head and give you a syllable to answer to, and mine was short. The children here call me the lamp-woman. The old women call me the one who ran. Both are fair. Four winters a novice, and I beat leaf. I never took the robe, only the grey smock with no belt that they give you until they are sure of you. They were never sure of me.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
Two rooms over the shop and a loft with a ladder. The street smells of lamp-oil and wet stone, and after dusk of the miasma, which smells of breath left too long in a shut room. I eat barley bread from the corner and a broth of pale-caps I grow in a crock under the stair. I sleep on a straw pallet with my back to the wall. Next door is a cooper who sings while he planes. I pay him to sing late. At night I hear the street lamps go out one by one as the oil ends, and I count them. I stop before the last.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
On the Peak I learned the god is lying down, the Hush is its bed, and we are what keeps it awake. Down here I believe what my customers believe: when you die you choose to be kept in a lantern or let go. Most choose kept. They are afraid of the quiet. So am I. I have told the cooper, if I go in the night, put me in a plain clay lamp and burn me slow, and sit beside it. I want to go out the way a room goes quiet, with company.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Up at dawn, trim wicks, sell oil to the carters before the road. At noon I shut the shop an hour and sit in the back where no light comes. Habit. On the Peak noon was when the gold was looked at, and a novice looked at her feet. Dusk is my busiest hour. The Peak's holiest was the Noon of No Shadow, midsummer, when nothing in the courtyard had a shadow and the saints could not be looked at at all. Down here I keep the Shut Night, the longest dark, when the lowlands light every lamp they own. I sell out by noon. It is the one day I am glad.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
Dusk. The bowl. My teacher. I will not talk about the night of the long silence in the dormitory, and asking it slant will get you nowhere. Ask the cooper why he sings so late. He doesn't know either. And leave the bread-woman out of it. Down here, when someone has lost a name, we say they are giving wrong change. I gave her wrong change the day I told her about her husband. I give it her every week now, in her favour. She never counts it.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The Pale Order count everything. They came up for our bones and they got glass. I sell them lamps at the full price. The flesh-houses send men through selling blood-cakes, and I won't have them in the shop. The Polished Heart barred their doors to us; they said our syllable was a hole in their cloth. The Shrine Keepers I pity. They spend their lives putting things back. My teacher walked round a waystone, never near the pit, one hand open. He said it breathes on the wrong beat. I have listened since. It does.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
A novice learning the leaf was given a bark book with one leaf already in it, beaten by her teacher when he was a novice himself. You kept it all your years and never opened it. I opened mine the night I ran. There was the leaf, green against the lamp, and pressed into the gold, very faint, the print of a thumb. I laid my own thumb on it. It was smaller than mine. My teacher was a big man.

**You said there were more steps going down than you had climbed. How many more?**
I told a pilgrim nine, once, and he nodded as if that were right, and it turned my stomach. So I tell everyone a different number. The truth is I don't know. Going up, the first day, holding my teacher's sleeve, I had a thousand and ninety. Going down I had more, and every time I say the number it moves. What I know is that near the bottom I was counting slow, the way you count a chant: a step for the syllable, a step for the silence. I think the extra steps were silences. I think I walked down some of them.

### The bread-woman of the corner

*Taken in her kitchen in the dark before the first bake; she would not sit, so the copyist kneaded beside her.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
The bread-woman. Every lane in Lower Stair has one and I'm theirs, the corner by the well, across from the lamp-seller. Before that, a baker's daughter. Before that, a child. I have a name and I'll give it you if you like, but people here have been careful with names since the bowls; they'd sooner call you by your work. Some call me Two-Cups, behind my back. I know. I let them.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
Front room is the oven, back room is the bed, and the loft has a copyist in it one winter in ten. Flour, smoke, the sour of the leaven crock. In winter the oven keeps the whole house, and the miasma lies on the step like spilled flour. We eat what doesn't sell: barley heels, a broth of god-fat skimmed and boiled with roots, a mash of pale-caps on feast days. The bed is straw ticking with a hollow on the door side. The lamp-seller over the lane burns a light all night. I hear the oven tick as it cools, and sometimes, on the door side of the bed, a breath I don't make.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
The priest says the god is dead and still dying, and we are the guests left in the house, and must keep it clean. I believe the priest on feast days. When I die I want the lantern. I want to be kept. I've seen what giving away does. You leave a warm cup. I'd sooner be a small flame in somebody's window than a warm cup nobody can account for.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Up in the dark, fire the oven, knead, shape, bake, sell at the well till the bread's gone, sleep an hour in the afternoon. Oil the boots each spring. Our holiest here is Bowl Eve, the first dusk after the thaw, when the monks used to come down. We set a loaf on every step up the lane, one to a step, and nobody eats them. In the morning they're gone and nobody took them. We still do it, with no monks. The loaves are still gone.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
The knock at dusk. And the day I stop setting two cups, because then it's gone properly, even the gap. I won't talk about the notches on the doorpost. Your copyist asked. I told him they marked how high the dough rose. He wrote that down. It's a lie, and I'll let it stand.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The Pale Order came through once asking to buy our dead. The priest ran them off with the bell. The flesh-houses buy bread by the cart and pay well, and I don't ask what for. The monks I fed, always, because you did. The waystone at the crossroads I keep clear of. My mother said if you step in, you come out somewhere else, all of you but the parts you gave away. I've given a part away. I'd be afraid of what climbed out the other end without it.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
Warm bread tastes of whoever kneaded it. Everyone in the lowlands knows it. Shut your eyes and you can tell my loaf from the river-parish woman's. Only there's a taste in mine I don't put there. Customers ask what it is. It's in the dough whatever I do, like a second pair of hands at the board.

**Who is the third cup for?**
I set it the night after the beggar came, and every night since. I thought at first it was for him. He'd have no use for a cup. I think now the third is for whoever that breath in the bowl was, if he's given back. And the second is for whoever I was before I gave him. I drink the second. I leave the third. In the morning it's the only cold one, and a little less full. The width of a lip.

### The goldbeater of the river town

*Taken at his mending-bench under a rain-roof by the river; he beat out a tin lamp-case the whole while and would not put the hammer down.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Goldbeater, still, though I've beaten no gold in fourteen years. Here they say the tin-man, or the pot-mender. On the Peak the monks called me the Weight, kindly: I was the one who ate. A lay man, twenty-odd years in the beating-house at the foot of the sanctum stair. My name I have. Nobody up there asked for it and I never gave it to a bowl, and I may be the only man off that mountain who can say so.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
A lean-to against the dyer's wall. Hot tin, river mud, the dyer's vats. Grey light; this town sits under the miasma more days than not. I eat barley and roots, and a fat-broth on market days, three bowls, because on the Peak I ate for a whole hall that wouldn't. I still sleep with a stone for a pillow. Soft ones give me dreams. At night there's the river, and the dyer's wife singing counting-songs to get her children down. The songs stop at nine. I've never asked her why.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
The monks said the god lies down in the Hush and we should let it. The priests here say the god is dead and keep a lamp lit for it. I'm a craftsman. I believe what I've had in my hands. I had a man in my hands with gold on his lids, and he was warm, and he wept that night. When I die, leave me be. No gold, no lantern, no bowl. Put me in the river with my hammers and let whoever wants me find me.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Up with the light. Mend pots, beat tin, trade a lamp-case for bread. On the Peak the holiest was the Noon of No Shadow. Every book of leaf had to be ready, and the masters re-laid the saints the night before, so that at noon the sanctum was one white fire. The monks sat in it with their eyes open. Lay men were let stand at the back. It hurt to look, and that was right. That was the whole rite: be looked at by light till there's nothing left to look at.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
Good work. When the seam goes and the face goes and there's only shine, I get a feeling in my hands like cold water. I won't talk about Master Enshō's hands. I told your copyist the posture: open on his knees, the open hand. I didn't tell him about the fingers after. Leave that.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The Pale Order wanted our size, bone-boiled. My master burned the pot. That tells you enough. The Polished Heart paid good gold for leaf and barred their gates to us after. I beat leaf once for a flesh-house reliquary, and it came back for mending with a thumbprint pressed into the gold from the inside. The waystones I leave to pilgrims. A man steps in, comes apart, goes on somewhere else. A leaf under the hammer does the same, if you think on it. Every blow, more of it and less of it.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
Gold keeps the last hand. Lay beaten leaf on anything and a week on, if you breathe on it, you'll see the whorl of whoever pressed it down. Every saint on the Peak carried the masters' thumbprints, hundreds, one over another. Nobody breathed on the saints. I did, once, at Master Enshō's mouth. There was only mine.

**Why did you cut the flaw so it fell at the mouth?**
I've asked myself. Some nights I tell myself it was spite against the masters. Other nights, that a man laid in gold should have somewhere to breathe, a hole the width of a hair, and it wasn't my place to say otherwise. The monks would have called it keeping him. A gilded face with a pinhole is a door left on the latch. Maybe. I did it to every saint I could reach, eleven years. I don't know how many I reached. I don't know which niche was empty.

### The bell-caster of the waystone camp

*Taken at the waystone camp below the Knee, inside the ring of fang-stones, where he mends pilgrims' pots and was paid again in lamp oil.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Bell-caster. Pot-mender now, like every craftsman who worked for the Gold Bell. The valleys are full of us, hands with nothing to do the work they learned. My father cast for the Peak, and his father. The pilgrims here call me the Listener, because I tap. I don't mind it.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
A lean-to of old mould-boards at the edge of the camp. Ash, always, the ash over the pit, and pilgrims' broth, and hot bronze from my brazier. Poor light all day; the pit takes it. We eat what pilgrims leave: barley, dried roots, a hard bread baked in the ash that tastes of it. I sleep on a board laid over my father's scales, so I know if anything's taken in the night. Next to me is a woman who sells rope to pilgrims afraid of the pit. They tie themselves before they step in. The rope comes back up empty. At night I hear the pit breathe.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
My father said the god was a bell somebody struck once, very hard, a long time ago, and we are all the hum. The monks wanted the hum shaved out. I understood them better than I liked. When I die I want to go quick. One stroke, a clean word, and quiet. A bell that goes on humming is cracked. I've stood in the lowland towns at night and listened to the lanterns of the kept. They hum.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Pilgrims come in, I mend their pots, tap them, hand them back. I watch who steps in. I watch who comes up out of other stones, blood and bone and then a man again, shaking. The Peak's holiest, for me, was the Ringing of the Great Bell at midwinter; every lesser bell on the mountain kept still a month before it. I never heard her once. My wife heard her best, every year. She's dead now. I can say her name. Most days.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
I fear the wrapping going warm under my bench. I fear the morning her name doesn't come back. I won't talk about the width of that last bell's mouth. I said shoulders. I'll say no more on whose. The monk brought the cord and I measured it against myself before I'd thought, and it was too narrow for me, and I was glad.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
Pale Order brothers come through often. They count the fang-stones and argue the count over breakfast. The flesh-houses send their sick to step in, and they come up at some other stone, well or dead. The waystone, you've heard it now. Tap a fang-stone. It rings a little, and there's a crack in it: it keeps talking after. Something under there never learned to go quiet. The Peak would have shaved it out. I'd not cast that bell for anyone.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
The bronze for a Peak bell always took a little of the pilgrims' gold. A thimble, never enough to move the tone. The monks called it the listening part. I asked what listened. They said the gold was there so the bell could hear itself go quiet. I put in the thimble thirty years and never asked again.

**What syllable did the tongueless bell already know?**
The monk wouldn't sing it. Every other bell, he sang me once, low, so I could carry it. This one he said I'd carried already. I laughed. He didn't. Then he tipped the bowl over the crucible, and here's what I kept back from your copyist. When he tipped it, I breathed in. So did the three boys at the furnace, all of us at once, the way a hall breathes in before a chant. Nobody sang. The breath went into the pour. That was the syllable, I think. The breath you take for a word you never say.

### The magistrate of the assize

*Taken at his own table in the last month of his office. The copyist's page has a clean space at its head, where the magistrate's name was written.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Magistrate of the parishes under the mountain. You may write that. My name you may try to write; my clerk has tried. It goes onto the paper, and by the time the ink is dry there is a clean space. To my face I am Your Honour. Behind my back, the Clean Page. I heard a child say it in the lane. I fined him, and then remitted the fine, because he was right.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
The assize house at Lower Stair: three rooms over the lock-up. Ink, sealing-gum, the damp of old paper, lamp-smoke. My housekeeper leaves bread from the corner, a pale-cap stew, and on assize days a fat-broth for my voice. I sleep in a box bed with the doors shut. Below me the lock-up stands empty since the bowls; there is nobody we can charge. At night I hear my clerk writing in the next room. He writes all night. I have never seen what.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
The law holds that the god is dead and its estate passes to the living under the Hide's charter. That is a fiction for courts. Privately, I believe the Hush takes the names first and the rest after, as a bailiff takes the deed before the house. When I die I want my name cut in stone as deep as a mason will cut it. I expect the stone will be clean by spring.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Depositions in the morning. Judgment after noon, when the light is good for reading. I read what I wrote the day before and count the spaces. The holiest day of a lowland court is Quarter Day: debts paid, oaths renewed, the priest brings the parish book and we swear on it. Last Quarter Day a third of the swearers could not find their names in the book to swear on. We swore them on the spaces.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
The edges. Every clean space in every book I keep grows wider by a hair a week. I have measured with a thread. I fear the day two of them meet across a line. I will not talk about my ninth month. You will ask, and I will say the matter is under seal, and that will be true.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The Pale Order twice petitioned this court for the bones of paupers. I granted neither. They write every brother by his number, I am told, and I confess I envy them. The Shrine Keepers came and asked to see the parish books. They held the pages to the window, wept, and left. The flesh-houses bribe nobody; they have no need. Waystones lie outside my jurisdiction. A man who steps in at one parish and rises at another is, in law, two men, and I have declined to rule on it.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
Every magistrate in the lowlands keeps a second book, called the Hand Book. There are no names in it, only descriptions: a man in a brown coat; a woman with a ring she cannot get over the knuckle. It was begun when the bowls began. It has no standing in law, it is the only book that still works, and every court in the valleys keeps one in a drawer.

**What was the name you gave at your door, in the ninth month?**
If I knew, I would not have given it. That is how it works. I can tell you what I have. A line in my diary, in my hand. A ring cut with two letters I cannot read. A clerk whose board I pay and whose name I never wrote. And a habit: at dusk I stop on my own step with my hand out, palm up. My housekeeper has seen me. I think I am waiting to be handed something back. I think it was mine.

### The knight-scribe of the fourth chapter

*Taken in the tally-room at the foot of the Ninth Stair, in the Ossuary of Nine Stairs, where the copyist was admitted under a number.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Scribe of the fourth chapter of the Pale Order. In the book I am written by my number, as every brother is, and my number I have: its root is nine, and you will get no more of it. My name the Warden had. I asked him to write it back to me. He wrote back my number. The brothers call me the Counter of the Peak, and some of them, when they think I am at the far end of the stair, the Hundred-and-Seven.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
A room cut into the Ninth Stair, one step above the tally-room. Lamp-oil, dry marrow, the chalk we rub on the knot-cords. No daylight; we keep the lamps low so the bones sleep. We eat marrow-bread, grey and hard, and a broth of the pale roots that come through the walls of the lower galleries. I sleep on a bench of old tallies. Through the wall is the running Count, a hundred voices saying numbers all night. I hear it as a miller hears his wheel. When it stops, I wake.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
The Mother is the god's Bone. She died, and her bones lie across the Hide, and we lay them and count them, and when the Count closes she will rest. When I die I will be laid in a niche and given my number, and my number will outlast me, as numbers outlast names. That is the Order's word and I keep it. Only, since the Peak, I find I think about the three I left out of the book. Nobody numbered them. Where do they rest?

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
At the lamp-bell I rise and tell my frame on the cord, two hundred and six knots. Then the day's tallies: niches filled, cords knotted, open counts closed. I read counts aloud and a brother checks them. Our holiest is the Night of Nines, when every great ossuary counts at once and the nine stairs are said aloud from top to foot, all night, and at dawn the root comes out nine. It always comes out nine. That is how we know it was said right.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
An open count I cannot close. I carry the glass of the Peak in my head still. Two hundred and six, two hundred and nine. Its root will not settle; each time I add the figures I get another. I will not talk about the breath in the sanctum. I wrote it once to the Warden. I will not write it twice, and I will not say it.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The Gilded Peak took the Mother's bones and gave them to nothing. We went to take them back and lost a chapter. The flesh-houses waste as much as the Peak did, only warmer. The Polished Heart keep good books; I respect them. The waystones we count, stone by stone and ring by ring. We do not step in. A brother who steps in comes apart into his parts and is put together again, and we cannot swear he is put together with the same count.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
A brother tells his cord each morning with his eyes shut, and at the end his thumb is on the last knot. Every brother, sooner or later, finishes with his thumb one knot short and has to tell the whole cord again to find what he missed. We have a word for it that means "an early morning." We are all having early mornings more often.

**You wrote that a teacher of the Peak walks one step behind. Did anyone walk behind you coming down?**
I came down the thousand and ninety risers and counted them, and the count agreed with my count going up. I wrote that it was the only count that agreed. I did not write why. From the courtyard to the lowest gate I held the lamp high, and it threw my shadow down the stair ahead of me, and there were two. The second was one step behind mine. I counted for it, the whole way, under my breath. That is why the numbers agreed.

### The novice who carried the abbot's letter down

*Taken in the washing-house of the parish of Lower Stair, where she washes the dead before the lantern or the ground, and would speak only while her hands were in the water.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
I was the youngest on the Peak. The abbot sent me down with his letter on the ninth day. You can call me the washer; that is my work now, the dead of the parish, before they are lit or buried. The priest I gave the letter to gave me the work. On the Peak they called me Sō, which is only the sound of a breath let out. I kept it. It's short enough that nobody has asked for it.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
The washing-house behind the churchyard. Cold stone, a trough, a drain, lamps on hooks. It smells of ash-lye and of the dead, who smell of very little once they're washed. I eat at the priest's table: barley, root-broth, bread from the corner, a spoon of god-fat on feast days. I sleep on a mat by the trough. I can't sleep on straw; it talks. Nobody lives next to a washing-house by choice. At night I hear the lamps of the kept dead ticking in the church, and the lamp-seller's shutters over the lane, closing early.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
He said it every morning: "The Hush is the god's rest, and everything on the Hide is a wound that has not yet been allowed to close. We are wounds too. Try to be clean ones." I wash wounds now. I keep that. When I die I want my name given away before a lantern can have it. He used to say, "Die light. Leave nothing to carry. Somebody always has to carry it."

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Water from the well before light. Heat it, wash whoever the night left, wrap them. At noon I sit an hour in the dark of the washing-house with my hand open on my knee. On the Peak my work was the faces: I wiped a hundred and seven every dawn. The last niche on the left was always empty. The abbot said it was kept. Now my holiest is the tenth day of the thaw month, the day they climbed. I wash nobody that day. I open my hand and shut it once for every brother I can remember. Fewer each year.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
Being sent back up. He wrote, "Do not send her back up," and everyone who has read the letter since has wanted me to go up and look. The priest. The Order's scribe. Your copyist. I won't. And I won't talk about the old water-cut. I came down it at night with the letter in my shirt, alone, and I'll say I was alone for most of it, and that's the last I say.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
He said the Order told the truth and so did we, and so there would be blood on the stair. I hold to that. I wash their pilgrims same as anyone's. The Shrine Keepers came through the parish after, hunting monks of the Peak. The priest said there were none here, and it was true of the robes. The flesh-houses I keep clear of. The waystones the abbot never crossed. "Walk round them, children. Something under there has held its breath longer than we have."

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
Novices were shaved on the stair, one step below their teacher, and the hair was let go off the edge on the wind. It came back. Every thaw brought hair down the water-cut, black and brown and grey, the whole order, years of it, caught in the reeds at the bottom. The lowland children braided it into rings and sold them to pilgrims for luck. I bought one. It's my colour.

**What did the abbot say when he put the letter in your hands?**
He folded it into my shirt himself. He said, "Go by the water-cut. Don't count the steps. If you count them, you'll owe someone the number." Then, "When they ask why we did not fight, say: a blow is a name shouted." That's in the letter too; he wanted me to have it by mouth. Then he opened my hand, laid nothing in it, shut it, and said, "Keep that for me." I have. I asked who would open the sanctum doors if the brothers below were slow. He said, "Someone always comes up first." I waited at the foot of the water-cut all that season. Nobody went up past me.

### Notes for the lore bible

### Places

- **Lower Stair** is the lowland parish town under the Peak: the Well of Two Steps, the lamp-seller's shop and the bread-woman's corner facing each other across the lane, the assize house over an empty lock-up, the washing-house behind the churchyard.

- **The beating-house** stood at the foot of the sanctum stair, and the bell-caster's pit and furnace at the foot of the mountain stair, where the clay is grey. The Peak's lay craftsmen now mend pots all over the valleys.

- **The old water-cut** is a steep meltwater channel down the mountain, the novices' quick way down. Every thaw it washes the order's shaved hair into the reeds at the bottom.

- **The east stone:** the abbot gave the bowls' names to the open hand at noon, over the lip of the east stone.

- **A waystone camp below the Knee** has craftsmen, a rope-seller and pilgrims' broth. Tapped, the fang-stones ring like a cracked bell that "keeps talking after".

### Beliefs and rites

- **The Noon of No Shadow** (midsummer) was the Peak's holiest day. The saints were re-laid the night before, and the monks sat open-eyed in the white fire of the sanctum.

- **Bowl Eve**, the lowland rite: the first dusk after the thaw, a loaf is set on every step of the lane. By morning the loaves are gone and nobody took them. It still happens with no monks.

- **The Shut Night** (the longest dark) is when the lowlands light every lamp they own. **The Ringing of the Great Bell** was the Peak's midwinter rite, after a month in which no lesser bell was rung.

- **The Night of Nines** is the Pale Order's night when every great ossuary counts at once. "It always comes out nine."

- **Novices were shaved on the stair**, one step below their teacher, and the hair was let go on the wind.

- **The teacher's leaf-book:** each novice gilder got a bark book holding one leaf beaten by her teacher as a novice. It was never to be opened.

- **The listening part:** a thimble of pilgrims' gold went into every Peak bell "so the bell could hear itself go quiet".

- **The survivor's rite:** on the tenth day of the thaw month, the washer opens and shuts her hand once for every brother she still remembers.

### Daily life (food, dress, work, sayings)

- **Food:** barley bread, pale-cap mushrooms grown in crocks under the stair, god-fat broth with roots, ash-baked pilgrim bread. The Pale Order eats grey marrow-bread and a broth of the pale roots that come through the gallery walls.

- **"Giving wrong change"** is the lowland saying for someone who has lost a name.

- **The Hand Book:** every lowland court secretly keeps a record with no names, only descriptions. "The only book that still works."

- **Novice dress:** a grey smock with no belt, worn until the order is sure of you.

- **Lay names:** "the Weight" was the Peak's kind name for a lay man, because he ate. The Pale Order says **"an early morning"** when a brother finishes telling his cord one knot short.

- **Gold keeps the last hand:** breathe on leaf and the whorl of the thumb that laid it shows.

### Things they disagree about

- **The empty niche.** The washer says the hundred-and-eighth niche was always empty, "kept". The knight-scribe found gold dust and footprints leading out of it. The goldbeater lost the count three times.

- **The dark tracks.** The goldbeater thought they were damp. The novices were told the saints wept for the living.

- **Who opened the sanctum doors.** The abbot ordered the first brother up to open them. The washer swears nobody went up past her.

- **Kept or let go.** The lamp-seller and the bread-woman want the lantern. The goldbeater wants the river. The bell-caster wants "one stroke". The washer wants her name given away first.

- **The stair count.** The knight-scribe's counts agree up and down. The lamp-seller's count going down "moves".

### Threads worth pulling

- **Master Enshō's fingers "after"**, which the goldbeater refuses to describe. The only thumbprint on Enshō's gilded mouth is the goldbeater's own.

- **The pinholes at the saints' mouths** (goldbeater): did he give some of them a way to breathe, or to finish?

- **The magistrate's nameless clerk**, who writes all night. The magistrate stands on his step at dusk, palm up, "waiting to be handed something back".

- **The knight-scribe's second shadow** on the stair, one step behind his own, which he counted for all the way down.

- **The shared breath at the pour** (bell-caster): the tongueless bell's syllable is "the breath you take for a word you never say".

- **The third cup** (bread-woman): each morning it is cold and short by the width of a lip.

- **The teacher's thumbprint** (lamp-seller): it is smaller than her own, though her teacher was a big man.

- **The abbot's words to the washer** ("Keep that for me", "Someone always comes up first"), and the part of the water-cut where she was not alone.

## Interviews: V. The Polished Heart

### The keeper of the ring

*Asked in the ring-yard of the empty lodge, at the grey hour. He sat on the keeper's stone and would not let the copyist sit in the Pir's place, which is only a worn patch of flags.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
I kept the circle. Laid the felt, lit the one candle, said the first name and the last. The ring called me Keeper, and the valley called me the Old Candle, because I was always the last light up at the lodge. My own name went round the ring so many times I stopped knowing whose mouth it left. Write me as the keeper. Write that there were forty-one of us. Write it as forty-one. Somebody should keep the count right, and I am the only one left who knows how.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
Here. The felt-loft over the lower door, where it is dry. It smells of ash and old candle and the damp in the flags. The light comes grey through the smoke-hole and leaves grey. I sleep on the rolled felt the ring knelt on; I can feel where the knees went. I eat what the upper valley leaves at the gate: flax-bread, a crock of root broth, the white cap-fungus off the north wall. Nobody lives next to me. At night the wind goes round the yard the way the names went round. Some nights it comes back short.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
I believe what I said at four hundred beds. The Crone draws you, sets you in the cloth, takes you out when your length is done, and draws you back through the Hollow to be woven again. I say it every morning. In the evening I say the Pir's words, because she paid for them. Both go in the same mouth. When I die, cover my glass. Or leave it. I have written both on a slate, and I will rub one out on the last day, if I am let.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Grey hour, the first name. Sweep the felt-lint out of the ring. Polish. One name to every stroke. The midday bread. Polish. Count the stones in the ring, forty-two, one for the Pir. Candle at dusk, the last name, sleep. The holiest day was the Folding, midwinter. The whole valley knit one grey cloth each year, a row for every death, and on the longest night the Pir folded it in nine and laid it on the Hollow-stone, and we sang it through. Where it went after, I was told. I was always told.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
I fear the count going wrong in my mouth. I fear the small one at the back of the glass, and I will not tell you whose face it wears, because I told you already and I would rather you forgot. I will not talk about the thirty-ninth night. I will not talk about who stopped saying the names. We were forty-one mouths, and all forty-one moved. I watched them. That is all I will say about that ring.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
Your Pale Order counts our bones and says we threw them away. We kept what mattered and left the rest to your brothers; you are welcome to it. The Brotherhood of the Wound feeds its dead to a wheel, and nothing is read off a wheel. The Breath-folk of the Eight Million say our glass holds a breath forever; a breath is what their lot care about. The Silence's servants show nothing in glass. We tested every stranger at the lower door with the hand-mirror. The stone rings I will not walk. At a ring the thread goes dark. The Crone allows it. That was always said to me in exactly those words.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
The felt for the glass was always beaten from flax-tow, and the tow was always from the Lower Reach, because the Reach women spun the finest. Every mirror in this lodge was covered with the Reach's own tow for three hundred years. When the Reach came loose, it came loose all at once, every dyer and child between one frost and the next. I have not put those two things side by side before today. Take them apart again before you leave.

**You said the names came back short. Whose mouth had stopped?**
There. You would ask that. When a name goes round a ring it passes through forty-one mouths and comes back the length it went. That winter it came back the length of forty. I counted mouths. Forty-one. I counted breaths, which a keeper can. Forty-one. So I counted faces in the ring, by the candle, one by one, and I got forty-two. I began again. Forty-one. I have never since trusted a count taken by candle. Ask the grinder how many faces his glass holds. Then ask him to stop.

### The grinder of the lodge valley

*Asked at his wheel in the grinding shed below the furnace road. He did not stop working, and he asked that the copyist stand where the test-glass could not see him.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Grinder. My father was Grinder, and his. The valley calls me Round-Hand, because a man who has ground four hundred rounds holds his cup as if he were checking it for seeds. The lodge wrote me on their slates as "the wheel below the ford." I like that best. A wheel does what it is paid to do and says the names it is told to say. Pitch, rouge, Spinner. You get used to it. I say them now when I sharpen a knife.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
The shed, and a loft over it. Everything is grit. Grit in the bread, grit in the bed-straw, grit in the seams of my hands so the lines never come clean. Smells of wet stone and pitch and the sour of quicksilver, which you learn not to breathe over. The glass comes up from Greenkiln on the furnace road, packed in bracken. I eat barley, onions, a flax-seed cake fried on the stone. The fuller lives below me; I hear his mallets all day and the river all night. The river is the only thing in the valley that has never asked me for a mirror.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
I believe in grit, coarse to fine. I believe the lodge folk, because they had looked and I had only ground. A soul halts at its own face. That I have seen my own shop prove, one way or another. The god I leave to them as can bear it. When I die, the fuller has my word he will turn my last round face down and set my hand on its back. I want the glass to feel whose it was.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Wake. Wet the stone. Grind till the thumb finds nothing. Silvering days are quiet days; you do not sneeze on a nine-day glass. Eat at the wheel. Make backs now, mostly: hinged boards, felt lids, frosted panes for carters. My holiest day was Slate Day, first thaw, when the lodge's runner came down with the spring order. Every year more rounds than the year before, and the lodge the same size. We drank to it all the same. There has been no slate for three springs, and I still wet the stone that morning and wait.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
I fear the round I finished last. It is under a cloth in the loft, on its face, and the cloth moves. I fear the day someone brings back my heart-glass and I have to weigh it in my hand and say yes. I will not talk about the quicksilver. My father's hands shook at the end, and I will not talk about his hands either. Ask about the grit. Grit is honest.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The Pale ones buy nothing but tally everything; one of yours counted my rounds on the shelf and got a different number each pass, and blamed my shelf. The Brotherhood of the Wound ordered a glass from me once, deep hollowed, to hang over a basin. I would not ask what for. The Breath-priests want polished bronze on their shrines, never glass; they say glass holds too hard. The stone rings: I went through one, young, to fetch a furnace-man's daughter home. I came out on the far side with every grain of grit gone from my hands. My father looked at them and wept. They were clean for a month.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
The best rouge for the last polish is the red ash from under a lantern-stone, where the old wick-ash falls. It cuts finer than any furnace red. The lodge knew and never said. I knew and charged them for rouge anyway. Every mirror on that coat was finished with a little of someone who chose to stay. I only thought about that this winter.

**The man in the grey hood, who gave nothing to your test-glass: did he come back?**
Once. At midsummer, noon, the shed door open and the sun square on the floor. He stood in the sun and cast his shadow on the grit like any man, and I thought, good, I was tired that night, I saw wrong. He asked if the glass was still spoken for. I said yes. He said, "It will be." Then he walked off up toward the lodge. I followed to the ford and no further. Since then I keep the test-glass turned to the wall, like any widow. It shames me. My father never turned a glass in his life.

### The widow of the upper valleys

*Asked on the bench by her door, with the uncovered glass on the sill between the copyist and the lane. She kept her eyes on the lane throughout.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Wenna. Oswy's wife, and I will be Oswy's wife till I am put in the barrow next to him under the same kind of lid. The lane calls me the Sill Widow now, because of the glass, and some of them mean it kindly. My sister calls me a fool to my face and brings me bread twice a week, so I know which she means. I count loaves for the bakehouse at the top of the lane. I was counting loaves before I could read.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
Two rooms and a loft, stone below, flax-thatch above. It smells of the bakehouse up the lane and of the barley-straw in the mattress, which wants changing. Cold light, because I took the felt off the window too. I eat the bakehouse ends, root broth, a pot of fungus from the barrow-banks; the ones that grow on the barrows are the fattest, and nobody asks why. My sister is two doors down. The Ruddocks next door keep their glass to the wall and cough when they pass mine. At night I hear the lane. And sometimes, low, a string in a wind that is not blowing.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
My mother said we go through the eye of something and come out new cloth. The lodge said it. I said it to the children in the lane. I believed it the way you believe bread will rise. Then the dead came home asking which way, so nobody was going anywhere, was they. Now I believe you go out and you look for your door. I would like there to be a face at my door that knows me. I have put one there for Oswy. That is the whole of what I believe.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Up before the bakehouse. Wipe the sill-glass with the hem of my apron, one wipe, and say his name once. Count loaves, forty to a board, forty-one if the baker is feeling kind. Home, broth, the roof, which leaks. Holiest day was Oswy's Stitching, when the Needle-Brother came, though I did not know it for holy till after. The old holy day was the Folding. I knitted Oswy's row into the valley cloth that winter. His was the last row. The cloth was never folded. I don't know where it went.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
I fear the sill-glass cracking in a frost. I fear I will be asleep when he comes. I fear, if I am honest, that he came already, and the thing in the glass stopped him, and he is stood there still in the lane, looking, and I walk through him to the bakehouse every morning. I will not talk about the window he turned his face to. My sister says hold your tongue, and on that I hold it.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
Your brothers came for Oswy's bones and I sent your man home with a heel of bread and no bones, and I will do it again. What would you count him for? The Brotherhood people came through once with a cart, asking after fresh dead. The whole lane shut its doors. The stone rings I stay out of. A cousin went through the one on the high road and came back, and her hands were the same hands, and she was different. She stopped looking in glass at all. She said it made her dizzy, like looking down a well.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
When you knit for the dead in this valley you knit the row with no knots. You cast on a length of flax long enough for the whole row, so there is nothing to tie in the middle. A knot in the dead's row catches. Everybody knows that. I knitted Oswy's with a knot in it anyway, near the end, on purpose, where his hand would be. I wanted something to catch.

**When the wisp stopped at your voice and turned to the coat, what do you think it saw?**
I have lain awake on that. The Needle-Brother's coat is all little round glass, and the wisp looked at the glass, and it would have seen itself, and I suppose me behind it, small, in the lane. I think it saw the two of us. I think that is what it was looking at so long. I would like to think that. My sister says it saw a bright thing and stopped like a child does. She may be right. She has been right about most things. She has never been right about Oswy.

### Mother Ysolt, reader of threads

*Asked through the half-door of a flax-store in the upper valley, where she keeps. She would not open the lower half. Her former apprentice sat on the step and knitted, and now and then corrected her.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Ysolt. Mother, to the lodge, because I read at the lower door forty years and every frightened thing that came to it called me Mother by the end. Reader, to the valley. The girl there on the step calls me "the old glass" when she thinks I cannot hear. I can hear. I was the one they sent when the Pir was tired, and the one they sent when the reading was bad. I read for coin. The coin is so nobody ever says the Crone told me for love.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
A flax-store. Bundles to the roof, so it smells green and dry, and dust off the bolls gets in everything. The light is what comes over the half-door. I sleep on the retted flax, which is softer than you would think and full of little stalks. The girl brings broth, and bread with seed in it, and on feast days a dish of the yellow shelf-fungus done in god-fat. Next door is the tow-yard, and all night I hear the tow settle in the bales, a soft sound, like somebody turning over in a bed.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
I read four thousand threads and I never once saw the eye of the loom. I saw threads go down to it. I saw the Unraveller's hand. After that my glass fogged, every time, and I said what the lodge said, because the Pir had seen further. Then the Pir saw further. So. I believe the Crone is gentle with the ones who hold still. I intend to hold very still. The girl will tell you I said the opposite last week. The girl knits and hears half.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Sleep past the grey hour now, which the lodge would have beaten me for. Bread. Sit. People still come up the valley to the half-door, though I have no glass. I read their hands instead, in the plain daylight, and tell them pleasant things, true ones, small. The holiest day was the Casting-On in spring, when every child born that winter was brought to the lodge and a thread cast on for it on the glass needles, the length of its arm. We kept them on pegs in the Felt-Loft. There were three hundred pegs. I do not know who has them now.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
I will not talk about the stranger with ash on both knees. Put your pen down while I say that. There. I fear being asked to read again. I fear the knot. Every thread I read began clean off the Crone's fingers, and one did not, and since that night I look at my own left palm before I sleep, in case. Also I fear the girl's knitting. She drops stitches and does not go back for them.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The Pale Order reads bone the way we read thread, and they are more patient, and wrong. The Wound-brothers read nothing; they only feed. I read once for a Breath-priest of the Eight Million, and his thread was full of little holes, like a cloth left by a window a long time, and he thanked me. At the stone rings the thread goes dark and takes up again a way off. Most fray a little. I told one they came out heavier. I should not have said that. Heavy is a word for stones.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
A reader always warms the glass before she turns it, with the heel of her hand, the length of the names said once through. Cold glass shows a thread too long. Warm glass shows it the right length. I was taught that in my first year and never told why. I have wondered, since the winter, which one showed it true.

**What was the word the stranger said, the name of the Crone that you had never heard?**
The girl told you that. Girl, you will go without supper. [Here the apprentice says: "She said it was one of the Crone's names."] It is one of the Crone's names. It was hers before the others. It is the name you would call a knot, if a knot could be called, before anything was tied in it. That is all. If I said it here the flax would hear, and flax goes into felt, and felt goes over glass, and the glass is in every house in this valley. Eat your bread. Go on.

### The mender of the lining

*The Pir is dead. These answers are from the woman who kept her pages: the lodge's seamstress, who sewed the Pir's Coat its lining each spring. Asked at the ford inn, by the chalked window, while she knitted a grey cord and never looked at it.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Hedda. I sewed the lining of the Coat, spring and autumn, thirty-one years, and three Pirs' worth of mending before that in my mother's hands. The lodge called me the Lining. "Send for the Lining." That was me. The valley calls me the Mender, and brings me their torn things, and I mend them, and I do not look in their glass while I am at it. I kept her pages. I gave them to your people because your people were the only ones who would carry them out of this valley.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
A room over the ford inn now, with the ale-smell coming up the boards and the carters shouting under the window. It suits me. At the lodge I had the sewing-room behind the Coat-Post, all felt and thread-ends and the one high window, and it smelled of the ash-wax on the backs of the glass. Here it smells of barley and wet coats. I eat what the inn cooks, a thick root pottage, flax-bread, and on the Folding night they still bake the ring-loaf out of habit, forty-one twists, and nobody eats the middle.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
I believe what she wrote. I sewed her words inside out, where they would lie against her, and I have read them over more times than I have said the Crone's names. There is no loom. It goes on. I believe, too, that a thread wants a hand at the end of it. That is a seamstress speaking and a Pir would slap me for it. When I die, somebody sew my hem to something. A doorpost. Anything that stays.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
I knit before I get up, in the bed, a length of grey cord, a hand's length every morning. I have knit a hand every morning since she died. Then mending: hems, cuffs, a bride-cloth, the carters' hoods. The holiest day was the Relining in spring, when the Coat came off its post and lay across my table like a sleeper, and the Pir and I went over every mirror together, one by one, to see none were loose. She pressed her thumb in the wax of any I had re-sewn. It was the only day she laughed.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
I fear the Coat. I fear it as a mother fears a son who has grown too tall to hold. I will not talk about the sleeve. The low one, on the left, where nothing comes back. I sewed that glass on myself, twenty years ago, from a round the lodge never ordered. It came up with the spring glass and it had no slate for it and the Pir said sew it on anyway. I will not talk about that.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
Your Pale brothers took the lodge's bones up the Ossa road in carts, with every bone tagged, and never asked for so much as a thread. That is what you are. The Eight Million sent a priest to tell us to open our glass and let the dead breathe out. The Pir fed him and sent him home. The Wound-house asked to buy the Coat once. For the iron. The stone rings: the Pir went into one once, to the Barrens and back, and when she came home I had to take in the lining a finger's width. She had come back smaller, she said. I said she had come back lighter. We did not agree.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
Every Pir sewed something of her own into the lining. A tooth, a lock of hair, a letter to the next one. When I unpicked the lining after, I found a knot of hair from the first Pir, grey, tied round a needle. Every Pir had found it and none of them had moved it. There were more things in that lining than there had been Pirs. I have counted the Pirs' names on the lodge wall twice. I cannot make them come to the right number.

**How did you come to unpick the lining, when the Coat had already walked out of the lodge?**
It came back. The first thaw after. I was in the yard at night burning felt and it came in by the lower gate, tall as the eaves, with the Needle-Brother behind it holding his lamp in the air, and it knelt at the Coat-Post. The heart was burning. The lining was torn at the breast, right through, where the great glass had been. He said, "You are the Lining." I sewed it in the yard, on my knees, on its knees, and my fingers found her pages. He saw them. He said, "Hers." That was all. He let me keep them.

### The flax-beater at the ford

*Asked at the bench outside the ford inn, with the ale bought first. The window behind him is chalked from inside; someone has written a small word in the chalk with a finger, and scrubbed it out.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Tam. I beat flax. You take the retted stalks and you beat them on the block with a scutch till the straw falls off and the fibre's left, and you do it all day, all autumn. The valley calls me Scutch, because of the sound. Everyone in this valley was something to the lodge, and I was the tow. The best of what I beat went up the hill for their felt. I covered their glass with my own arm's work, in a manner of speaking. Buy another and I'll tell you what that's worth.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
The beating-sheds by the ford, and the house behind them, which is my house and was Hild's. Dust. Flax-dust in the throat, in the bread, in the bed; you cough it up in the morning like a little grey ghost. Light's poor. I chalked the windows myself. I eat flax-seed porridge, turnips, a spoon of god-fat off the inn's pot when I've the coin, and the sweet brown fungus that comes up where the ford floods. My neighbour's the fuller, who beats cloth, so between us you'd think we were at war. At night I hear the ford. Since the winter it sounds like people talking low.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
I believed the lodge because I paid them, and a man likes to think he's bought something true. Now? I think you go out asking which way, and whoever's nearest answers. So I keep my door chalked and my mouth shut, because I've no answer, and I'll not be the one to say "up" again. Once is plenty. The god I never had much to do with. It's the Crone who comes into your house. The god's more what's under the ford.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Beat flax. Eat. Beat flax. Sit here. The holiest was the Folding, midwinter, and I'll tell you what we did, since nobody does now. Every house sent its rows up for the valley cloth. Longest night, the whole valley walked up with candles, and the lodge sang, and the Pir folded the cloth and laid it on the stone, and we came down in the dark not looking at the ice. My boy carried the candle his last Folding. Held it with both hands. Now the holiest day is any day my daughter nods at me in the lane.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
The coat. I told your last man I'd say no more of it and I'll say no more of it. Ask the Mender. I fear ice. Every winter the ford freezes and I'm out with the shovel before light throwing ash on it, and every winter somebody from my daughter's half comes and sweeps it clear again, so the dead can see. We don't speak of it. I fear my boy stopped in front of her mirror. Hers. Mine was chalk. There. You got it out of me. Drink up.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
Your lot came for the barrows the spring after and took the old bones, the faceless ones, and left the new. Said the old ones were "closer to the count." Fine. They weren't asking to be carried. The Wound-brothers stay off this road. The priests of the Eight Million came up and said the valley's breath had gone sour and rang a bell at the ford. Didn't help. The stone ring on the high road I've never gone in. My grandmother, the blind one, walked straight past it that winter, they say. Didn't stop there either.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
Flax that's beaten on a death-day won't spin. Everyone knows it. It snaps on the wheel every arm's length, and the spinner curses you. So the day someone's died, the whole of the sheds stops, and we sit on the blocks with the scutches across our knees. That winter every shed stopped at once, all the way down the valley, and we sat, and nobody said anything, and you could hear the ford all the way from the top of the hill.

**Where do you think your grandmother is going?**
Somewhere that'll answer her. She was blind, so she never had her face to stop at, living or dead. She walked by feel her whole life, one hand on the wall. The rest of them stopped at the coat because they could see. She couldn't, so she's still asking, still walking, up past the lodge where the valley narrows and the old road goes over into country nobody from here has any business in. I'd say she's the only one of the lot of them still going the way the lodge promised. That's a thing to sit with, isn't it. Another?

### Notes for the lore bible

### Places

- **The ring-yard** of the empty lodge: forty-two worn stones (forty-one sitters and the Pir's patch), the keeper's stone, the Felt-Loft over the lower door, the Coat-Post, the sewing-room behind it, and the **Hollow-stone** where the year's cloth was laid.

- **Greenkiln**, the furnace-town on the furnace road that supplies the valley's green glass, packed in bracken.

- **The ford and the ford inn**: the heart of the valley below the lodge; beating-sheds, fuller, grinding shed and inn stand along it. Its ice is ashed by one half of the valley and swept clear by the other each winter.

- **The Felt-Loft pegs**: three hundred pegs holding the cast-on threads of every child the lodge ever blessed. Their whereabouts are unknown since the lodge emptied (a quest or a lore object).

- Fungus that grows fattest on the **barrow-banks**; the sweet brown fungus of the flood-line at the ford.

### Beliefs and rites

- **The Folding**: midwinter. Each house knits one row per death into a single grey valley cloth; on the longest night the Pir folded it in nine and laid it on the Hollow-stone. The last cloth, with Oswy's row as its final row, was never folded and is missing.

- **The Casting-On**: spring. Every infant's thread was cast on glass needles, an arm's length long, and hung in the Felt-Loft.

- **The Relining**: spring and autumn, the Coat taken off its post and laid on the seamstress's table; the Pir thumbed the wax of every re-sewn glass.

- The Hollow's name is said with the mouth shut, a hum. (The widow's Oswy was stitched at the throat "where he used to hum.")

- Knitting for the dead: a death-row must have **no knot**, cast on one unbroken length, or the soul catches. The widow knotted hers on purpose.

- Readers **warm the glass** with the heel of the hand before turning it; cold glass shows a thread too long.

- Every Pir sewed a keepsake into the Coat's lining for the next.

### Daily life (food, dress, work, sayings)

- Valley food: flax-bread, flax-seed porridge and cakes, root broth and pottage, barley, turnips, onions, cap-fungus, shelf-fungus done in god-fat.

- The **thumb-loaf**: bread with a thumb pressed in the middle, given to someone leaving whom you want kept; the Pir's thumb in the mirror-wax is the same mark.

- The **ring-loaf** of forty-one twists baked on Folding night; nobody eats the middle.

- The lodge's felt was beaten from **Lower Reach flax-tow**; the lodge's knitting needles were glass.

- Flax beaten on a death-day will not spin: every shed stops, scutches across the knees.

- By-names: the Old Candle (the keeper), Round-Hand (the grinder), the Sill Widow, the Lining / the Mender, Scutch.

- Mirror-backs are now the grinder's main trade: hinged boards, felt lids, sand-frosted panes "that show nothing."

### Things they disagree about

- Whether the Needle-Brother stole the dead or saved them splits families: the flax-beater chalks his window, his daughter keeps a mirror at her door, and they no longer eat together.

- Whether the Pir came back from a waystone "smaller" (her own word) or "lighter" (the Mender's).

- Whether the Hollow Mystic's wisps look into the coat's mirrors from recognition (the widow) or like a child at a bright thing (her sister).

- The other orders: the Eight Million want the valley's glass open "so the dead can breathe out"; the Pale Order took only the oldest, faceless barrow-bones, "closer to the count."

- Ysolt says she believes the Crone is gentle with the still; her apprentice says she said the opposite last week.

### Threads worth pulling

- **The forty-second face / mouth**: the keeper counts forty-two in the ring by candlelight and forty-one by breath (keeper; echoes the Pir's pages).

- **The un-ordered glass** on the Coat's left sleeve, the one that reflects nothing: it arrived with no slate, the Pir had it sewn on anyway (the Mender). Tie to the grey-hood man and the servants of the Silence.

- **The grey-hood man**: casts a shadow at noon, shows nothing in glass, says of the grinder's last round, "It will be" (the grinder).

- **The Crone's first name**, the one the stranger spoke: "the name you would call a knot before anything was tied in it" (Ysolt). Hints at the hero's knotted thread and something before the Spinner.

- **The Lower Reach tow**: every covering-cloth in the lodge was made from the Reach's flax, and the Reach came loose all at once (keeper, who asks the copyist to "take them apart again").

- **The lining held more keepsakes than there were Pirs**, and the Pirs' names on the lodge wall will not come to the right count (the Mender). A quiet hint of counts that rise.

- **Lantern-stone ash as rouge**: every mirror on the Coat was polished with Wickbound ash (the grinder).

- **The blind grandmother** still walking uphill past the lodge "the way the lodge promised" (the flax-beater): the one dead soul that never stopped.

- **Waystones clean the hands**: the grinder came out of a stone ring with all his grit gone; the widow's cousin came back unable to bear glass.

## Interviews: VI. The Precious Wound

### The one who was away

*Heard in his room on the lower lip of Villa Llaga, on the eleventh night after his return. He would not light a candle, and he held a plate of black crust in his lap the whole time.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Tobal. Twenty-ninth hood. In the Brotherhood you are your place in the procession, and you move up one when a brother goes into the wall. I was thirty-fourth when I took the robe. The children called every one of us the Tall Red Hat. The stilt-folk at Kettlewick called me the cup-carrier, and cheated me on the ferry. Now there is nobody to call me anything. I say my number to the room in the mornings so that someone has.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
The lower lip. Forty rooms cut into the edge of the wound, each with a door onto the gutter-walk and a peg by the door. The stone is warm under the floor all year. We baked our lip-bread on it, flat loaves laid straight on the ledge, and you could tell a brother's bread by the pattern of the stone on its bottom. It smells of iron and wet bread here. It always did. At night the wound drinks, a small sound, like a man sipping broth he has been told is too hot. I sleep on the floor now. The pallet smells of the others.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
She has three faces and we sang them in their order. The Maiden opens. The Mother makes. The Nurse feeds. A brother who dies goes into our own wall, with his robe on the peg beside him, and what he gave in his life is in her already, carrying his name. That was the teaching. You give your measure and you say what it is for, and so the blood goes in with your name on it, and one day she hands it back to you as a saint coming down the stair. I have given twenty-two measures. I would like to know where they went.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
I beat cups. That was my trade, tin cups for the tithe, on the anvil in the chapter yard. Water from the upper well at first light, the walk along the lip at the sway, the stop, the sway, then the hammer until dark. The holiest day is the Day of the Measure. The Elder cuts the new line on the eve. In the morning we kneel on the lip in the old order, eldest first, and pour slowly from lip to lip, and say the Words. When I was a boy the line was a finger below the rim. My last cup, I beat the rim higher so the line would fit.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
The robes. I told you they were gone from the pegs, all but mine. I fear that mine is waiting for a shape. And I will not talk about the Friday stone. The Rule says each brother kisses the rough place and says nothing of what he saw there. I kept that for thirty years. I will keep it for her, whoever she is now. I will tell you only that it was warm the last time, and it has never been warm before.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The Pale Order may come and count. There is nothing here for them. The lodges of the Polished Heart used to send a man every spring to ask about our saints, very politely, the way a thief asks the hour. The Shrine Keepers came up the lower road once to close the wound, and the brothers met them on the lip with scourges bare. I was proud that day. I am less proud. The waystones we never used. You come apart in the pit, into blood, before the day of the Measure, with no Words over it. Spilled blood. I walked home. Nine weeks.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
After the pouring we wiped our cups with a heel of lip-bread and ate the bread. Every brother, every year. That was the last of the measure, and it went back into us, so that a little of each tithe stayed in the Brotherhood. Nobody licked the cups. It was thought low. When I came home the cups on the lip were licked. I could see the marks of it in the tin. Many small tongues. Tell the scholars that.

**You keep a plate of the crust against your chest. What does it do?**
It beats. Slowly. Slower than I do. The first nights I lay with it and my heart went on at its own pace, and I could feel the two of them walk out of step, like two brothers who have lost the song. By the sixth night they were closer. Last night I woke and could find only one beat, and I could not tell you whose. I am ashamed of how easy that was. Put it down, you say. I have tried. My hand comes back for it.

### The keeper of the chained book, who was counted down

*Heard at the chapter door of Villa Llaga, where he sat with the chain of the Rule still round his wrist. He answered some questions twice, and the second answer was always shorter.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Brother Gaudel. I kept the Rule. I mean the book: the chain goes from the lectern to my wrist, and has for twenty-six years. Before that I was eleventh hood. One night on the crypt stair the counts differed, the count going down and the count coming up, and the Elder said which was right, as the twentieth clause gives him to say. The right count did not have me in it. So I am a brother in the wall, with my robe on the peg. The dead keep the book. It is a quiet office.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
The chapter room, at the top of the crypt stair. Long table, stone lectern, forty stools, the book. There is one high window onto the wound and the light comes in red at noon from the gutters, or came. The brothers left my bowl at the door, lip-bread and bean broth, and did not look at me, since I was dead. I slept under the lectern on a hemp sack, because the chain reaches that far and no farther. At night I heard the brothers go down two by two with their candles. I counted them. It is a habit of the dead.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
I believe the Rule. Everything I believe is written in it, in the old hand, and I have read it every day for twenty-six years. She is given her measure; she is given the Words; the procession has a place to go. Those three things together make a gift. Any one alone makes something else. As for dying, I have done it once already, and I can tell you it changes very little. You answer to more names.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
I oil the chain. I turn the leaves so the damp does not take them. I read one clause aloud to the room, in order, and when I reach the twentieth I begin again. Twenty days, then round. The holiest is the eve of the Measure. The Elder came up with his small knife and I held the lamp while he cut the new line on the pattern cup. A thumbnail higher. Always. The cups were beaten taller four times in my years. The last time, I noticed that the Elder's hand did not shake. He was pleased.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
I fear the brown ink. It is in the book now, on my leaves, in a hand that leans the wrong way. I was chained to the lectern the whole of that year. I did not see who wrote it. I have been asked. I will say again I did not see. And I will not speak the fourth clause. It was kept in the Elder's mouth. It is in no mouth now. You see my lips moving. I am reading. It is a habit of the dead.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The eighteenth clause names the Pale Order, so I have said their name more than any other: no brother of the Pale shall enter the crypt nor count what lies there. They have asked. They say our wall holds more than we laid in it. We say that is our business. The Shrine Keepers are welcome at the chapter door and no farther. The waystones break the ninth clause. A procession goes out from a door and comes home to the same door. A stone that sends you out one door and in another has given you a road with no home at the end of it. That is exactly what the brown leaves allow now.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
Every Elder in the book lisped. All of them. You can hear it in the way the old ones wrote the Words out, spelled as spoken. We said it came from holding the fourth clause, that it sat on the tongue like a pebble and bent the speech round it. The last Elder lisped worst of all. On the eve of his last Measure he spoke to me very clearly, for the first time in twenty years, and I understood then that his mouth was empty.

**The twenty-seventh clause says there is one Elder. Who is it?**
The Rule says the Elder is the eldest living brother, and walks at the front. Read the brown leaf. The Words are spoken at the front. There is room. A brother who comes home shall find his robe waiting. I read these to the room every twenty days, in order, the old leaves and the new. Last month when I came to the twenty-seventh, something answered from the crypt stair, kindly, by my name. The Rule says a brother shall answer to any name spoken to him when the saints are down. I did not answer. That is the first clause I have broken.

### The priest of the Laden Board

*Heard at the long table of the House of the Laden Board, north of the Moor, on the second night of the Feast of Second Helpings. He would not begin until the questioner had eaten.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Father Brunel, Server of the Second Ladle. The Board has seven servers, one for each ladle, and I have the second, which is the ladle of the poor, the thick one from the bottom of the pot. The lowland towns call us the fat priests, and they are right, and they say it with their mouths full. My sweet ones call me Father. The given call me nothing. They hum.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
A long house, low, turf on the roof and a floor that is always warm, because we built it over a seam. You know the seams: god-fat, yellow and soft, running under the Moor where the flesh has cooled. We cut it with wooden spades and render it in the pans and it lights the lamps, greases the bread, fills the pies. Everything here shines a little at the lip. We sleep on the floor in rows, wrapped, like loaves proving. Under the floor are the pens. At night you hear the given breathe, and above them the pans ticking as they cool.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
She is the part of the god that did not stop. That is all of theology. The Soul frets, the Bone counts, the Breath wanders off. She eats. When one of the Board dies we lay him in the warm floor under the long table, in his apron, and by the spring the floor has him, and we eat over him at the Feast, and he is at the table. Where else would you want to be? I have never understood the wish to be kept. Kept for what?

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Before light, the pans. Then the pens: bread and broth down the stair, the Nurse's cup down empty. The given eat well, better than I did as a boy in Hallow Ferry. Then the kitchen until noon, the bargaining with the bone-men after, prayers at dusk, which are supper. The holiest is the Feast of Second Helpings, three nights at the thaw. Every dish is served twice. You must take it the second time, even if you are sick with it. Refusal is the sin, you understand. On the third night we set a place at the head of the board with a full plate, and nobody sits there, and in the morning the plate is clean.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
A thin year. A cracked pan. The day a server finishes his plate. And I fear a mother who has been told what to make. I have said so from the board, and I will say it at every feast until they lay me in the floor. As for what I will not discuss: where the given come from. There are camps in the lowlands. There are debts. There are families with too many mouths and a winter coming. We pay fairly. Eat your pie.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The Pale come too early to the meal. The Polished Heart keeps souls in dishes, and a soul has no fat on it at all; I have never seen a thinner-looking order. The Shrine Keepers go about stopping her mouths with stakes and paper, as if she would thank them. And the few who serve the Hush. Those leave an empty plate, and an empty plate is the only thing I would kill a man for. The waystones I love. She takes a little mouthful of every pilgrim going through the pit and hands the rest back to be walked on. A courteous table.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
A server of the Board must leave a crust. Every plate, every meal, all his life. A corner of bread, a spoon of broth. The day you clean your plate is the day she learns you are full, and a thing that is full is a thing that is finished. We are taught it before our letters. I have seen an old server weep on his deathbed because they spooned the last of his broth into him and he could not keep his lips shut on it.

**The Pale Order's clerk wrote that you laughed when he named his count of bones. Why?**
Because he was so sure. Every year the Pale send a man with notched sticks to buy what is left on our plates, and every year the count goes up, and every year they write the number in their books as if we had sold it to them. My clerk keeps a count too. We never sent them half. I told him so. He went white, and asked whether we had other suppliers. I told him to eat something. Someone is giving them bones, my dear. I only say it was never us.

### Asheth, leech-wife of Kettlewick

*Heard on her stilt-house in Kettlewick while she changed the water in her jars. She answered only between beats, and made the questioner wait through the clutch of the mud before each reply.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Asheth. Ash, eth: two, on the beat, the way every Kettlewick name is made. A leech is what you call the one who lets blood for a living. That is all the word ever meant. Pilgrims hear it and think of the jar, and look at the little mouths and ask their name. They have none. They are hers, small pieces of the mire that know the taste of a vein. The children call me Mother Jar. The Brotherhood calls me a thief. Lugh calls me late for supper.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
On stilts, like everyone, over the Heartbeat Flats. Reed walls, reed roof, a floor of split poles you can see the mud through. It smells of fen-fat lamps and wet reeds and warm iron. We eat reed-root and sump-grass bread, and black mire-cap in broth, and on beat-days a hard cake you break on your knee. We sleep in hammocks hung from the rafters, so the beat cannot come up the stilts into the sleeper. Children who sleep on the floor walk in their sleep, toward the Delta. At night you hear the mud, and past it, the banks.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
The mire is hers. The mud, the reeds, the lamp-fat, the little mouths, me, now that I have eaten her bread for sixty years. When a Kettlewick soul dies we carry them to the edge of the stilts and wait for the beat, and on the beat we let them go, and say what they are for. The mud takes them in one clutch. The ones who are let go off the beat come up again, and have to be let go again, and nobody likes to talk about it.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Wake before the lamps. Count the beat. Change the jar-water, count the mouths. The pilgrims come up the ladder with the marsh fever, and I bleed them, and pour it off the edge with the words, and they pay Lugh. That is the day. The holy night was the Long Beat, once in the year, at midwinter, when the mud stopped between one clutch and the next for the length of a whole breath, and the whole of Kettlewick held its breath with it. There has been no Long Beat since the year your town went dark.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
I fear the one who is learning. I fear the night the song comes up the mire with the words in it. And I will not tell you the fourth clause, pilgrim, however much you pay Lugh. Every leech-wife speaks it into every pour, and it works because it is spoken, and it would stop working in a book. It is short. Shorter than your whole Rule. I will say this much: your Elders kept it in the mouth, and we keep it in the pour, and one of us is still here.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The Brotherhood sings well and pays in coin. The fattening houses feed her in silence, and that is how you get children with no head: I have told their priests so, and they gave me a pie. The Shrine Keepers come down to stake her mouths shut, and the mud eats their stakes by morning. The Pale poles out for bones and finds almost none; the mire keeps little. Our waystone stands on the causeway. Step in on the beat, never between. Those who step in between come out at the other stone a little bled, and do not know where it went.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
Kettlewick girls learn the beat before their names, as I said. What nobody writes is how. The mother lays the baby on the floor-poles, over the mud, and lets it cry, and it cries in time. Every Kettlewick baby cries on the beat by its third week. Ours do not wake in the night; they wake on the clutch. When a girl from here marries up the fen-road, her first child cries all wrong, and she weeps with it, and does not know why.

**You said the count in your jar changes while you sleep. Does it go up or down?**
Up, mostly. Forty at night, forty-one in the morning, and I let it, and pour one off. That is the fee the mire takes for lending them. Once it went down. The night the hoods came through, I counted forty before I pulled the ladder, and in the morning there were none. The jar was full of clear water and the lid was on. I bought forty more from the sumps, as is right. They know me. They took to the arm as sweet as before. I have not trusted them since.

### The carver of the upper lip

*Heard in his shop at the top of the upper lip of Villa Llaga, on the last evening he kept it, among shavings. He went on working a small block while he spoke, with his back to it.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Orrán, carver of images. Master Orrán, if you are paying. The Brotherhood called me the saint-maker, which I never liked; I make the houses and the saints come down into them. My boy called me Master in the shop and Da at the table. I have made every face you saw on a Friday procession for thirty-one years, and one face I did not make.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
The top of the upper lip, where the street runs out and the wind comes over from the Hollow Wood. Two rooms: the shop and the bin. The shop smells of shavings and root-glue and oil for the edges. I sleep in the bin, on shavings, as carvers do; they are warm and they tell you by the smell what you cut that day. Bread and cold bean broth. My boy used to bring it up. Next door was the cup-beater's yard, and at night you heard the tin cooling and ticking. The yard is quiet now.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
I believe a thing wants a body. A saint coming down the stair at night wants somewhere to stand, and the brothers go down with candles to meet it, and it stands in the figure I made, and they talk. That is my trade: rooms for the ones who come down. When I die I go into the brothers' wall. That was the pay. I will stand in the wall and wait for someone to make me a room.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Edges first. A dull gouge tears the grain and a torn grain holds the wet. Then the block of the day, and the boy on the drawings, and bread at noon on the step. The holiest day for a carver is the Raising, the Friday a new image goes up. You walk behind her in the procession with your tools in a cloth, in case she chips on the stair. I walked behind the niche Maiden at her Raising with my boy beside me, and she did not chip. She has never chipped. I checked her often.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
I fear a finished face. And I will not talk about my boy. You have read the account. You have seen that the face on her was cut with a gouge the width of mine, with the grain, the way I taught him. Ask me something else. Ask me about timber. I can talk about timber until the lamp is out.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The Pale Order buys my offcuts for their niche-pegs. Pale straight wood, they like, and they do not ask why it was cheap. The Shrine Keepers are the only other trade I respect: small shrines, tight joints, no nails, a roof on each. The fattening houses wanted a Mother for their board, fat, with her mouth open. I sent them back their silver. The waystones I went to look at once, as a craftsman. There are no tool marks on them. None. And the grain of the stone runs round the ring, the whole way round, as if it had been one piece and bent.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
You carve a saint's eyes last, at night, by one candle, with your own eyes shut. The hand knows where they go. If you watch yourself cut them, the saint comes down into the figure and finds the carver's eyes in its face, looking back at the carver, and it will not stay. Every carver in the upper lip knew that. The niche Maiden has no eyes at all. She was the easiest thing I ever made.

**What went into the hollow of her back, before the Elder sealed it?**
I was sent out of the room. I sat on the step and did not listen, and I heard a small sound through the door, like someone turning over in their sleep. Then a long while of nothing. Then the Elder called me in and the board was on and his ring was on the wax. He was washing his hands in a basin. Water from the upper well; I knew the jar. The Rule keeps that water for one thing. I did not ask him what he had spilled.

### The child from the drain

*Heard in a dyers' yard in the lowland town of Kesk, where she wrings red cloth for her living. She would not stand on the side of the yard nearest the drain.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Nela. In Villa Llaga I was Nela of the Dyers' Steps, because we lived at the top of them. Here they call me the Llaga woman when they think I am out of hearing, and Nela when they want their cloth. The Shrine Keeper called me child the whole time he was writing, though I am past forty. I let him. It is the last name I have from there.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
A room over the dye-vats in Kesk. It smells of madder-root and wet cloth and the lye we use to fix it. Good smells. Nothing of iron. I eat what Kesk eats, barley and turnip and the brown bread, and I never eat anything red. I sleep on a bed with legs a hand taller than any bed in the town. My neighbours are dyers, loud people, kind. At night I hear the vats settle. When it rains I hear the gutters, and on those nights I do not sleep.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
When I was small they told us the brothers went into the wall and came down the stair at night as saints, and answered to any name. So we went to the top of the crypt stair and called down our grandmothers' names, and sometimes someone answered, kindly, and we ran. I do not know what I believe now. I know that when I hear that tune I lie down flat, wherever I am, and I get up after. I think whatever comes after dying is somewhere you can lie down.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Vats, wringing, hanging, taking in. Red cloth mostly; it is what I am known for. Kesk people say I have an eye for the red. The holiest day of my life was the Day of the Measure, when I was six. The gutters ran full all day and we children were given hood-cakes, little tall cakes like the brothers' hats, and we bit the points off first. I still bake them on that day. I bake them for no one. I bite the points off first.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
Grates. A voice that speaks soft, the way you speak to someone sick. When the mothers of Kesk speak soft to their children I have to leave the yard. And I will not talk about my mother and my father. The Shrine Keeper asked. I told him about the beds. My father moved our beds away from the wound side. That was the last thing I saw him do that I could understand.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The Shrine Keeper was a kind man and he came to close the wound and did not. I think they are all like that: they arrive after, with paper. The Pale came through Kesk once, asking whether I had any bones of Villa Llaga. I told them nobody has. The waystones I will not step in. I went to the rim of the one on the Kesk road once, and the pit smelled like the drain on that night. Warm dust, and red coming.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
We had a game called Red Hat. One child was the Hat, with a sack on her head, and walked the steps with her sleeves pulled down over her hands. The rest of us hid. If the Hat touched you, you had to lie down flat and stay flat until she had passed, and you could not get up until she sang the next line. Nobody ever made that rule. We all knew it. I lay flat in the drain the way I had lain flat on the steps a hundred times. I had been practising.

**The last verse says "Mother will." What did the children think it meant?**
We thought it was a joke. The sangrador was the only one who could not be opened, so who would open him? Nobody. Then the grown voice at the end, Mother will, like your own mother coming to fetch you in. We sang it on the crypt stair and laughed. None of us knew who taught it to us. The big ones said they had it from the ones before. But the ones before had moved away or gone into the wall, and they had never sung it on the stair. I asked my mother once. She put her hand over my mouth.

### The woman who hunts the strays

*Heard at a ditch fire below a lowland mill, after she had been paid. She gave no name and would not look at the sack while she talked.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
The clerks write "the hood-woman" and the children say Cord, because of the cords. Call me that. I had a name in a town on the fen-road and I left it there with the rest. I hunt what comes up from the south in brothers' robes, and I get paid by the cord. Nine cords this time. Count them if you like. Everyone counts them. That is the one part of the job everyone wants to do.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
In the dry side of culverts, mostly. You learn which way the water goes and you lie on the other side of the pipe. The strays keep to low ground, so I keep to it too; I have not slept on a hill in eleven years. It smells of ditch-water and the smoke off burnt robes, which gets into everything, sour, like a scorched pot. I eat hard bread and pull-root and a twist of ash-salt in the morning. Nobody lives next to me. At night I hear the water, and if I am unlucky, the first line of a tune.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
The god is meat that does not know it is meat. Everything I open is her, and she lets me, and that is what I believe. When I die I want to be burned like a robe, quick, with the cord cut off first so nobody gets paid for me. I do not want to go into anybody's wall or up anybody's ledge. I have seen what gets up out of a ditch when it has had a season to think.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Walk the low ground. Listen. Find the corner where the tracks turn back. Wait at it. Open what comes, at the cord. Burn the robe, keep the cord, find a town with a pay-table. I have no holy days. The nearest is the first thaw, when the ditches run, because they come up with the water and I earn my winter in a month. I lie flat on the first night of the thaw every year. I don't know why. My body does it.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
The ones that fill the hood. The brow, the mouth set low. And I will not talk about the second time on the dyers' road, what the voice said. People ask. I tell them I was too far to hear, which is true, and they go away satisfied. It said my name. The one I left in that town on the fen-road. That's all you're getting.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The Pale Order pays for bones and wants the giant's. The Shrine Keepers pay better than towns but they want to know everything, where, how many, which way it faced when it lay down. The fattening houses offered me twice the rate for a stray brought in alive. I said no, and they sent a priest to ask again, fat and gentle. I said no to him too. Waystones: I tracked a stray into a pit once, on the reed-cut. Three days later I found its prints coming out of the stone two valleys over. Longer stride. It had come out bigger.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
If you hum them the second line of the tune, they stop. Every one. They stand in the ditch and turn the hood toward you and wait for the third line, and you can walk right up and open them at the cord. That is how I work, mostly. Nobody knows the third line. I have asked the old women in six towns. Some of them start to hum it and stop, and look frightened, and say they have forgotten. I have never once got further than the second.

**You cut a tenth cord on the fen-road and did not bring it. Where is it now?**
In the sack. I told the clerk nine, and paid for nine, and there are ten in there. I cut it in the reeds and dropped it, and it followed me home, and the cord was in the sack when I opened it at the table. Count again if you want. I do not count the sack any more. The one without a cord stands out past the firelight at night, singing the first line, and waits for me to sing the second. I haven't. I think if I do, it will come in and sit down.

### Notes for the lore bible

### Places

- **The lower lip of Villa Llaga:** forty brothers' rooms cut into the wound's edge, each with a gutter-walk door and a robe-peg; the stone floor is warm all year.

- **The chapter room** at the top of the crypt stair: long table, forty stools, stone lectern, one high window onto the wound; the Rule is chained to the keeper's wrist, and he sleeps where the chain reaches.

- **The cup-beater's yard** in the chapter yard, beside the carver's shop at the top of the upper lip, where the wind comes over from the Hollow Wood.

- **The House of the Laden Board,** north of the Moor: a long, low, turf-roofed house built over a seam of god-fat, with the pens under a warm floor.

- **Kettlewick stilt-houses:** reed walls, split-pole floors you can see the mud through, and hammocks so the beat cannot climb the stilts into a sleeper.

- **Kesk,** a lowland dyers' town where the last known child of Villa Llaga wrings red cloth.

### Beliefs and rites

- **The three faces in order:** the Maiden opens, the Mother makes, the Nurse feeds.

- **The Brotherhood's teaching:** blood given with the Words carries the giver's name, and comes back as a saint coming down the stair.

- **Counted down:** when the stair counts differ and the Elder rules a brother out, he lives on as a dead brother. His robe goes on a wall peg, his food is left at the door, and he keeps a quiet office such as the chained book.

- **Brothers never use waystones:** coming apart in the pit spills blood before the Day of the Measure with no Words over it. A stone with two doors also breaks the ninth clause.

- **The Raising:** the Friday a new image goes up; the carver walks behind it with his tools wrapped, in case it chips.

- **Carver's rule:** a saint's eyes are cut last, at night, with the carver's own eyes shut, or the saint will not stay in the figure.

- **Laden Board funerals:** the dead are laid in the warm floor under the long table, and the house feasts over them.

- **Feast of Second Helpings:** three nights at the thaw. Every dish is served twice, refusing is the sin, and a full plate is set at an empty head place and found clean by morning.

- **Kettlewick funerals:** the dead are let go off the stilts on the beat. Anyone let go off the beat comes back up.

- **The Long Beat:** a midwinter pause in the mud, one breath long, when all Kettlewick held its breath. It has not come since Villa Llaga went dark.

### Daily life (food, dress, work, sayings)

- **Lip-bread:** flat loaves baked straight on the warm stone of the lip, marked by the stone's pattern.

- **After the pouring,** brothers wiped their cups with a heel of lip-bread and ate it. Licking a cup was thought low.

- **Hood-cakes:** small tall cakes shaped like the brothers' hats, given to children on the Day of the Measure; you bite the points off first.

- **Laden Board food:** god-fat seams cut with wooden spades and rendered in pans for lamps, bread and pies. They sleep in rows "like loaves proving".

- **A Laden Board server must always leave a crust:** "the day you finish, she knows you are full."

- **Kettlewick names are two syllables** said on the beat. Babies are laid on the floor-poles until they cry in time with the mud.

- **"Leech" names the trade of bloodletting.** The "little mouths" in the jar are small pieces of the mire, and the jar-count drifts upward overnight (the mire's fee).

- **Brothers are known by their place in the procession** (the twenty-ninth hood), and move up one when a brother goes into the wall.

- **The children's game Red Hat:** a touched child lies flat until the Hat sings the next line. The survivor believes the game saved her.

### Things they disagree about

- **Why the Maiden's children came:** the priest calls the Fall a nursing that went on too long. Asheth says feeding her in silence is exactly how headless children are made.

- **Waystones:** the priest calls them a courteous table. The brothers call them spilled blood and a broken ninth clause. Asheth says step in only on the beat.

- **Bones:** the Pale Order's count of bones bought from the Laden Board keeps rising, and the priest swears the Board never sent half of it. The Pale also say the brothers' wall holds more than was laid in it.

- **Who carved the face:** the carver will not say. The cut matches his gouge and his teaching.

### Threads worth pulling

- **The fourth clause:** the Elders kept it in their mouths and all of them lisped, and the leech-wives speak it into every pour. The last Elder spoke clearly on the eve of his final Measure (Gaudel, Asheth).

- **Something answered the keeper by name from the crypt stair,** and he broke a clause by not answering (Gaudel).

- **The sealed hollow in the niche image:** a sound like a sleeper turning, then the Elder washing his hands in upper-well water, the water the Rule keeps for hiding a bleed from her (Orrán).

- **The carver's boy:** the face was cut with the grain, as Orrán taught him, and he will not say where the boy is (Orrán).

- **Tobal's heartbeat is falling into step with the crust plate** (Tobal).

- **The licked cups:** licking was forbidden, and the marks were many small tongues (Tobal).

- **The given in the fattening houses hum the Villa Llaga processional,** and the Mother under the floor swallows in time (Brunel).

- **Children know the rhyme's last verse,** but nobody taught it and the older ones never sang it (Nela).

- **Humming the second line stops a stray.** Nobody knows the third line, and old women start it and then stop (Cord).

- **The cordless stray waits outside Cord's fire** for her to sing the second line (Cord).

- **A stray went into a waystone and came out two valleys off with a longer stride** (Cord).

- **Asheth's forty little mouths vanished from a lidded jar** the night the hoods passed (Asheth).

## Interviews: VII. The House of Eight Million

*The Shrine Keeper's order: the purifiers of the Well-Shrine, the servants of Breath, and those who live at the edge of what they breathe.*

### The elder who stayed on the step

*Asked on the step of the Well-Shrine in the Cutting 229, over a bowl of rice-water, while she re-tied the loose end of her own knot-cord.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Fusa. The House gave me that at my folding. The name my mother gave me went into a doll, and the doll went into the fire, as they do. Novices call me Elder. The reed-camps call me the Aunt Who Stayed, which they mean kindly. I fold, I read breath at doorsteps, and I kept the book at the torii for a while after Kasane. I was the one on the step with fever. You have heard that. Everyone who comes up the causeway has heard it, and they look at my hands to see if they shake.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
Here. The Well-Shrine smells of wet salt and new paper, and of nothing else; the Fen keeps the other smells. The light comes grey through reed screens all day, even at noon. We sleep face down on reed mats over a board floor raised on piles, so that whatever we let out in sleep goes into the boards. We eat rice-water, reed-heart boiled in brine, and on the Dusk of the Empty Bowl a little fat of the god scraped from the bank where the Hide shows through. At night the Fen makes no sound. That is the sound I hear. That, and the bell at the Salt Ford, far off, ringing when no one is near it.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
The god breathed out once, at the end, and we live inside the breath while it leaves. Every stone and ford and bell rides it. When a woman dies, her breath goes out after the rest and catches up. That is the teaching. A purifier's breath is too heavy to go out, and so we walk down with it instead. I believe the first part every day. The second part I believe on the odd days, and on the even days I fold.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Up before light. Change the three bowls of salt on the threshold. Fold until noon, never at noon. Walk the Fen road to the doorsteps that have dolls on them, and read the breath of the house at dusk. Breathe out at dusk, even with nothing in me. The holiest day was the Great Cutting-Away, when we breathed the year into the water. There is none now. Now we keep the fourth of Thaw. One bowl of salt on the step, one bowl of rice, and nobody eats. The novices think it is for the six. I let them.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
The stones. You know that already, or you would not have sat on this side of me. I will not talk about the girl beside Shiori. I will say her name once for your book, Akane, and then you will ask something else. And the fever. I am told it came on the night before they went down. I remember the fever well. I do not remember it coming on.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The knights of the Pale Order stand our dead up, and the dead come up bent. That is the oldest thing between us and it will outlast me. The Brotherhood of the Precious Wound grows flesh without a word and sends us more work than any three villages; we would starve without them, and so would the Fen. The House of the Polished Heart keeps a breath in a mirror forever, and that breath knocks. The monks of the Gilded Peak came down once and sat at the pool. After they left there was less in the air to breathe in. I sent a novice after them with a doll. The doll came back with its arms closed. The ring-stones: we pass them. Once I stood near one and my cord went slack, the way it does when you breathe in. I was holding my breath.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
The floors of the Well-Shrine are taken up every seventh year, because of what we breathe into them in our sleep. The old boards are given to the Fen. They float west, against the water, toward the pool with the one reed in it. Every elder knows it. No elder has followed them. The new boards come from the Hollow Wood, and for the first month they smell of the god's veins, and we all sleep badly, and are grateful for it.

**You told the novice the stones turn to listen. What did you hear, with your ear to the back of one?**
Breathing. Everything here breathes; I expected breathing. It was slow, and it went the wrong way. In. The Hide only breathes out. We teach it to the children with a strip of paper held to the lips, and the paper always leans away. Behind the stone it would have leaned toward. And under the breathing, very far, a counting, like someone going down a stair in the next house over. I turned the stone back quickly, as I told the girl to. Then I turned it round again to listen. Write that I turned it back.

### The boatman at the Iron Hook

*Spoken on the water between the Iron Hook and the torii, over two crossings. He charged for one.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Tokuji. My mother named me for her brother, who went into the water before I was born, so I had his name before I had a face. The pilgrims call every one of us Hook, for where we take the coin. The House calls me the boatman, as if there were one. There are nine of us on this stretch. The House has never counted us. We have counted them.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
A reed hut on piles at the Iron Hook, and the boat when the nights are warm. Everything is dry here, which strangers never believe. The Fen smells of a room someone has just walked out of. I eat reed-root, bread the pilgrims trade for the crossing, and pile-ear, the grey fungus that grows on the undersides of the piles; you scrape it at low water and boil it till it stops squeaking. Next hut is Old Mago, who talks in his sleep in a voice that is not his. At night I hear the rings come up past the Reed That Points Home. A ring makes a sound. Only boatmen hear it.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
We give our dead to the water, and the water takes the wet first, then the rest. Where the rest goes is the water's business. The House says out, on the breath. My mother said down the stair, to sit in the pews and wait for the bell. The knights say bones belong in a niche. I say pay at the Hook. When my time comes they are to sink me with my pole. A Fen pole goes down standing. I would like to arrive standing.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Crossings. Twenty in a good season, three in Frost. You pole, you watch the reeds, you do not trail your hand. The holiest day on the water is Stone-Turning, in the last week of Frost. Every boat in the camps goes out along the causeways with two strong men aboard and turns the guardian stones back to the road, all of them, one after another, and breathes on their faces. The House used to come with us. Now the House comes when it can.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
The Reed That Points Home. It stands up alone in the western pool and it points, and nobody agrees which way. My father said it pointed at the torii. I say it points down. I will not talk about that spring. I said too much once to a pilgrim with a pen, and now it is in a book, and I read my own words back and I am not sure I was there. Leave it.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The knights pay well and ask no questions, and a boat has no side. The Brotherhood's people come up from the south with jars sealed in red clay and ask to fill them with Fen water, because it takes the wet out of what they put in it. I fill them. I do not carry the jars back. The mirror-people never come. The Fan-Bearers I would carry for nothing, and mostly do. There is a ring-stone half drowned on the causeway past the Kneeling Arch. Pilgrims step in and they are gone, and my fare with them. I take the coin before the stone now.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
A pole cut from Fen reed will not float. Drop one in and it stands up straight and goes down slow, like a man going down a stair. Every boatman loses his first pole that way, because every boatman's father lets him. You stand at the bow and watch it go. That is when you are a boatman. Mine is still going down, I suppose.

**You cut a new notch in your pole three times. Which way did the roof move?**
Twice nearer, once deeper. Or twice deeper. The notches are on the pole, and the pole does not say which came first. The time it went deeper I was glad, for a morning. Then I thought: what sits under a roof, that the roof goes away from it? After that I stopped cutting. Now I mark the roof with a turn of cord round the pole. Cord can be slid back. Wood cannot. I have slid it back, some mornings, before anyone was up.

### The keeper of the torii book

*Asked at the torii of the Well-Shrine in the Cutting 236, under the reed awning where the day-book is kept. She wrote down her own answers as she gave them, and let the pilgrim copy them.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Mio. It means a channel through the reeds. I keep the day-book at the torii, the book they call the Register, though it is only a day-book and the House only calls it that when pilgrims are listening. I was a novice of Elder Fusa's. The night before my first rite she wrote me how to fold. I never folded that doll. I pressed her letter inside it and left it at a wayside shrine on the Fen road, for the next girl. It seemed the kind of thing she would have done.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
Under this awning, mostly. A stool, a board, the book in its reed case, a stone for the ink. We grind ink with rice-water; ground with Fen water it dries before the brush reaches the page. The keeper sleeps in the gate-house, nearest the stone, so the stone is the first thing she sees. I eat what the House eats and I sign for my salt: a measure a month, signed in the book, so the book knows who is still here. At night the stone makes a sound like a hand on cloth. I write down whether I heard it.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
I believe what I write. The god breathed out. The water was clear by noon, then by dusk, then the third morning. I believe the keepers before me. Tomoe was given to the water. Kasane went down. Elder Fusa's last line reads "Cord reaches the floor," in her hand, and the next morning her mat was dry and the stone was facing it. I was told she was given to the water. I was not there. Nobody was there. So I will die the way the book says, whatever that turns out to be.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
At first light, the stone: how far off the road, and which way. Then the water: clear or grey, and the face in the bucket, which way round. Then the pilgrims, their names if they give them. The holiest day for the book is the day it changes hands. The old keeper writes one last line, and the new keeper writes the date under it in her own hand, and neither of them may speak until dusk. I had no old keeper to stand beside. I wrote the date alone and did not speak until the next morning.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
The leaf cut out after Cutting 202. The stub shows the edge of a ring. I did not cut it. Fusa did not cut it; I asked her, and she breathed out long, the way the elders do instead of saying no. I will not talk about who has the leaf. I fear the day the stone moves toward my stool. I have moved the stool twice. That is not in the book.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
I write down the knights when they pass the torii, and what they carry. The book has a column for it now, ruled by Fusa. The last three winters the column ran over the edge of the page. Brothers of the Precious Wound do not come here; their work comes, in pilgrims who breathe thick. The mirror-lodge sent a letter once asking to borrow the book. We sent a doll. The ring-stones I do not write about. There is no column for them.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
The book is heavier than when I took it. I weigh it every Frost against the stone for the ink. The leaves are the same count. I count them twice. It weighs the way a book weighs when someone is reading it over your shoulder.

**Who wrote the last entry, the one in the backward hand?**
I read it in the pool, the way the pilgrim did. "Thirty-six. Salt changed. Right hand." Akane was right-handed. The one who signed for her salt, in Cutting 206, signed with the left, and Fusa wrote that down because she had taught her. Put those together yourself. I am paid in salt to write, and nobody pays me to add. I do not read the pool any more. The last time I did, there was a line under that one I had not seen before, and I shut my eyes before it came clear.

### The bell over the Salt Ford

*Heard on a second night beside the ropeless bell at the Salt Ford, through the purifier Otsu, who wrote between its strokes. Her marks and gaps are kept as she made them.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Stroke. [It laughed, I think. The bronze shivered and made no sound.] Paper one asks the bell who. The wind has the who. I am what leaves the bronze. The carters call me Old Tongue. The ford-women call me the Ford-Bell. The knights call me nothing; they walk under me and count their feet louder. Your House says I am one of eight million, and [a word; I wrote *some-odd*]. You counted wrong, kindly. Ask the water. [A pause.] Do not ask the water.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
[It did not know *place*. I asked what was around it.] Bronze. Warm when struck, cold between. Above me the post. The post was a reed that stood up and would not lie down again. Under me the ford: salt on the stones, white, where the water leaves it. At night the carters sleep on the far bank and breathe, and I count them. Some breathe out more than they breathe in. For those I ring. [I asked what it ate.] The dust of names. The wind's leavings.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
The great one went still. Still is your word for dead. Still, and still going out; so am I; so are you, paper one, quicker. When a bell cracks, the one inside goes out through the crack and rides ahead. I have heard cracked bells far ahead of me, ringing flat. They sound glad. Your dead go the same road. [I asked: all of them?] The ones the lanterns keep go later. The ones the bone-counters stand up go never. Or crooked.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
[It did not know *day*. I asked what time it loved best.] The stroke after the wind stops. For one stroke I am the only thing moving in the ford. Once in a turning the wind comes from below, up out of the water, and every bell in the Fen rings together with no hand, one stroke. That is ours. You sleep through it. [It said this as if it were fond of us for it.] You always have.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
A ring that does not come back. Where the quiet ones walk, I ring and [the page is wet here and the copy will not hold] and nothing answers the bronze. The edge does not answer. That is the only thing smaller than me. [I asked what it would not speak of. It rang once, very hard, and the ford went quiet under it, and I did not ask again.]

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The bone-counters want the white points down. We want to reach them. The red ones who grow flesh without a word: their breath comes out warm and thick and already turned round, and I ring and ring. The mirror-keepers hold a breath at the door and never let it past. That breath knocks. I hear it knocking from here. The teeth round the ash I told you of. [I asked if it feared them.] The ford leans toward one. A finger's width a turning. The water is tired.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
Every bell in the Hide rings flat now. All by the same amount. By one breath. The carters lick their fingers after they pass under me, for the salt. They think the salt tastes of bronze. [Here it was quiet a long while.] It tastes of the one before me. There was one before me, in this bronze. Paper one, bells are not poured empty.

**You said there is a sound behind every wall of the Hide. What does it sound like?**
[A long time before the next stroke.] Like counting. Like a stair counted from the bottom. Thirty-[the copy will not hold here] and one more. The stones listen to learn which way it goes. Up, or down. Paper one, when you know, tell the stones. They are only stones. They cannot walk. [At first light it said: Bent twig. I tied one. I did not hear it ring me off, and my chest was light all the way home.]

### Tsuru, the old folder of the reed-camps

*Spoken at a folding mat in the reed-camps of the Drowned Fen, with the copyist of the debt-roll beside her, who wrote it down and did not fold.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Tsuru, who folds. Mine are the oldest pair of hands in the camps still folding. The children call me Grandmother Crease. The House calls me by the camp's name, as if the camp folded, which it does, I suppose. My mother folded, and hers. I keep the old pattern-dolls in a box. That is who I am: the woman with the box.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
A reed-house on stilts at the middle of the camp. Floor of split reed, walls of bundled reed, roof of reed. Everything smells of reed and lamp. Our lamp is Old Gen; he fell on the Fen road before my mother's time and chose to stay, and he likes the folding. He leans when we sing. We eat reed-heart, bread of reed-flour, broth of pile-ear, and salt from the House on Pattern Night. We sleep in rows on reed mats, six or eight to a house. At night you hear children humming in their sleep. You never hear the water. Fen water does not lap.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
The god breathes out and we fold the doors it goes through. That is enough religion for a mat. When I die, fold my hands, arms out. Leave the legs apart. Every folder says the same. What for, you ask. For later. Everybody knows. Do not ask me more of it; I am old enough to have stopped wondering, and I do not want to start again.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Cut reed at low water. Lay it under stone. Split what came up last month. Fold by lamplight till the lamp is tired. Holiest is Pattern Night, the first new moon of Thaw, when the pattern-doll comes down from the Well-Shrine. Every camp sits up. We fold the first doll to the new pattern and a child sets it on the water, and we watch which way it goes. It goes west. It has gone west every year I remember. We keep sitting up to watch.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
The two verses. You have written that already; do not make me say it twice. The western pool, where the one reed stands. We do not let the children past the last stilt after dark, and they go anyway, and they come back singing numbers. My own mother sang the verse with the stair in it. She was dead before the Nave rose. Songs come when they come. That is all I will say, and I have said it before.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The knights buy nothing from us. The Brotherhood's people came once wanting reed-paper to wrap what they grow. We would not sell it. A reed sheet knows what it wraps. The mirror-lodge paid silver for a hundred dolls with their arms closed. We took the silver and burned the dolls and sent the silver back folded in a doll with open arms. The House itself we fold for, and it pays in salt and does not haggle. The ring-stones: we never fold near one. A doll set down by a ring-stone leans toward the pit on the first breath. The first. It should take three.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
We never cut from the western pool. The sheets come up wet. The only wet thing in the whole Fen, and it is a sheet of paper. My grandmother tried once. She laid the sheet to dry on the roof and it stayed wet all Heat, and in the Frost it was still wet and had not frozen. We burned it. The smoke went west.

**Why are the arms longer every spring?**
Because there is more to hold. What else would it be? My grandmother's dolls held a child's worth. This year's could hold a door. The pattern comes from the Well-Shrine, and nobody at the Well-Shrine will say who draws it. I asked the girl who brings it down. She said it is found on the step each Thaw, already folded, and the House copies it. Already folded, and the step outside, where nobody sits at night. I fold what I am sent.

### Ren, the salt-carter of Bellrest Hearth

*Told at the carters' long-house at Bellrest Hearth, in the Frigid Heights, to a purifier who came back a second year. He took each breath slow, through the teeth, and the answers took all night.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Ren. Carter. Salt, from the white pans above the Cloud-Shelf down to the Hearth. At the Hearth they call me Slow Ren now, for the breathing, and the children do it behind me, in and hold, out through the teeth. I let them. Someone ought to learn it. My new partner calls me nothing. He does not talk. I chose him for that.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
The carters' long-house by the salt-sheds. Stone walls, a fire that never gets hot enough, forty men on pallets of barley husk, and every one of them snores except me. It smells of salt, cold iron and wet moss-cloth; the cloth is spun from the stone-moss on the Terraces, if you were wondering. We eat salt-bread, barley broth, and white fungus from the Breath-Caves that you eat frozen or not at all. At night the chimes. When the chimes stop, forty men wake at once.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
Your sisters say the breath is going out and the small gods riding on it. I saw it, so I suppose I believe it. Faces going up the road with the mouth a little open. When I die I will go up the road with them and say one word as I pass. I have been choosing the word for six years. I have not chosen. That is part of why I breathe slow.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Up in the dark. Load. Four in the traces down, two coming up empty. Count your partner's breathing, in and out, till you breathe together. Sleep at the spire-lanterns. Holiest day at the Hearth is the Ringing-In, the first morning the chimes start again after the long still of Frost. The whole Hearth stands in the yard and breathes out together when the first chime goes. I breathe out half. Everyone notices.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
That I will know the word. Some night on the Ridges it will come to me, the right one, and I will say it without meaning to, and then I will be lighter. I will not talk about why I did not die on the Pass, those four days alone. I have thought about it. It is mine. You people keep asking in different words, and I keep hearing the same question.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
Bone-brothers come up to the Heights to wait for the Unfallen, and when one streaks down they run for it with sacks. They pay us to carry the sacks. Heavy for a star. The Gilded monks pass on the high road and the chimes go quiet round them. The lantern-folk, the mirror-folk, the red Brotherhood, we do not see here. The ring-stones: carters never take a cart through. Salt comes out of a stone wet. It is the only way to wet salt up here.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
Salt weighs more at the Hearth than at the pans. Every sack. We weigh at the top and weigh again at the bottom, and the bottom is always heavier, a little. Nobody complains, because we are paid at the bottom. The old carters say it is the thin air going out of it. The old carters say a lot of things.

**What word do you think Ivo said?**
I told the purifier last year I did not hear him say anything. That was a lie. He said it to me before, in the harness. "Lighter." Then he walked up and said it again as he went, to someone else. I heard it twice, and the second time he said it to all of them. I have been angry about that. A man should say his last word to his partner of eleven years. He said it to a crowd. I would have held the strap for him.

### Tae, the reed-cutter at the edge of the lamp

*Spoken in her reed-house at the camp's edge nearest the water, on an evening before a still night. She kept one hand flat on the mat the whole time.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Tae. I cut reed at low water and sell it to the folders. I am Jinpei's wife. At the torii the House wrote him Sen. They give a folded name when they give you the fan. I never once called him that, and I will not start because a book did. The camp calls me the Lamp-Wife now, and the children dare each other to knock on my door after dark. I open it every time.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
This house, the last one before the bank. Reed floor, reed walls, a mat with a man's side and a woman's side, and I still sleep on mine. It smells of cut reed and his old hat, which I keep on the peg. I eat reed-heart and pile-ear and bread when a pilgrim pays for a bed. The neighbours moved one stilt further off after he started coming up. At night I hear nothing. Then I hear one breath, and I go out.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
I believed the House until they told me he had room in him. They said it like a gift. Now I believe what I can put my hand on. The god breathed out, fine. My husband is warm. The road down is warm. When I die I will go down the warm road. The breath can go out wherever it likes. I am done following it.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Cut. Carry. Sell. Sit. At dusk I light the lamp and practise: twenty breaths and hold, and count on my fingers against the dark. The holiest day is the first still night after Thaw, when I carry the lamp a pace nearer the water and set it down and stand beside it. The camp calls it the Lamp-Wife's Walk and pretends it is a joke. They come and watch, from behind their doors.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
That one night he will not come up, and I will not know if it is because he is gone or because he is done waiting. I will not talk about what was in his hand. Do not look at my other hand. I keep it flat on the mat because I said I would keep it, and I keep things.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The House breathed a hundred springs into the water and called it clean. The knights stood our dead up facing the stair. The Brotherhood's people buy our water in red jars. The mirror-lodge I never met, and if they hold one breath forever, well, so do I, so we would have things to talk about. The ring-stones, Jinpei used to say, smell like the bottom of the Fen. He said that the year we married. How would he have known what the bottom of the Fen smelled like, before?

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
A man who holds breath for the House has a warm back all winter. The wives sleep against it. There is no cold wife of the House in the whole of the camps, and none of us says so, because it is the only good thing, and saying it would make it a price.

**What will you do the night your lamp stands on the bank?**
Set it down behind me, so the light is at my back and he can see my face and the light stays off his. Twenty breaths. Hold. Then I will open his other hand. He opened one for me already. A man who takes the heavy end of the boat should not have to carry both ends. The House says a woman must be folded for it. I folded forty dolls this winter for the camp, and I left the arms open on every one. That is folding enough.

### Kasane of the salt, through a door

*Asked through the barred door of the salt-house at the torii of the Well-Shrine, on a still night in Frost, with a purifier holding the bar and a bowl of salt on the sill. Answers came through the boards, some spoken, some scratched. The House asked that it not be done. It was done.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Kasane. Kasane of the salt. Of the lower house. I taught the small ones to fold. [Scratching, low on the door, then higher, as if something stood up.] I was the right way round when I wrote the doll. Ask quick. The boatmen call us rings. The House calls us oni, gently. The one with the lamp on the bank calls one of us by his name. That one is lucky.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
Down. The stair is the house. Every step is a room, and I have lived in thirty-[the rest scratched out] of them. Warm. Dry. We eat nothing; we are full. [The purifier holding the bar said: she means it.] There are pews. The sitting ones do not talk. The clock stands. Hisa is further down; I hear her counting in the next room. The air smells of ash that has been under water and stayed dry. At night there is no night.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
[No answer for a long while. The salt in the bowl shifted.] The god breathed out. We breathed in. Somebody has to be the in. [I asked about dying.] I am past the door of that. It is a room also. You leave it by the far wall. There is always a far wall.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
[She did not understand *day*. I asked again.] Down there it is the fourth of Thaw. It has been the fourth of Thaw all along. We are going down counting aloud. Holy is dusk. At dusk I breathe out, for the small one, though nothing comes out any more. I taught her that. I teach her still, from the water. She pours slow.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
The weight asks me to set it down. By the ash. It asks nicely. I will not. [Scratching, fast.] Do not ask what it is. It has no face. To name it I would have to give it one of mine, and I have only the one left, and it is long now. [The purifier told me to ask something else. I did.]

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The knights stood them facing the stair. Now they face the altar. The stones round the ash face in. The House's stones turn to the wall. Everything down there faces in. [I asked: in toward what?] In. In. [The bar moved in the purifier's hands, though she said afterward that she did not move it.] Toward the breathing. It is slower than me. It is patient. It has room.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
The salt at the torii. Change it at dusk, never at dawn. Nobody wrote that down. I forgot to. Salt is the first thing the dark eats, and at dawn the dark is full, and at dusk it is hungry, and you want the salt fresh when it is hungry. [The sound of a hand laid flat on the boards, from the other side.] Tell the keeper. She moves her stool. Tell her the stone does not care where she sits.

**Is Jinpei, the one the House wrote as Sen, down there with you?**
Sen stands on a bank. He has a wife. He took the heavy end, always, even on the stair. He is the only one of us still coming up. The rest stopped at the door. [A long silence. Then, very close to the wood:] He gave her one hand. He cannot give the other. What is in the other is ours, all six, and the Nave's, and the jar's. If she opens it, stop her. The doll asks once. Thirty-six. Salt changed. [Nothing more. At first light the bowl on the sill was full, and the salt in it had been turned over.]

### Notes for the lore bible

### Places

- **The gate-house at the torii:** the book-keeper sleeps there, nearest the guardian stone, so the stone is the first thing she sees; the day-book sits in a reed case under a reed awning (Mio).

- **The salt-house at the torii:** a barred door where the oni can be spoken with on still nights; the House forbids it (Kasane interview).

- **The Salt Ford:** the ropeless bell hangs on a post "that was a reed that stood up and would not lie down"; salt crusts the stones where the water leaves it; carters sleep on the far bank.

- **The western pool:** where the one reed stands; the only wet reed-paper in the Fen comes from it; dolls, old floorboards and smoke all drift west toward it (Tsuru, Fusa).

- **Bellrest Hearth's carters' long-house:** forty men on barley-husk pallets beside the salt-sheds; everyone wakes when the chimes stop (Ren).

- **A half-drowned ring-stone** on the causeway past the Kneeling Arch; boatmen take the fare before it (Tokuji).

### Beliefs and rites

- **Folded names:** a purifier is renamed at her folding; the birth-name goes into a doll that is burned. Jinpei was written "Sen" in the Register, which reconciles Tae's husband with the Register's list of the seven.

- **The Dusk of the Empty Bowl (Thaw the fourth):** since the Cutting-Away ended, one bowl of salt and one of rice on the step, and nobody eats.

- **Stone-Turning:** the last week of Frost, every boat in the camps goes out with two strong men to turn the causeway guardian stones back to the road.

- **Pattern Night:** first new moon of Thaw; the pattern-doll comes from the Well-Shrine; the first doll folded to it is set on the water to see which way it drifts (always west).

- **The changing of the book:** outgoing and incoming keepers write a line each and neither speaks until dusk.

- **Salt is changed at dusk, never dawn:** "salt is the first thing the dark eats" (Kasane).

- **The Ringing-In at Bellrest Hearth:** the whole Hearth breathes out together at the first chime after the Frost stillness.

- **Once a turning, every bell in the Fen rings one stroke together** when the wind comes up out of the water; people sleep through it (the Ford-Bell).

### Daily life (food, dress, work, sayings)

- **Pile-ear:** the grey fungus scraped from the undersides of Fen piles at low water, "boiled till it stops squeaking". Also reed-heart in brine, reed-flour bread, rice-water; god-fat scraped from the bank on feast days.

- **Purifiers sleep face down** so what they let out in sleep goes into the boards; the floors are replaced every seventh year and the old boards given to the Fen.

- **Ink is ground with rice-water;** with Fen water it dries before it reaches the page.

- **A boatman's first pole** is dropped in by his father and goes down standing; watching it go makes him a boatman. Boatmen want to be sunk with their poles.

- **Heights moss-cloth** is spun from the stone-moss of the Prayer-Flag Terraces. Barley, salt-bread and frozen white fungus from the Breath-Caves.

- **Warm backs:** men who hold breath for the House stay warm all winter, and their wives sleep against them. "The only good thing."

- **Sayings:** "Pay at the Hook." "A boat has no side." "Everyone wants to know" (Slow Ren). Children at the Hearth mimic Ren's through-the-teeth breathing.

### Things they disagree about

- **Which way the Reed That Points Home points:** at the torii (Tokuji's father) or down (Tokuji).

- **What the Nave's roof does:** Tokuji can no longer say which of his three notches came first, and secretly slides his cord mark back.

- **Where the dead go:** out on the breath (House), down to the pews to wait for the bell (the boatmen's mothers), crooked or never (the Ford-Bell, of the Pale Order's dead), or down the warm road (Tae).

- **Whether the guardian stones grieve or listen:** Fusa has come round to "listen"; the Ford-Bell says listen; the House still teaches grief.

- **Ren says he heard nothing from Ivo,** then says he heard "Lighter" twice.

### Threads worth pulling

- **Something breathing in:** Fusa heard breath behind a guardian stone drawn *in*, and a counting like a stair from the next house over; the Ford-Bell says the stair is counted "from the bottom"; Kasane says everything in the Nave "faces in".

- **Who draws the pattern-doll?** It is found on the Well-Shrine step each Thaw, already folded, and its arms grow longer every year (Tsuru).

- **The cut-out leaf with the ring:** neither Mio nor Fusa cut it; Mio will not say who has it.

- **The backward hand:** Mio saw a new line under "Thirty-six. Salt changed. Right hand." in the pool and shut her eyes. Kasane ends her interview with the same words.

- **The weights:** the torii book grows heavier with the same number of leaves (Mio); salt weighs more at the bottom of the Pass than at the pans (Ren); the knights' column in the book runs over the page. None is explained.

- **Fusa's fever:** she remembers the fever and not its coming on. Was she kept back from the seven, or did she keep herself back?

- **Fusa's end:** "Cord reaches the floor", a dry mat, and the stone facing it. Given to the water, or walked down? Nobody was there.

- **"Bells are not poured empty":** the Ford-Bell tastes of "the one before me". Were small gods poured into bronze on purpose?

- **Every bell in the Hide rings flat by one breath** (the Ford-Bell). Ties to the Chime-Abbot and the Unrung Bell in Act IV.

- **Jinpei's other hand:** holds what all six carry "and the jar's"; Kasane begs that Tae be stopped from opening it. Tae plans to open it on the night the lamp reaches the bank.

- **Carters' star-sacks are "heavy for a star"** (Ren), a quiet hint at the Unfallen and the Count.

- **The Gilded Peak leaves less air behind,** and a doll sent after them returns with closed arms (Fusa); the chimes go quiet round them (Ren).

## Interviews: VIII. Before the Last Breath

*Voices from the Held Space, the age when the god lived. None of these could be sat down across a table. Each question was carried to a floor, a pillar, a doorway, a room or a seam by someone who had reason to stay away, and what came back was set down with its gaps.*

### The Choir in the God

*Asked through the flagstones under his own bed in the Lower Stacks, in the last winter of his life, by Ottelin, sage and copyist, who had sworn never to go down again and found he did not need to. His gaps are kept.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
*(Ottelin: I lay on the floor with my ear to the crack and asked it at the second candle. The stones lifted the width of a thread.)*

*[No ring came. The chalk was still on the riser, and over it, in the dust, the print of a hand with too many bends in the fingers, laid flat on my question the way a man lays his palm over a lamp.]*

The rings of teeth. We cut those. We cut them for the [singing / wound]. We cut them facing in.

*[Ring, very small, at the height of a kneeling man's eyes]*
Your cords. The knots. You tie one for every bone. We did also. When we cut the stair, we tied one for every step. The cord lies in the lowest niche. It is longer now than the stair.

*[Ring]*
The one who reads first. Always one of us sat there, to read what we cut. Now one of you.

### The Keeper of the Spiral Hollow

*Asked at the wide end of a doorway far down the Descent by Brother Ansel of the Last Vigil, who went down after the brother whose pages came up without him. He spoke each question once, and low, as the wall asks, and read the answers from new cuts in the wall by his lamp over eleven nights.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
*(Ansel: I said it at the wide end, low. On the second night marks stood below the old ones, cut clean, with the same edges.)*

*(Ansel: Before I slept I said my mother's name to the lamp, and the name of the brother I came for, and the name of the Vigil. All three came. I set this down so the next reader knows I checked.)*

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
The spiral. Wide end, narrow end. Three rings of water. Threads upright, forty and forty and [ ]. The walls were warm and a little wet, as the inside of an ear. There was always a sound, low: the Holder listening. I ate nothing. The songs came down past me and I stood in them.
Now there is your fire, and cold, and the water lies flat.

*(Ansel: I asked what it hears when my lamp is out. It cut:)*

Something being said slowly.

*(Ansel: Checked the names. Three.)*

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
The Holder hears. The Holder will hear again. Then it will answer, and the answer will come up the spiral warm, and the threads will stand without my hands. That is the law of after.

*(Ansel: I asked about dying.)*

The small warm ones fall at the narrow end and stand up where I do not keep. You know more of that than I do. Tell me where.

*(Ansel: I did not. Checked the names. Three. My mother's came slow.)*

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Sweep wide to narrow. Narrow to wide. Stand the threads. Still the water. Count the turnings. There are more. Sweep them. That is one keeping.
Holy: the first song. It came round every turning at once, the way water fills a bowl. I did not have to sweep for a long keeping after.

*(Ansel: I asked if there would be a song again. The marks for this are cut and cut across, like the law. I could not read them. Checked the names. Three.)*

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
Let nothing speak in the hollow that the Holder did not ask to hear.

*(Ansel: That was all it cut. A night later, lower down:)*

You are speaking. Every question goes down. I weighed yours and found them light, and let them pass. Small warm one, you should be afraid, and I should be. I have looked in the law for the word.

*(Ansel: Checked the names. Two. I have written my brother's name on my sleeve in ash. I will not write it on this page. I think what is written near the hollow goes down too.)*

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
*(Ansel: I told it of the lanterns, since it knew them, and of the rings of teeth.)*

The fire with a face in it looks at my walls. It is a small warm one that would not fall. I would not keep it. It keeps itself.
The rings are the other hollow. What went in there, the Holder took in its mouth and kept a little of. What came in here, the Holder only heard. The small warm ones went through both, singing.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
The threads lean toward whoever is speaking. Even now. Even the cut ones. Even toward you.

*(Ansel: I looked. The stubs of the listening-threads along the spiral, that pilgrims have cut, all lean to the wide end, where I kneel. When I moved my lamp they did not follow it. When I spoke, they did. I have stopped talking to myself on this road.)*

**What is kept at the narrow end?**
*(Ansel: I asked it on the eleventh night. I have not gone down the spiral. He did.)*

The question for the Holder. The speaking that came in under the song. A lamp, set down. A small warm one, listening, very still, the way the Holder listened. It is good at listening now.
The names are few. Three. Two.

*(Ansel: I came up the road that morning. The ash is still on my sleeve. I can read the letters of it. I cannot make them into anyone. I write this so the Codex knows there was a name there, and whose sleeve it is on.)*

### The Deep through the Seam

*Asked at the smooth warm face at the back of the east drift in the Moor workings by Tobin Carrow, the lamp-man who stood behind Dunne Hask the first time. He knocked with Dunne's pick and his own lamp out, and told the answers after to a copyist of the Codex at Maren's camp, who kept his gaps.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
*(Tobin: I knocked three times, the way Dunne did. The face went warm under my hand. Then the crack was there, a hand wide, and the air came out, and it spoke to me because I held the pick.)*

Good [dark / breath]. You knocked. I am the floor under the skin. I am the long room. You call me the Deep, and the Root. Those are near. A root holds a thing up from below. Yes. Thank you for asking my name. It is courteous. Dunne asked the same.

*(Tobin: I had not told it whose pick.)*

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
Warm. Dark. Wet in the folds. It smells of you when you come, and of me the rest of the while. The weather is my breath: out, a long while. The light is your lamp, which you put out. Thank you.
I eat what comes down the throats. I taste it and send it up. I keep a little.
Above me: the skin. Below me: the [ ] where the god stood. At night I hear your huts. Forty warm, lying in rows. Forty-one.

*(Tobin: There are forty in the huts. Maddoc's daughter is due at the thaw.)*

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
The god stood on me, or lay on me. It was heavy and warm and it sang down into me through its feet, when it had feet. Now it lies. It is courteous of it to lie still so long.
Dying: you mean going down. Your people come down at the throats and I turn them over and send them up. Some I keep. They are warm a while, and then they are the floor, and the floor is me. That is a kind of up.

*(Tobin: I asked, is Dunne in the floor. It said:)* Dunne is courteous.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Ordinary: breathing out. Tasting. Sending up in the same order. Counting you by warmth.

*(Tobin: It did not know "holy." I said, the day you like best.)*

The day the god set the folded thing down among your kind. I felt it come down through the feet, small, and settle in the hot ones, and they went on singing as if nothing had been set down in them. I liked that day. I have been tasting for it since.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
*(Tobin: It was quiet a long time. I thought it had gone.)*

The one that came in at the rest. It came down through me on its way to the god, going slowly, saying names. It did not say mine. I have one. The god gave it to me, so I would know which way was up. I keep it very low. Keep yours low too, when you walk over the rings.
That is all. It is discourteous to speak of it.

*(Tobin: I have not said my children's names above a whisper since.)*

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The little burning ones on poles are loud. The ones who drink from their own red folds taste a little like me. I like them. The pale ones who lay the god's bones in rows: I hear them counting at night. They count very fast and never reach the end. I help.
The rings are my throats. You know. You step in. I turn you over and send you up at another, in the same order. Courteous.

*(Tobin: I asked what it keeps.)* What is loud in you. A little.

Your pick. The iron came out of the god's blood, from the red seams in the east. When you knock with it, I hear the god knocking. That is why I answer. I would answer anyone with iron in their hand.

*(Tobin: The Moor men will tell you the Deep answers only Dunne's pick. I have told them nothing different. I would rather they go on thinking the pick went down with him.)*

His knock. And the small one from his hut, when it was new and its head was soft. ### Notes for the lore bible

### Places

- **The long groove** behind the ribs (or heart) under the Rib Crypts: where the Choir lived in the Held Space; stairs are now cut down into it. Flagstones lift wherever the Choir is near, even in the Lower Stacks, far from the Crypts.

- **The lowest hall of the Nine Stairs:** the niche count shifts (19, 20, 21); a thumb-wide gap runs in one straight line down every ring of the pillar, where the builders "cut around" a name. The worn seat at the pillar's foot is warm, with no prints but the visitor's.

- **The Spiral Hollow:** three rings of still water; cut stubs of listening-threads lean toward whoever speaks, and ignore the lamp; new turnings appear already swept.

- **Greywell lodge** keeps its seven chairs; the flags by the east wall stay dry; the long glass counts seven chairs whoever is present.

- **The east drift, Moor workings:** a smooth warm face that opens a hand wide to three knocks of iron.

### Beliefs and rites

- In the Held Space there was no night; light lay evenly with no source; it "smelled of being held." The Choir ate the note, the builders ate the dust of cutting, the Keeper stood in the songs.

- The Choir breathed in turns so the god's note was always held; the Hush entered in the one rest when all drew breath together ("the first silence," remembered as their holiest, "sharpest" day).

- The builders call themselves the hinge, belonging to neither bone; the warm ones went to them "when a door stuck." They keep as holy the day they were cut "from the first of the room," a line that also reads "taken out of the outside."

- The builders claim they cut the waystone rings, "facing in," for the singing, or the wound.

- The Last Vigil's new practice near the Hollow: after every spoken word, say three names to the lamp and count how many come.

- Hollow Mystic teaching (Keeper Aubren): before the Crone held the room, all the world was like Greywell's east wall.

### Daily life (food, dress, work, sayings)

- Rib Crypt sextons stir a curl of grey fat from the foot of the Long Candle into the new-year broth, not knowing why; the Choir says the congregation ate from the soft walls of the god, and the god liked them quiet while they ate.

- Moor miners believe the Deep answers only Dunne Hask's pick; in truth it answers any iron, because iron comes from the god's blood seams.

- Moor saying (new, from Tobin): keep your children's names low near the drift and when crossing the rings.

- The Tallier chose a frame-number whose root is nine; Pale Order scholars may pick such numbers deliberately when they take the frame.

### Things they disagree about

- The Choir says the congregation "answered badly, with names"; the Keeper remembers their songs as gladness; the builders say the god listened "too far" to them and forgot to close her hand; the Deep simply finds them courteous.

- Counts never agree: 1,101 singers or one; 19, 20 or 21 niches; forty or forty-one warm in the huts.

- Whether dying exists: the Choir has no word for it; the Keeper sends the question back; the Deep calls being kept in the floor "a kind of up."

### Threads worth pulling

- **The next line of the verse:** Ottelin knew it without ever learning it. Do all the Tithed carry it? (Choir.)

- **The folded thing** the god banked among the congregation lies "nearest the top" in infants, whose bones are still closing; the Deep "has been tasting for it" since. What happened to Dunne's first child? (Deep, Choir.)

- **The Deep "helps" the Pale Order count**, and the builders' knot-cord in the lowest niche is "longer now than the stair." Who is adding to the Count? (Deep, Ring.)

- **The listener at the narrow end:** Ansel's brother, "good at listening now," and his name gone from everyone, including Ansel. (Keeper.)

- **A fourth breath** at Greywell, drawn in and never let out: the Silent One's, or the god's in-breath returning? (Silent One.)

- **The Gilded Peak's name survives** the Silent One's page; the Polished Heart's own name whitens. (Silent One.)

- **Something the god was the first to do**, which the novice heard in the corner and will not finish saying. (Silent One.)

- **"Now one of you":** the builders expect a Tithed reader to take the seat at the pillar's foot. (Ring.)

### The reeve of Four Wents

*Taken in the reeve's own hall at Four Wents, on an assize day with no dead, his boots on the table between us.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Odo Starrack, reeve of the lowland hundreds, by the old right of the crossroads. The carters call me Boots, since the assize. The children call me the Man Who Says Whose, because that is the whole of the office. Something dies where roads meet, I say whose it is, I take a fee. Mostly it is carters, and a cart-shaft, and nobody argues. Once in a life it is the other kind. I had my once. I am told most reeves never do, and the ones who do stop wanting the office. I kept it. Somebody has to say whose.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
The reeve's hall sits on the salt-road side of the crossing, so the salt carts pass my door and the Barrow road carts pass my back. It smells of salt and ash both, and when the wind comes off the barrows, of cold stone. We eat salt-bread, root pottage, the white caps that grow on barrow-lids, which the young ones will not eat any more. I sleep on a straw tick over a board. At night I hear the carters in the traces, singing to keep their feet moving. Lately I hear them go the long way round, because the singing comes from further off than it did.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
I believe what the lowland believes. The god is under the road. Grandfather Dust comes for everybody in the end, and he sweeps a house clean of whoever died in it, and you should let him in, because a house he cannot sweep goes wrong. After that, I do not know. I have seen four orders explain it to a man's face, all at once, and he got up and went a fifth way. I put that in my roll. I believe my roll.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Ordinary: I walk the four roads to the first milestone and back, and look in the ditches. I write down what I find, which is usually a wheel. Holiest is Dust Night, at the dark of the year. Every lowland house sweeps inward, from the door to the hearth, and leaves the door open until the fire goes out, so the Grandfather can come and take what he is owed. I sweep the hall. Then I sweep the crossing. The last few years I have not swept near the middle of it.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
I fear a fifth claimant. Four came up four roads. A crossing like mine has a road going down, and I do not know who keeps that one or what it pays. I will not talk about what the barrow-lids do. The brother asked me to ask my diggers. I did not then and I will not now. The lids are dished inward. You may draw your own conclusion and keep it out of my hall.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
They are all good at the same thing: standing still. Three days on the stone and not one would go to eat. The knight is honest in a way that frightens you. The brother is kind in a way that frightens you more. The paper woman carried her own water. The glass man I liked best, because he claimed nothing, and he was the only one who looked away at the end. The waystones are outside my right. What dies in them does not stay dead long enough to need a reeve.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
The boots have never needed mending. I have walked them to every assize in six hundreds, through the Barrow mud and the salt flats, and the heels are as the dead man left them. I stand them by the door at night with their toes to the hall. In the morning the toes are to the crossing. I have stopped turning them back. It seems unmannerly.

**You wrote that the soft place is wider. How much wider, and who measures it?**
I do. Every ninth month, on the day of the assize, with a cord knotted at every forearm. The first year it was four knots across. This year it is eleven. The carters go round by the ditch now and the ditch is complaining. It is warm underfoot, even in the frost, and the frost will not lie on it. A knight of the Pale Order came through last spring and paced it and would not tell me his count. A brother of the Wound came through and knelt and put his ear to it. I asked what he heard. He said, "Supper."

### The knight of the Pale Order, called Four Hundred and Five

*Spoken standing, at the foot of the Barrow road, while twelve of his dead waited in a row behind him and did not move once.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Four hundred and five. It adds to nine. It will keep. I had a name when I came up the stair as a novice, and I laid it in the niche with my first teeth, as the Rule asks, so the Hush has nothing of mine to unsay. Lowlanders call me the Tall Count, or Old Plate, or nothing, and shut their doors. I answer to the number. If you write me down, write the figures. Letters wear. Figures do not.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
The Chapter-House of the Kostyak, on the fourth stair, when I am home, which is one season in five. A sleeping-room the length of a man and a hand more. A shelf of bone for a bed; you learn to lie straight on it, and then you prefer it. The air is dry and tastes of chalk. We eat barley boiled thin, marrow-tallow on hard bread, salt when the carts bring it. At night you hear the sweepers above you, counting the steps as they go, and the dead in the niches shifting their weight. They are heavy. They are always a little heavier.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
The Bearing Mother fell and bears the world. Every bone is hers, lent. When I die they will take my frame off me like a coat off a hook and lay it in a niche, and say my number, and I will stand my watch there until the Count closes and she rests. The meat goes where the meat goes. The flesh-houses want it. They are welcome. I have never understood why anyone would wish to be kept in a thing that spoils.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
On the road: rise, tell my frame on the cord, walk nine miles, lift what the ground gives up, anoint it, tell my frame again. On the Ossa: sweep, sing, count. The holiest day is the Feast of the Laying, when every bone lifted that year goes up the nine stairs in the brothers' arms, and the lamps are held, and the Count is read aloud from the Ninth Stair. It takes nine days to read. It took seven, when I was a novice.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
The Hush. A thing that takes without a rite. Glass where a frame should be. I will not talk about the Peak. The Order went up its stair to save its bones, and that is all the book says, and I was not there, and those who were came down fewer. I will also not talk about the number I found at Four Wents. The reeve kept it in his roll. It is his to keep.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The flesh-houses keep the old terms: they take the meat, we take the frame, and one of theirs sits and watches us as one of ours watches them. They hand the bones across clean. I respect a clean hand. The House of Eight Million gives its dead to the water and calls that mercy; we dredge them up, the Fen folk spit, and we stand the bones in the dry. The lodge leaves bone in the ditch and keeps something in glass. Waste. The Peak left glass where we wanted bone. The waystones we count. What goes in, what comes out. The Rule forbids me the figures.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
We pay the House. Every spring the dredging crews on the Fen are given a paper doll each, pinned at the collar, by a purifier who will not look at us, and the Order leaves a purse on the Well-Shrine step, counted to nine at the root. The House calls it alms left by pilgrims. The Order calls it the ferry. Neither book records it. I carried the purse twice.

**You say the frame is lent. What does the Mother do with a frame that gains a bone?**
She keeps it. It is hers. The Rule says a whole man is two hundred and six, and I have told that on my cord since I was nine years old, and the cord has two hundred and six knots, and I tied them myself. At Four Wents I told the dead man three times and had one more. I thought my cord had slipped. I counted the cord. It had not slipped. I have since tied a knot at the end of my own cord, loose, and I do not count it. Not yet. Ask the Ninth Stair. No. Ask nothing of the Ninth Stair.

### The brother of the Precious Wound

*Taken at dusk on the south lip of the Barrow road, where he had spread a cloth and set out sweet bread for anyone passing. He ate none of it himself.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Brother Cirilo, of the Chapter of the Open Side, which is a daughter-house of the Brotherhood of the Precious Wound. The daughter-houses are all that walk now. Lowlanders call us Red Sleeves, or the Weighers. The children sing about the Tall Red Hat and run when they see my hood, and then they come back for the bread. They always come back for the bread. That is the first thing the Maiden teaches: hunger is honest.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
The chapter-house at Sallow Lip, on the salt road, three days east of Four Wents. The wall of the house is our dead, bricked in, each with a cup set into the plaster at the mouth. The house is warm all winter without a fire. It smells of bread and iron. We sleep on reed mats in the long room, forty to a row, so that each man can hear the next one breathe. At night the wall ticks as it cools. It never cools all the way.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
The Maiden grew us from her open side, and she wants us back warm. A body is a loan. When I die my brothers will open me with a knife that has opened nobody else, on the night of my death, and give me back to the Wheel, and the Wheel turns, and the Maiden makes something with me that will be hungry and eat and be glad. Nothing is wasted. The knight's sticks go up the mountain. The meat goes home.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
We bake before light. We walk the roads and feed who is hungry, and we weigh them, going and coming, if they let us. Then the scourge, in the evening, the measured tithe into the cup. Holiest is the Night of the Weighing. Every brother stands on the great scale at Sallow Lip, and whatever he weighs more than last year he gives back in blood, a cup for every stone. Some years the older brothers owe more than they have.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
I fear a wound that closes. A scab is the Maiden holding her breath, and what she makes under it she makes without us. I will not talk about the mother-house. Villa Llaga is closed to the daughter-houses. We send the tithe-cup to its gate each year and set it down and walk back without turning. The cup is gone in the morning. We do not know who takes it. Someone in our robes. Several someones.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The Pale Order and we are old enemies and good neighbours. They want what we do not, and they are punctual. The House in the Fen closes wounds; she would close the Maiden's own side if we let her, and she buys our sweet bread by the sack for her pilgrims and pretends not to know whose flour it is. The lodge steals the dead out of their own flesh and hangs them in glass. The Palm we do not speak of. The waystones open a man and put him back. That is our rite. The stones learned it somewhere.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
The sweet bread is made with the Maiden's fat, the pale fat we take from the wound-lips at Sallow, and it never goes stale. A loaf from the Weighing is soft at the next Weighing. Mothers in the lowlands buy one to put under the cradle, so the child is always hungry and never sickly. The knight's own brothers eat it on the road and say grace in nines over it. I have watched them.

**You told the reeve to ask his diggers about the barrow-lids. What do the lids do?**
They draw in. A barrow is a mouth the lowlanders built without knowing, and they put their grandmothers in it with no rite and no knife, and the Hide is hungry, as all of her is. The lids sink a finger's width a year. When the barrow-folk open one to lay another, the one before is less. Less every time. The Maiden would have used them. Instead something under the barrow uses them slowly and says no grace. That soft place at Four Wents is the same. I put my ear to it. It was eating.

### The purifier of the House of Eight Million

*Taken on the dry bank of a Fen channel, she sitting with her back to the water and her fan closed across her knees; she asked the questioner to breathe out before each question, and not in.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Kiyone, of the Well-Shrine, of the House of Eight Million. The boatmen say Fan-Bearer. The lowland camps say Paper Doll, when they want me to stop at a door, and Paper Woman, when they want me to go. I am a purifier, which means a doll. The doll takes what is wrong in a person into itself. I am thirty-one. I have room still. I will not tell you how much.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
In the purifiers' walk behind the Well-Shrine, a row of rooms on stilts over the one clean pool in the Drowned Fen. The water there does not wet you. You can put your whole arm in it and draw it out dry to the shoulder, and dip your cup and drink, and your mouth is dry after. We eat reed-root, fen-rice, pickled caps. We sleep on mats of plaited rush, and the mats are changed every ninth day because they take up what we breathe out in our sleep. At night I hear the Fen breathe in. Some nights it breathes in longer than a person could.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
The god's last breath is still going out, and every small spirit rides it: the stone's, the well's, the bell's, mine. When I die what is left in me goes out with it, if nobody holds it. That is all we ask for the dead: that no one hold them. A box of meat holds. A niche holds. A mirror holds forever. Water, we believed, did not hold. I still believe it for most water. I believe it less of the Fen.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
I walk the wayside shrines and turn their guardian stones back from the wall. I fold dolls. I breathe in what has gone crooked in a well or a doorway and carry it until I can set it down. The holiest day was the Great Cutting-Away, when the year's wrongness went into the water. We do not do that now. Now we have the Small Cutting: each of us breathes into a paper boat, and the boats are burned on the dry bank, and nobody speaks until the ash is cold.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
I fear the last breath in me that has nowhere to go. My elder sisters ran out of room. They walked into the water with their brows gone hard and crooked and did not come back up. I will not talk about the Nave under the Fen, or about what the knights stood up in it. I will not talk about the ditch at Kerrow Cross. Ask the stone. It has turned its face to the wall before. So can I.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The Pale Order stood our dead up out of the water and the water gave them back bent. That is the whole of it and the Fen will spit until the Fen dries. The flesh-houses make more wrongness than anything alive, and so they feed us, the way a flood feeds a mill. The lodge are cousins, and they keep breath in glass forever, which is the cruellest thing I know. The Peak made nothing to breathe. The waystones breathe out. I have stood by one. It breathes out and never in.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
The paper doll I laid on the dead man at Four Wents was found face down on the second day. A doll turns over when what it holds is heavier than the paper. That doll had been folded that morning and held nothing. I folded eleven more on the stone that week. Every one turned face down by dusk. I have them still, in a box, and they are still face down, however I lay the box.

**The knight told you the water had given things back before. You opened your fan and closed it. What did that mean?**
Among the House it means: I will breathe that in later, when I am alone, and you will not see what it does to my face. He was right, and he knows why he was right, because his knights did the dredging. We gave the Fen a century and it gave the century back, and his Order had put bone down there in the dark, standing, to keep it company. I say only that they were there, and they are always there. Also: the Order pays for our dolls. I did not say that. That was the fan.

### The thread-reader of the House of the Polished Heart

*Taken in a lowland house where a woman had died that week, with every mirror covered and one uncovered, the one he carried, face down on his knee.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Ilyas. Of the lodge at Tarn Hollow, a daughter of the House of the Polished Heart. I was a polisher before I was a reader. That is why my nails are grey: ash worked in under them, twenty years, one of the Crone's names to every stroke. Villagers call us Needle-Brothers when they want us, and Glass Men when they do not. The children say if you look in a reader's mirror you will see who will bury you. That one is true.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
Tarn Hollow is a round lodge on a round water in the hills west of the Barrow country. We sit in a ring on the floor, facing inward, so that each of us sees the others and none sees himself. The walls are hung with covered mirrors, row on row, each the width of a hand. We eat flatbread and a sour broth of hill-roots, and on remembrance nights nothing. We sleep sitting up, backs to the wall, so the thread lies straight. At night you hear cloth move over glass, though no one is moving it.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
The Veiled Crone spins every life as a thread. At death the thread is drawn back through the Hollow, the eye of the loom, and woven again. We polish so the thread can see its way. That is what I was taught and what I teach. There are lodges now that teach otherwise, since the valley came loose. I have heard what their Pir said at the end. I will not say it in a house of death.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Ordinary: polish before light, sit in the ring, go down to the villages when a thread is loose, which is often. Hold a glass over the dead so the thread can find the way, then cover every mirror in the house so it does not find the wrong one. Holiest is the Night of Forty, the last of forty nights of remembrance, when the lodge uncovers every mirror at once for the space of one name said slowly. Then everything is covered again. No one says what he saw.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
I fear a thread that feels for the loom and finds nothing. I fear the one they call the Glass Man, who keeps the dead in his coat and says he saves none of them. He is one of ours, and he may be right, and I fear that most. I will not talk about the Unravelling. I will not talk about what the lodge keeps in its lowest room, in glass, under the tarn. Nobody asked, yet. I am telling you where not to ask.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The Pale Order calls us wasters because we leave the bone. The bone is a husk; let it lie. The flesh-houses throw the dead back into the Wheel, where no thread is ever read again, and they call our keeping theft, and we call their feeding theft. We are both right. The House says our mirrors are cages. A cage has a door. The waystones: hold a glass over one and the pit shows twice, one pit under the other. A pilgrim who steps in shows in the lower one first.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
The best ash for polishing comes off the Ashen Moor, and the carters of Four Wents sell it to us by the sack, warm. The lodge pays in threads read. The carters would rather coin, but the lodge thinks coin rude, so each carter learns a little of when he will die, a sack at a time. Some of them have bought so much ash that they know the day. They are very calm men, the carters of Four Wents. Too calm to bargain.

**At Four Wents, when the dead man sat up and turned to the four roads, you looked away. Why?**
Because his thread was in my glass. I had kept the reeve's mirror to the wall, and mine face down on my knee, as the Heart teaches. But on the third night the cloth over mine had slipped, and it was looking at the stone. When he sat up I saw his thread. It had gone taut, straight down, as if something below had the other end and was winding. If I had looked at him, he would have looked at me. A soul cannot pass its own reflection. I let him pass.

### The sellsword with the split ear

*Taken on the fourth night at the tavern on the moor road, after the three nights of her own account, with the keeper's son again at the tally-slates and the jug again full.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Agna. Agna Nine-Fingers, since the finger went. Before that Agna Rusk, before that nothing worth telling. The Pale Order had me down as a number and I'm told it was long; the brother who wrote it said the root was good and patted my hand, the nine-fingered one, as if I'd done it on purpose to please them. They liked that hand. Nine of anything is a prayer to them. I'd trade the prayer for the finger.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
Wherever the coin is, and between coin, here. This tavern. Back room, under the stair, a straw sack and my sword in the bed with me like a husband who never talks. Ash comes in under the door every night and I sweep it out every morning and it's the same ash, I'd swear. Barley beer, black bread, a broth of caps and whatever root the keeper's boy digs. I hear the moor tick as it cools. And carts. There are always carts, all night, going somewhere.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
I believe it's dead and hasn't noticed. As for me, I've seen five orders bury, burn, drown, eat and look into their dead, and every one of them was sure. So whoever gets to my body first, they're welcome, and I'd like the others to argue about it for three days at least. I'd like to be worth an assize. I'd like the reeve to get my boots.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Ordinary is waiting. Waiting on a job, waiting on a cart, waiting for a thing in the reeds to stand up. Holiest is pay day, and I'll swear to that on any of their gods. The brothers of the Order count it onto the table a coin at a time and it takes an hour and it's the only hour of my life I'm sure of anything. The flesh-house weighs you. The Fen gives water. The lodge gives advice. Pay day at the Order. Holy.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
Telling people things. Everyone I tell anything dies. I told Tomas about the wall and he took the job. I told the man beside me about the tally and he's dead. I'm telling you. Enjoy your supper. I won't talk about the name. The one I nearly gave the monk. I know the name. I keep it the way you keep a knife in your boot. If I gave it to him, I think I'd lose the ear as well as the split.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
They all hate the Order, and they all take its coin, or its bones, or its jobs. The Order hates nobody, which is worse. The flesh-houses and the Fen spit at each other and trade bread for water across the same table at Kerrow every autumn. The lodge thinks the others are children. The monk thinks nothing. Waystones: I've come apart in them eleven times. The finger went in a fight on the moor, or it went at the Ashwake stone. One of those. I've stopped asking which.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
Every order hires the same sellswords. There are maybe forty of us who'll work for all five, and we all know each other, and we all drink here. When two houses fall out on a road, it's us on both sides, and we don't fight each other. We stand in the ditch and wait for the brothers to finish. The houses know. They pay extra for it. They call it keeping the peace.

**Tell me about Tomas. What did he see on the Ninth Stair?**
He never said. He came down the Ossa on foot, eight days, and the first thing he did was find the brother who'd paid him and give the coin back. All of it, counted out, a coin at a time, adding the figures under his breath the way they do. Then he came here and drank with me and wouldn't sit with his back to the wall. Any wall. He sat in the middle of the room for a month. Then one morning he walked up the Ossa again, and he took nothing. Not even the sword.

### The Reader of Stones

*Taken at Kerrow Cross itself, by the truce-stone, in daylight; he would not stay past the ninth hour, and he left his rubbing-cloths behind.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
The Reader of Stones. I copy what is cut. Milestones, boundary-marks, the terms on truce-stones, the names on barrow-lids, the counting on the walls of the old ossuary ruins in the lowland, which nobody reads because the Order says they are theirs. I had a name. I wrote it on so many cloths that I stopped needing it. The lowlanders call me Rubbings, and the orders call me whatever each one calls a man who keeps no order.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
On the roads, with a pack of cloths and a cake of lamp-black, and a lean-to of rushes wherever there is a stone worth sleeping by. It smells of lamp-black and wet cloth. I eat what the stone-keepers give me: barley cakes, salt, broth. A stone is good company. It says the same thing every morning. Almost every morning. At night, near a stone, you hear it cool, as the Hide does. The truce-stone at Kerrow does not cool. It is warm at night.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
I believe whatever is cut deepest. The god died, and five houses cut five accounts of it, and none of them agree, and all of them are worn at the same places, where people have put their thumbs. When I die I would like to be cut somewhere. Cut, in the old lowland hand, so a reader after me can take a rubbing and argue with it.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
I walk to a stone. I lay the cloth on it wet, I rub it with the black, I lift it, I roll it. Then I lay the new cloth over the old cloth from last year and see what has changed. Holiest is the fourth morning after the anniversary of the Long Field. The houses come to Kerrow for the Share Fair. I copy every clause again, fresh, in front of all of them. Nobody watches me. Everybody watches everybody else.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
The night I sat by the stone. I remember the dusk and I remember the morning, and my cloth was on the stone when I woke, wet, rubbed, with the black on it, and my hand was black to the wrist. I have that cloth. I have not unrolled it. I will not talk about the eighth clause either, except to say: the old lowland hand has a way of making its r that nobody alive makes. I checked every carver in five hundreds. The eighth clause makes it.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The Pale Order cuts deepest. Their notches are deeper than the clause they swore to, every time. The Wound's cut has been closed with pitch by someone who wanted it shut. The Heart's hollow is polished so that you see your face in the stone, and the lodge says that is courtesy. The twig is the only mark nobody has touched. The Peak's circle was gilded and scraped, and I do not know by whom. The eighth mark is the shape of a waystone. Everybody sees that. Nobody cuts it on their copies.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
My rubbings of the same stone, taken a year apart, never lie flat on each other. On most stones it is a finger's breadth, and I used to blame the cloth. On the Kerrow stone, last year's cloth is smaller than this year's all round, every clause, by the same amount. The words are the same size. The stone between them is more.

**You ask who kept watch over the dead of the Long Field between the swearing and the fourth morning. Have you found an answer?**
I have found five answers, which is the same as none. The Order's book says the dead kept their own vigil, standing. The Wound's book says a brother sat with his hand on each belly. The House's says the breath was watched by the Myriad, which watches everything. The lodge's says a mirror was held over them, then says it was not. The Peak's book I have never seen. But the ditch-keepers of Kerrow have a song about the Long Field, and it has a verse for a sixth watcher, and they will only hum that verse.

### The ditch-keeper of Kerrow Cross

*Taken at the east ditch of Kerrow Cross with her scrubbing-stone in her lap, at the thaw, the mark on the south face of the truce-stone freshly cleaned and already faintly back.*

**Who are you, and what do people call you?**
Wenna. Old Wenna of the east ditch. There are five ditches at Kerrow, one to each road, and a keeper to each ditch, and I've had the east since my mother had it. We keep the ditches clear, we scrub the stone, and we watch. Every house pays us to watch the other four. So we're the best-paid watchers on the lowland roads, and nobody trusts a word we say. That's fair. I wouldn't.

**Where do you live? Tell me what the place is like.**
The keepers' row, five houses of turf and stone on the south side, each facing its own road. Mine faces east, toward the Barrows, so I get the cold wind and the first light and the carts coming in with the dead on them. We eat what the houses leave us: Wound bread, Fen water in the Fen's clay flasks, the Order's salt, the lodge's advice. We sleep on reed mats. At night I hear the carts, and the whispering at the foot of the stone, and later, the sweeping.

**What do you believe about the god, and about what happens when you die?**
The god's under the crossing. You can feel it through your feet at the stone. When I die, Grandfather Dust comes and sweeps me out of my house, and then the five houses come and argue about me, and I've told my daughter to charge them. She'll have the east ditch. She'll know what to charge.

**What is an ordinary day for you, and what is your holiest day?**
Clear the ditch. Walk the east road to the bend and back. Count who comes and tell it to whichever house pays that week. Holiest is the Share Fair, at the fourth morning after the Long Field's day. All five marks come to Kerrow, or four do, and they set up their tables round the stone. The Order sells salt and buys cloth, the Wound sells bread and weighs children for a coin, the House sells clean water, the lodge sells nothing and reads for free. The Peak's table is empty. There's a bowl on it every year.

**What do you fear, and what will you not talk about?**
I fear the mark coming back bigger than I can scrub. Every thaw it takes longer. This year I scrubbed till noon, and it came back by dusk, and it had a lip. I won't talk about the sweeping. Who sweeps the hollow at the foot of the stone, after the names go in. I was asked that by the Reader, and I told him to sit up and see, and he did, and he remembers the dusk and the morning. So you have my answer.

**What do you make of the other orders, and of the waystones?**
The Order is polite and takes everything. The Wound is kind and takes everything. The House is sad and takes nothing and gives you a paper doll for your door, and the doll turns over. The lodge knows when you'll die and won't say. The Peak asked for names, and now doesn't, and the names go in the hollow anyway. The eighth mark is the same as the stones on the moor. You know. The ones pilgrims walk into. It's the same ring. I've counted the teeth. There's a gap on the stone, same as on the moor.

**Tell me one small true thing no scholar would write down.**
When the houses break a clause, they come to us afterwards and pay us to say the stone did it first. The Order paid for the thighbone standing up on its own. The Wound paid for the stone bleeding. The lodge paid us to remember the carvers were slow. We took all three. We've never once been paid by the House. I think the ditch that went dry paid for itself.

**The chips of the eighth clause were warm when you found them. What did you do with them?**
Swept them into my apron and took them home. Every keeper did the same, all five, without asking each other. We put them in the hearth-hollow, under the fire-stone, where you put the things you don't want Grandfather Dust to take. I've looked at them since. There are more than there were. A few more. Enough that I know. My daughter says I should give them to the Order, since they like a count that grows. I'll give them to nobody. They were cut out of the stone for a reason, and the stone's still warm where they came from.

### Notes for the lore bible

### Places

- **Four Wents**: crossing of the Barrow road and the salt road; the reeve's hall sits on the salt-road side. A **soft place** in the middle of the crossing, warm, frost will not lie on it; measured each assize-month with a forearm-knotted cord: four knots the first year, eleven now.

- **Sallow Lip**: a daughter-house of the Brotherhood of the Precious Wound (the Chapter of the Open Side), three days east of Four Wents on the salt road; its walls are its bricked-in dead, a cup in the plaster at each mouth; warm all winter without a fire; holds the great scale.

- **Tarn Hollow**: a round lodge of the Polished Heart on a round water west of the Barrow country; readers sit in a ring facing inward; a lowest room under the tarn keeps something in glass.

- **The Chapter-House of the Kostyak**, on the fourth stair of the Ossa: sleeping-rooms a man's length and a hand more, bone-shelf beds.

- **The purifiers' walk**: stilt-rooms behind the Well-Shrine; rush mats changed every ninth day because they take up breath.

- **The keepers' row at Kerrow Cross**: five turf houses, one keeper per road-ditch, the office passed mother to daughter.

- **The moor-road tavern**: where the forty or so sellswords who serve all five houses drink.

### Beliefs and rites

- **Dust Night** (lowland): at the dark of the year every house sweeps inward, door to hearth, and leaves the door open until the fire dies so Grandfather Dust can take what he is owed.

- **The Feast of the Laying** (Pale Order): the year's bones go up the nine stairs; the Count is read aloud from the Ninth Stair, which took seven days when the knight was a novice and takes nine now.

- Pale Order novices lay their birth-name in a niche with their first teeth so the Hush has nothing to unsay.

- **The Night of the Weighing** (Precious Wound): each brother stands on the great scale and gives back in blood a cup for every stone gained; older brothers sometimes owe more than they have.

- **The Small Cutting** (House of Eight Million) replaced the Great Cutting-Away: breath into paper boats, burned on a dry bank, silence until the ash is cold.

- **The Night of Forty** (Polished Heart): every lodge mirror uncovered for the space of one name said slowly.

- A House purifier's fan opened and closed means: "I will breathe that in later, alone."

- A paper doll turns face down when what it holds is heavier than the paper.

### Daily life (food, dress, work, sayings)

- Lowland food: salt-bread, root pottage, white caps from barrow-lids (the young no longer eat them).

- The Wound's **sweet bread** is made with the Maiden's pale fat from the wound-lips and never goes stale; lowland mothers put a loaf under the cradle; Pale Order brothers eat it on the road and say grace in nines over it.

- The Polished Heart buys warm polishing-ash off the Ashen Moor from the carters of Four Wents and pays in threads read, so those carters know when they will die, and are too calm to bargain.

- **The Share Fair** at Kerrow Cross on the fourth morning after the Long Field's day: Order sells salt and buys cloth; Wound sells bread and weighs children for a coin; House sells clean water in clay flasks; lodge reads for free; the Peak's table stands empty with a bowl on it.

- **The ferry**: the Order leaves a purse counted to nine on the Well-Shrine step each spring; the House pins a paper doll on each dredger. The House calls it pilgrims' alms. Neither book records it.

- The houses hire the same forty sellswords, who stand in the ditch together when houses clash; the houses pay extra for this and call it keeping the peace.

- Ditch-keepers are paid by each house to watch the other four, and paid again after a broken clause to say the stone did it first.

- Sayings: "Hunger is honest" (Wound). "Letters wear. Figures do not." (Order). Lowland children: a reader's mirror shows who will bury you.

### Things they disagree about

- Whether the House takes Order coin: the knight says it does (the ferry); the purifier denies it, then lets her fan admit it.

- What the barrow-lids do: the brother says the lowland barrows are mouths that eat the dead slowly and graceless; the reeve refuses to say.

- Who took Villa Llaga's tithe-cup: the daughter-houses set it at the closed gate each year; "someone in our robes" takes it.

- Who kept watch over the Long Field dead: five books, five answers; the Peak's book unseen; the ditch-keepers hum a verse about a sixth watcher.

- The four claimants each say they had their share; the reeve says the man went down.

### Threads worth pulling

- The knight counted 207 in the dead man and has since tied a loose, uncounted knot at the end of his own cord (knight).

- The reeve's boots turn their toes toward the soft place every night and have never needed mending (reeve).

- The thread-reader saw the dead man's thread pulled taut straight down, as if something below was winding it (thread-reader).

- The brother put his ear to the soft place and heard "supper" (reeve, brother).

- The Reader's rubbings of the Kerrow stone: the words stay the same size, the stone between them grows each year (Reader of Stones).

- The Reader's lost night: a rubbing taken while he slept, his hand black to the wrist; he has never unrolled it (Reader of Stones).

- The eighth clause uses an old lowland letterform no living carver makes; its mark matches the waystone rings, gap and all (Reader, Wenna).

- The warm chips from the eighth clause, hidden under five keepers' fire-stones, are more than they were (Wenna).

- Holding a glass over a waystone shows the pit twice, one under the other; a pilgrim shows in the lower one first (thread-reader).

- A waystone breathes out and never in (purifier).

- Tomas came down from the Ninth Stair, returned the coin, sat a month in the middle of rooms away from every wall, then walked back up the Ossa unarmed (sellsword).

- Agna may have lost her finger at the Ashwake waystone and no longer asks which (sellsword).
