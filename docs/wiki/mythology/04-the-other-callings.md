# The Other Callings: Glass, Blood and Breath

*These are the stories of three faiths as they are told at Kerrow Cross on the fourth morning, when the faiths of the Hide come to the truce-stone and those who have sat at all their fires tell a newcomer whose dead belong to whom. Each faith tells its own beginning; where others tell it differently, the teller says so.*

---

## Part One: The Veiled Crone, and Those Who Keep the Glass

### The First Thread

Before the Veiled Crone, her readers say, everything was dry and unmoving, and nothing was tied to anything else. Then she came, and no one asks from where. She drew the first thread out of the nothing and tied the first knot, and the knot was the world. Every life since is a length of that one thread.

She has four faces. The Spinner draws a life out. The Weaver sets it in the cloth among the others, so that a dyer is knotted to a carter and the carter to a child, and a whole valley is one weave. The Unraveller takes it out when its length is done. The fourth is the Hollow: the eye of her loom, the empty place in the middle of the weave where the pattern shows through. Every life goes back through the Hollow at the end, the circles taught, and is woven again. Her eldest readers know an older name, Yh'Anuul, and say it seldom, because a name said too often wears thin like any thread.

The readers tell the fall of the old world as a slackening. In the age before ours the weave held tight, and the world held with it; then the threads began to go slack, the way cloth goes slack on a frame when the hand at the treadle tires. Whose hand, and why it tired, they do not say.

The oldest teaching of the Crone calls the Silence the Mouth With No Lips, and says it hears its name as you hear yours across a crowded room, so the readers do not say it. Since the loose winter a newer reading is whispered: that the Silence is not outside the weave at all but is the Hollow itself, and that every meaning in the cloth is held in the gaps between the threads. The old readers call that heresy.

### The Circles in the Valley of Cold Lakes

The Crone's people sit in circles, and their circles meet in lodges built where her threads come up through the skin of the world like roots through a path. The greatest stood at the head of a narrow valley with a chain of lakes down its floor, each colder than the one above.

Forty-one sat in that lodge's ring on worn stones, and the eldest sat in the middle. At dusk the circle-keeper lit the one candle and began the first name. *Veiled Crone. Spindle. Weaver. Tangle. Knot.* The names went round from mouth to mouth until they came back from the far side and no one could tell whose mouth they had left. By then, the teaching went, no one was sitting; there was only the saying, and remembering and the self were one thread.

Their work was the glass. A dying thread wants to see where it goes, so the readers polished mirrors with grey felt and ash, one of the Crone's names to every stroke, until the glass gave back the pattern. And at a death the glass was covered. This was the lesson the whole valley paid the lodge for: a soul that meets its own face stops to look. So when someone died the women went from room to room with grey cloths over their arms and covered every mirror, pail and window, a stone on each corner, so the soul would pass its reflection by and go on through the Hollow into the cloth. The eldest wore the coat: iron, sewn with small round mirrors, every eldest sewing on more, until it weighed what two grown people weigh.

### The Loose Winter

It came in the week the ford froze, in the year eleven hundred and six by the Ashwake reckoning. The whole of the Lower Reach came loose between one frost and the next, every dyer and child together, and down the valley people felt a slackness, like a bowstring cut. In the lodge the names went round the ring and came back short, as though someone had stopped saying them, though all forty-one mouths still moved.

The eldest put on the coat and looked into the Hollow, and the ring said the names around her for forty nights. She found the threads of the dead hanging out of the Hollow like the ends of a cut hem, and nothing taking them up. She looked for the frame of the loom, because a loom has sides, and found threads crossing threads, and a pattern that was nearly a face, and behind it another.

On the fortieth night she spoke, and the circle-keeper has given her words ever since exactly as she said them: *"There is no loom. It goes on and on, and it never finishes."* Then her hands opened, and whatever she was holding ran out of them, and she was lighter than the coat.

That night the dead came home: everyone the valley had laid in the barrows, and behind them dead so old they had only the shape of someone standing. Nobody saw them walk in. They were in the lane the way frost is on a pane, and they stood at every door and asked one thing: *Which way?* So the valley told them *up*, to the lodge, and walked behind them.

In the yard the ring sat round a dead woman in a coat of glass, and nobody answered. Then someone pulled the felt off the coat, every mirror at once. The dead looked, and the valley heard a sound like a breath drawn in by a whole ring of people, and they stopped, each at its own face. Then they were small pale lights, each trailing a thread, circling the one who had uncovered the glass.

Whose hand that was, the tellings do not agree. The ford inn says the foundling who had polished the coat for twenty years; others say the keeper, or one of the ring; the circles that still cover their glass say such a hand should have no name. What it did next all agree on. It hung the coat on its post and asked the dead into the iron, and the coat stood up as a knight twice a person's height, with a lamp burning where the heart-glass had been, and walked out of the lower gate after the kept dead. The glass, they say, keeps its own count.

One of the dead did not stop. She had been blind all her life, and had no face to stop at. She went through the far wall of the yard and on up the valley in the dark, asking. Nobody has seen her since.

### The Covered and the Unveiled

Since that winter the Crone's faith is split, and the split runs through families. The Covered still cover every glass at a death and send the dead on, faster and more fiercely than before, and burn uncovered glass where they find it. The loom is there, they say, and saying so is the work. The Unveiled took the felt off. They believe there is no loom, and that the lodge sent the valley's dead on for a thousand years, a tithe paid to nothing, into something with no end in it. So they keep them, at their own faces, in glass.

From the Unveiled comes the calling of the Hollow Mystic: a reader of glass who keeps souls rather than sending them. Who takes it up is that one's own affair; the circles say only what the calling does. It reads threads in polished glass and bends them. It sets down mirrors that catch a soul at its own face, for the oldest law holds for Covered and Unveiled alike: a soul cannot pass its own reflection. Its kept dead circle it as wisps, and some of its people walk beside an iron coat of glass that stands up full of the kept and does not die for good.

At the end comes the Still Winter: every thread that ever went through the eye will come back up its valley and stand at its door asking which way. The Unveiled say the dead will stop at their own faces and be home. The Covered say the weave will finish its cloth and fold it. Either way, nothing moves again.

### The Quarrels of the Glass

The Pale Order and the readers despise each other's ways in almost the same words. A reader at Tarn Hollow calls the Order's standing dead a desecration: a frame with no thread in it, walking, like a loom still knocking after the Weaver has left the room. A knight of the Order calls the readers' sin worse: they keep the soul and leave the bone in the heather for the frost to split, and he names it neglect in the same voice.

With the Bleeding Maiden's people the quarrel is over the dead themselves. The processions give the dead back into flesh, where no thread is ever read again; the readers keep the soul and give the Mother nothing. Each calls the other's dead stolen.

And the servants of the Silence show nothing in a mirror. No lodge, the readers say, ever let one past its door alive. They do not say how they know.

## Part Two: The Bleeding Maiden, and Those Who Walk in Procession

### The Wound That Gave

The faithful of the Bleeding Maiden say the world began as a wound. Something opened and did not close, and what came out of it was flesh, and the flesh grew, because nothing told it to stop. What was wounded, or by what, they do not say. The wound is enough.

The Maiden is that first opening, a wound that gives. The Mother is the growing. The Nurse is the third face, who tends what the Mother makes. The Elders know an older name, Nol-Shogthuth.

Above all the faithful believe that the flesh has no head. The Mother grows and makes, but does not know what she makes or for whom. So they give their blood back into the wound with the Words said over it, to tell her whom it is for and what it was spent on. *Blood given is not blood taken*, they say, and blood given without a word is given to no one.

Of the fall of the old world they remember nothing, and say so. The wound opened and gave; then something happened that none of them can tell, and when the telling picks up again the tithe-cup was in their hands, come down to them from somewhere.

Of the Silence they speak with their eyes on the ground. It unmakes flesh, and where its servants pass nothing is left for the Mother to take back. Their own end, the Filling, is its end too: one day the flesh will fill every hollow there is, every wound and grave and room, and the gap between one word and the next, and when nothing is hollow there will be no *then*. The faithful call this the Mother made whole. They say it carefully.

### The Rule and the Measure

The Maiden's faithful are processions, and each keeps a chapter along an open wound in the world. The greatest was Villa Llaga, a town built down both lips of a wound in the Hide as long as a morning's walk, which never closed. The gutters of both its streets fell inward to the wound. Pilgrims went home saying they ran red. Those who lived there said they ran the colour of old brown ink, and that anyone who said red had never been.

Its members are hoods, known by their place and not their names: the twenty-ninth hood, the fortieth. The Elder walks at the front. At the back walks the bloodletter, who opens the others' welts with a small blade and keeps the tally of what each has given, and by the oldest rule never opens themself: sleeves down, arms dry, for life. The reason is told to each bloodletter on the night they take up the blade, and to no one else.

The Rule hangs on a chain in the chapter room. Once a year, on the Day of the Measure, each hood gives one measure of blood, the tithe-cup of beaten tin filled to the line, poured slowly from the lip of the cup to the lip of the wound with the Words said over it. Where the wound asks more, the Elder raises the line a thumbnail's width, and it has asked more in every year that is written. Inside the crypt door many hands chalked the same line one under the next: *she asked a little more.* The lowland Tithed, watching the hoods go by, say they are the tithe themselves, and that the cup only keeps the count; the hoods say a tithe is what you give, not what you are.

By day the hoods walk forty to a procession with their own gait, the sway, the stop, the sway, laying on the scourge in time with the song, nine strokes to a verse. By night they go down the crypt stair two by two with candles, and the saints come down to meet them, into carved images made for them. In the niche above the stair stands the Maiden, carved with her side open and her face left rough from the axe, and no hand may finish it. The chapter's dead lie in its own wall, each with their robe on a peg beside them, and no knight of the Pale Order may enter that crypt or count what lies there.

### The Year of the Crust

There came a year when the wound at Villa Llaga closed under a black crust, ridged underneath like the roof of a mouth, and for that year the hoods poured their measure onto the crust, and it drank. Why it went as it did, no one agrees. Some say the Words were not said, or not enough of them, and the bleed-wives of the mire say that giving to her in silence is exactly how headless children are made. Some in the Open Vein say it was no mistake at all.

On the fast-day the crust split, and what came up was a year of the town's blood grown into the Maiden's children: low, many, without minds. They went up both lips of the town in the dark, and when it was over there was no Villa Llaga. The children dragged the dead hoods' robes over themselves and tried to walk in procession, the sway, the stop, the sway, going nowhere, because they were made of a procession's blood and knew nothing else. And they passed some doors by, as if those within had already given.

What came next is the beginning of a calling, and the tellings agree on what was done and not on who did it. Someone of that town opened their own vein for the first time, on the crust, drew a door in their own blood and called the Maiden down into themself. Most say it was the bloodletter, whose veins the Rule had kept shut for a lifetime; others name a hood whose number is no longer said. She took that one's blood and hands and voice, they say, and left them their mind. Then the children pressed together into one great sewn giant, the carved Maiden from the niche was lashed on its back, and the procession went out of Villa Llaga and has not come home.

Below the old Rule, new clauses were written after that year in brown ink, in a hand that leans the other way: *The measure is all of it. The Words are spoken now at the front of the procession, by one who knows what the blood is for. The eleventh clause is struck through. The niche shall hold a face. The procession goes out, and goes out, and goes out. It needs no door. She hears.* The carved Maiden on the giant's back has a face now, though her carver never cut it. Some who have seen her pass say she rides face up, some face down, and nobody knows who turns her.

### The Measured and the Open Vein

The other chapters disown Villa Llaga and fear it. The Measured, Sallow Lip among them, keep the Rule: blood to the line, with the Words, once a year. A gift must be whole to be heard, they say; too much is spilled, not given. They hold that whoever drew the door at Villa Llaga did the one thing the Rule exists to prevent: told the Mother what to make. The Open Vein say the measure starves her and the Words are a fence. They give everything, scourging in the streets, and their processions go out and do not come home. Hidden among them, the Measured whisper, is a heresy whose initiates take the Mother in rather than give to her.

From this faith comes the calling of the Hemomancer, whom the lowlands call the Red Penitent: a blood-adept who opens their own veins, lets the Mother come through, and leads what she makes. Its blood is flung and poured, and falls and pools, and heals the one who stands in it. It pays for everything with its own life. It wears its penances, the cilice, the thorns, the nails, as armour and as weapons, since a penance is only blood given slowly.

### The Quarrels of the Blood

The oldest feud on the Hide lies between the Maiden's people and the Pale Order: two mothers, and one world of dead between them. The Order wants the frame, bone by bone into the niche; the processions want what the bone was wrapped in, given back to the Mother while it is warm. For centuries they have parted the dead between them and never agreed where the one ends and the other begins. A knight does the parting, quick and not unkind, while a penitent sings the Maiden's names over it, and between them the knight's own, because the chapters carry every knight's name in their litanies so that she will know whom to take first when the time comes. The penitent means it kindly. The knight hears a threat.

With the Myriad it is war, and also trade. The purifiers hold that flesh grown without a word is the worst wrong breath in the world, and go to the open wounds of the Hide to close them; after the year of the crust one came to Villa Llaga to close its wound. Yet the purifiers need wrong breath to take in, and no one makes more of it than the Mother. The Maiden's people are the Myriad's worst enemy and, grimly, its best supplier.

## Part Three: The Myriad, Who Give the Dead to the Water

### The Long Out-Breath

The Myriad tell the beginning as a breath. The great one breathed in once, long, and every small god was inside that breath, warm, together, one sound. Then it began to breathe out, and the world is the out-breath. Every stone, ford and bell is a small god riding it on the way out. What the great one was, they do not say; it matters less what breathes, the purifiers say, than that the breath is leaving and we live in it.

The small gods are past counting; the Myriad say eight million, and stopped counting long ago. Their faith has no true name, because it is not one being. Each small god gave its name to the wind on the way out and is proud of it, and to ask one its name is an insult.

What goes wrong in the world, the Myriad say, is breath gone wrong. They call it miasma: the essence of a thing twisted out of true, left wherever someone moved a boundary stone, cursed a neighbour's well or grew flesh without a word. Wrong breath can be taken in, and carried, and given back.

Of the Silence the Myriad know more than they like. Every purifier learns the stillness at the bottom of an out-breath, before the next comes in, and learns that something is written there; the elders do not say what. But they fear the servants of the Silence above anything. A purifier can hold a wrong breath until it bends her. She cannot hold an absence, and where those servants pass there is nothing left in the air to take in.

### The Shrine and the Folding

The Myriad keep shrines: two posts and a lintel at a crossing, a stone at a spring, a strip of white paper knotted to a bell, wherever the wind snags on something. Every shrine has a guardian stone facing the road. When a shrine is forgotten its stone turns to the wall, a finger's width a year, and no hand moves it. A purifier who finds one kneels, turns it back with both palms and breathes on its face until the dust stirs, and the air clears; that is why common folk call them Shrine Keepers.

The first of the shrines is the Well-Shrine, at the one clean pool in the Drowned Fen, and its day-book counts the years by Cuttings, one to a year.

A novice becomes a purifier at her folding: she is given a new name, and her birth-name is written into a paper figure and burned. She ties a knotted cord at her wrist and learns the rite. The figure is folded by lamplight, never at noon, its arms left open; a figure with closed arms has already refused, and is burned without watching the smoke. The one who comes to be cleansed sits knee to knee with the purifier and breathes on it three times, and on the third breath the paper leans toward them a little, as if called. That is the wrongness leaving by the door it came in. The purifier breathes it in and holds it, and ties a knot. Then she opens her fan a rib at a time, breathes out across it and turns it toward whoever made the wrongness. The fan tells the breath where to go; a breath only knows how to leave.

The Myriad give their dead to the water. A body that has finished breathing must be let go like any breath, so that what is left of its last one can go on outward with the rest. And once a year, at the Great Cutting-Away, the purifiers of the Well-Shrine breathed the whole year's wrongness into the Fen, trusting the water to carry it away. For two hundred years the day-book says the water was clear by noon.

### What the Water Gave Back

The water carried nothing away. Under the Fen beyond the Well-Shrine lies the Drowned Nave, a great hall the water took long ago, and the boatmen notch their poles to mark how near its roof has come. Everything the Myriad gave the water, the year's wrong breath and their dead alike, sank into the Nave and stayed there.

In the two hundred and fourth Cutting, at the thaw, a novice drew a bucket from the well and saw her face reversed in it, and drew again and saw it reversed again, and this time it looked right. By the third day the water was grey, every reed leaned away from the shrine with no wind blowing, and the guardian stone at the gate faced the wall, and four purifiers could not turn it back. The Nave was full, and was giving everything back up the spring.

Seven purifiers went down the next morning, counting the Nave stair aloud. Those on the step counted thirty-three steps and heard thirty-four. A girl who had not been sent was gone from her mat. At noon the next day six of the seven stood at the Kneeling Arch with their hands full; they stood a long while, then turned and walked off into the water with what they had taken, their faces going long and hard, the horns coming in. At dusk one came up, carrying the girl who had not been sent. In the morning the water was clear and the stone faced the road again, though no hand had turned it.

What was done with the girl the day-book does not write. The Myriad whisper that the one who came up breathed in everything at the spring, the century of wrong breath and the warp in the girl and the grief of the drowned, and held it; that while she held it the clock above the altar did not tick; and that the girl's body lay down on the step, dry though it had come out of the water, and something stepped into the haze beside the one who had come up, reversed, as things are in water.

Why the Nave filled, the Myriad tell two ways. Some purifiers say the Pale Order had already been down there: that its knights dredged the Myriad's drowned out of the Fen and stood them in the Nave, and the raised bone came up bent, and it was that which turned the water. The day-book itself says the knights came three Cuttings after, with hooks and chain, paid the boatmen and carried out more dead than the book had ever given to the water. Both tellings are kept. Neither forgives the Order.

No Cutting-Away has been held since. The day-book asks where the year's wrong breath goes now, and answers itself: *it goes into us.*

### The Folded and the Horned

Every purifier knows how she will end. Every held breath leaves a little of itself behind, and the cord at her wrist grows longer than the knots she remembers tying. The face goes long and hard; the horns come in slowly, like a child's teeth. One morning she stands up from the shrine step with her hands full of everything she took and walks down, always down, into the water and the deep ground under the Hide, carrying it there so the rest need not. The reed-camps call what she becomes one of the Horned. She knows her time is near when the guardian stones begin to turn toward her.

The Folded are the orthodox purifiers, who turn wrong breath back by the rite, keep count on their cords, serve as long as they have room, and fear the day they do not. The Horned have begun to turn and stopped resisting it. The ending every purifier comes to, they say, is the true office: to carry the world's wrong breath down into the water and keep it there. The Folded will not speak their names.

From the Myriad comes the calling of the Shrine Keeper: a purifier running out of room. The calling breathes wrongness out of the world and holds it, and lets it out again as a drifting haze that sickens what stands in it and makes blows miss. It tells breath where to go with the fan, and turns what was done back on whoever did it. Some who take it up walk with a reversed presence in the haze beside them, and no one asks its name. And when one of them strikes to kill, the elders say, the small gods of the dying gather round her as pale omens, and she spends them in the finishing blow.

At the end, the out-breath will finish, and every small god will settle like dust. A purifier who has already walked down into the water will come back up and breathe the world in, once, as the last rite, and the world will be folded like a paper figure with its arms left open and set on a step, and nothing will come to breathe on it.

### The Quarrels of the Breath

With the Pale Order the Myriad curse each other in the lanes and pay each other on the step. A traveller once saw a knight lay a purse on the step of the Well-Shrine and turn away, and a purifier came down behind him and pinned a paper figure between his shoulder-blades, and he let her. The Myriad call such purses pilgrims' alms.

The Crone's lodges are cousins, which is harder. Both keep something of the dead that the knight and the penitent leave behind. But to the Myriad a soul held in a mirror is a breath held forever, and no one knows better than a purifier what holding does to the one who holds. On the salt road a purifier and a reader once shared a fire, and when the reader uncovered his hand-glass to see by, she opened her fan and shut it again, which among the Myriad means *I will take that in later, alone.* Neither of them slept.

And the Silence, which all three faiths on this page fear above the others, and each has been glad of once. A village at the edge of the Fen went wrong past all mending, its wells tasting of old coins, past anything a purifier could take in, and the Order would not touch its bones. Then a servant of the Silence went through it. Afterwards there was nothing wrong in that village, and nothing else in it at all. The purifier who had stood at the end of the lane bowed to the empty street before she left it.
