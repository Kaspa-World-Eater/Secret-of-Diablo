# Legends of the First Lands

*These are the tales told at the banked fire in Maren's camp on the Ashen Moor, to pilgrims just come in off the road,
about the places they must walk first. The old ones of the camp tell them, and the man who is always already sitting at
the fire when you arrive listens and corrects no one.*

## The Cheek

Put your hand flat on the ground before anything else. Warm? It is always warm. This is the Ashen Moor, and the old
people call it the Cheek, because here the god's face struck when it came down. The ash is warm because the flesh under
it is still cooling. It has been cooling for a thousand years and more, and it has not finished.

The grandmothers tell how our people came here. They knelt inside the god when it was alive, in a warm room wider than
the Moor, and sang to it. When it died around them they got up off their knees, took hold of each other's belts in the
dark, and walked a long road until they came to a door that opened and shut, opened and shut. That was the mouth. The
god's last breath was going out of it, and our people walked out on the breath and fell down here on the cheek, and
kissed the ash. That is why the camps of the Moor keep their fires low and banked: you do not scorch the place where you
were let out.

The black glass in the ash is where a Husk bled: the ash drank the blood and hardened, and the glass stays warm in the
hand.

A surveyor who drew roads for this camp came to believe the ground draws in with the cold and lets out with the warm, the
way a face does. One winter he laid all his sheets edge to edge on the floor of the tithe-barn and saw that he had
drawn a face, the Moor at the cheek and the Fen at the throat. His early sheets and his late ones disagreed in one way
only. The face had turned a little, toward the camp. After that his pen drew the eyes closed, and he let it.

The first lantern lit on the Moor after the fall hangs over Maren's camp, and it has never once been put out. How the camp began, or who lit that lantern,
Maren the Gravekeeper does not say.

There are two rules of the Cheek, and the children learn them before they can walk. You do not sing on the Moor at night,
not even to yourself. And you keep your own name behind your teeth. The grandmothers give the reason for both in one
breath: the things out there know the tune.

## The Lanterns That Are Someone

No lantern in the lowlands burns oil. You will forget this inside a week, because they look like any lamp, so the
tenders say it plainly to every newcomer: every flame on every road is somebody. A pilgrim who fell on the road. A mother
out of the crypt niches. Most often someone nobody remembers. When they died, the Last Breath went out of them with
everyone else's, down and away the way water finds a low place, and they did not go with it. They stayed, and were kept
in glass. The camp calls them the Wickbound.

How a soul chooses to stay, nobody can say for certain. The old tenders say the dying soul looks back over its shoulder,
sees someone still walking the road, and the ones who look back are the ones who stay. In the lowlands there is an older tale, told low, of a company of craftsmen called the Wick-Binders, who were the
first to keep a soul in a lantern and were hunted out for it long ago. Whether the souls chose, in those days, the tale
does not say. The tenders will not tell it at their own stones.

A lantern chooses whom it follows. At the lantern-stones one of them leans toward a traveller, and when the traveller
walks on it goes too, a little behind and a little to one side, the way a friend walks with you after dark. It cannot be
struck or ordered.

When its traveller falls, the lantern fetches them home. It comes in low off the Moor, lays what it carries in the warm
ash beside the stones, and the traveller gathers themselves up out of it. The lantern gives you back. It keeps a little,
as it always does, and what it keeps is what it burns: a way of whistling, a sister's name, the second verse of a
harvest song. The tenders weigh their lanterns on the tithe-scale while the travellers sleep. Some come home heavier. The
numbers are written inside the lid of a tin, and nobody adds them up.

So the tenders wipe every glass every morning and tell the ones inside the weather. Scratched on nearly every lantern on
the road are the words *Dust on the glass. Wipe it. It is someone.* No tender has ever seen anyone cut them.

The oldest lantern-tale of the camp is the Sighing Lantern, an old bronze lamp with a cracked bell in it. Where it stands
depends on who tells it: the beggar at the ninth stone wipes it every morning at the Moor crossroad, the tenders put it
at the far end of their round past the Widow's Stumps, and the folk of the Ridge say it hums on their crest. For longer than anyone can count it walked with a woman in grey,
and fetched her home more times than a tender's teacher's teacher could notch. Then one evening it came in off the Moor
alone, set itself down on its stone, and never followed anyone again. The tenders say it lost her. Others say it had
carried her home so many times that at last there was nothing left of her to set down, and so she is in there with it.
Scratched small at the foot of its glass is a name, Aveth, and after it: *She was kind about the road.* Since that
evening it has breathed, in and out, slower than you or I, and in a wind it hums through its cracked bell.

In some seasons the Sighing Lantern falls quiet, and the camp says that something comes to the crossing then and drinks
the light before it reaches the road. Warden-Crone Esk has sent more than one pilgrim to kneel at it and hold it until
the drinkers are gone. When it sings again she says it sings badly, and that it always did.

## The Foreman and His Daughters

On the Sighing Ridge, among the broken colonnades, stand three stumps that are always warm. The camp calls them the
Widow's Stumps.

A caravan was crossing the Ridge, in a year nobody can now name, and its foreman had his two daughters with him, one by
each hand. Something came on them on the crest. Some say Husks; some say the wind off the Ridge, which sounds like
breathing and sometimes is. The foreman did not let go. He died holding both his daughters' hands, and when they were
found there were three warm stumps on the crest, a father's hand and two daughters' hands, never let go.

That is how the Ridge tells it. In the camp they tell one thing more, quietly. One of the daughters let go, and lived.
Her name is Nell, and for a long time the drowned kept her in the village under the Fen, and nobody could say what they
wanted with her. When at last she was brought out, she asked that no one be told she had let go, and offered to teach
whoever freed her to stand as her father stood. So there are three stumps on the Ridge, and the Ridge will not say which
of them is empty.

## The Things That Were the God

Every grandmother on the Cheek tells the children why the things out there want us, and she tells it the same way. Every
Hand that creeps on its fingers, every Gasp in the wind, every Weeper by the barrows: all of it was the god once. When the
god died, each piece forgot what it belonged to. A finger forgot the hand. A breath forgot the mouth. They remember one
thing only: being warm, with people kneeling inside them, singing. Then they smell us. Some want us back inside, where we
were. Some want to put us on like a coat, and kneel, and be us. Either way they are homesick, and we are the only home
they have left to smell. They do not hate you, the old ones say. That would be simpler.

The **Husks** you meet first. The gravediggers say they were pilgrims who found the red places on the Moor, where the god
still bleeds near the surface, and drank. Others in the lowlands say a Husk is one of our own who was tithed hollow:
who gave and gave, to a faith or to the toil of keeping the world going, until there was nothing left in them to give.
The tithe was taken, they say, and what is left of a tithe does not lie down. Both tellings agree on what a Husk does. One stands like a post for days. Two stand closer together.
When there are three, the heads snap up all at once, the arms go wide, and they come. So the diggers say: see one, walk;
see two, run. If you must fight, do it in a doorway, one at a time.

The **Tithe-Hands** are hands the size of a man, walking on their fingers, coming round behind you in threes and fives.
Nobody tells where they come from, only that the god had many hands and kept none of them. Put a wall at your back, and
when the fingers spread, strike.

The **Weepers** are mourners with no faces. They keep far off, behind a broken wall or the Grieving Stone, and when the
head tilts back the tears come out hard and white, needles of bone a finger long. A digger of the Barrows once pulled
eleven out of his own coat. Whose mourners they were, no one has ever said. Up close one
only stands there and shakes.

The **Gasps** you feel before you see. The air leans toward something. Then there is a veil of small mouths, every one
breathing in, coming through a barrow wall as though the wall were mist. They are scraps of the god's breath gone astray,
and they cannot bear light. Keep your lantern high and near. If one comes through the wall at you, the hunters say,
breathe out, and give it nothing to take.

## The Kneelers

Out on the Moor, past the long rise the surveyor called the Jaw, a stone prayer-bench lies broken clean in two. This is
the Broken Kneeler. Something fell on it the night the god died, they say, and nobody has moved the halves since. On the Ash Shore there is another halved bench beside the grey water, and the shore folk call it by
the same name. The Moor and the Shore have never agreed which is the true one.

The other Kneelers are flesh: penitents on their knees in the crypt mouths and along the road, praying, and never
finishing. The pilgrims say each one crawled the last league of some road on its knees and never rose. Their knees are
strapped to kneeling-boards, a slab of carved prayer-stone is roped along each back, and a votive candle stands on each
bald crown with the wax run down over the eyes.

The digger of Widow's Lane has never seen one rise, but he once saw a Kneeler a hundred paces further down the road than
it had been the morning before. Kneel nowhere near one, he says, and pray where it cannot hear you, because it is
listening for how. On Low Day, the dark of the year, the whole of Widow's Lane comes down with lanterns, stands one at the
foot of every barrow, and says a list nine times over: *lie down, lie still, lie low, lie long, till the Kneeler
finishes.* That is the fear of the Lane. Whatever the Kneeler is praying, the digger says, it has nearly got it. Nobody
knows what happens when it finishes.

## The Barrows and Widow's Lane

The Barrows are where the Tithed bury their own, nine barrows to a ring and nine of a family to each barrow. Every lid in
the Barrows is dished inward, like a cheek sucked hollow. Earth settles at the edges; these lids sink from the middle,
deeper at dawn than at dusk, and a new lid laid flat goes the way of the rest within a year. The diggers say the ground
there breathed out once and never in. Draw a line from the deepest part of each dish and every line runs to the ring of
tooth-stones east of the Lane. In the lowlands it is whispered now that the lids have begun to lift. The diggers do not
repeat it.

The dead of the Barrows rise wrong. They come up clean, cleaner than they were laid, and walk to their own houses and
stand at the door. The mother under the Turned Mound came up three times, each time a little further along Widow's Lane,
facing home. The diggers put them back face down with a flat stone on the neck and say no name over them, because the
name is what they follow.

Then there is the Empty Ninth. A digger opened the ninth barrow of his own ring to lay a new death in it and found none of
his own dead inside. It was full of other bones, long and clean, laid in rows like a wall set by someone careful, and more
of them than the barrow had room for. He gave them to the knights of the Pale Order when they rode down that spring, and
one knight wrote a number, crossed it out, wrote another, and they took the load up the mountain fast and said nothing.
The lid of the Empty Ninth is the only flat lid in all the Barrows. The digger has dug his own place in it, and sleeps
near it, because there, he says, his breath goes out the way it should. The villagers say the Empty Ninth is waiting.
They do not say for whom.

The **Bellwethers** walk the Barrows too: great hulks with their heads shut up in bells. A Bellwether tolls twice and
comes straight on, and if you stand before stone and step aside on the second toll, it strikes the stone and stands
ringing. Names are cast round the rims of the bells. The digger read one, and it was the name of a man he had buried
whole and dressed himself. He will not say what that bell was made of, only that it was not bronze. The man who sits at every fire said once that the bell in the ash used to be in a tower, and the tower
did not move; the bell did. He would not say what he meant.

The **Gravebloats** shuffle among the mounds, walking stomachs that swell and burst when they come close, and of
them the diggers tell no story at all. The grandmothers say only that this is what they mean when they say some of the
god's pieces want us back inside.

## The Rib Crypts and the Carrion Warden

Under the edge of the Moor the Rib Crypts go in between the god's ribs, nine galleries, one to each space between them.
The old name is Dur-nang, the inside grave, and the pilgrims call it the Hollow Crypt. The flagstones lift under your
boots, slow and even, and settle again, because something below is sleeping and breathes.

Every niche in the crypt held a mother's bones once. That was where the Moor laid its mothers. Then came what the old ones
call the Great Standing, and the niches were emptied. What the Great Standing was, the camp does not agree. Some say the
mothers stood up and walked out. Some say they were taken. Most will not speak of it.

The crypt has its holy things. The Long Candle has burned at the threshold since before the fall, and leans toward you
when you come near, listening. The Counting Wall is covered with tally-marks in groups of a hundred and eight; every
pilgrim who passes adds one, and nobody living knows what was being counted. Where Sister Un's reliquary rested on its way below, no
dust settles. On some nights
there is singing through the floor. A copyist named Ottelin lay nine nights on the flags to write it down, and on the
ninth night it stopped and waited for him, as a choir waits for a voice that is late. He knew the next line without ever
having learned it, and did not sing it, and climbed the stair with his candle out. The grandmothers keep three low notes of the old song and never sing the fourth. The crypt, they say, knows
the fourth.

In the dark of the crypt lived the **Carrion Warden**. He was set to guard the place, the camp says, and in the long years
he forgot what he guarded and began to eat what the Tithed buried, so that the Moor could no longer lay its mothers in the
niches. Who set him there, and what he guarded before he forgot, nobody can say. It is told that a pilgrim went down at
last and let him stop, and that Esk took the news and said, "Then the Tithed can bury their mothers again," and gave the
pilgrim something that had been his, because he would not want it now.

## The Drowned Fen

Below the Moor lies the Drowned Fen, which the old books call the god's lymph. The water is clear and knee-deep, and it
does not wet you. Put your arm in to the shoulder and it comes out dry, and a little lighter. Every reed bends the same
way, whatever the wind, toward something.

There was a village there once, with wet water and a good well, and an old woman in a reed hut at the Fen's edge, who says
she is the last who can give it as it was, tells how it drowned. Her father let his iron hook down the well in a dry
summer, and it caught, and the rope ran slack and tight and slack, as though something below were breathing on it. He
pulled, and what came up was water, only water, clear as a held breath, and it kept coming, to the sills and the lintels
and the bell. He dropped the hook where he stood. It is still there, the Iron Hook, and the Shrine Keepers of the Myriad
hang a paper figure on it every year. Because the water was dry, the drowning never finished. The water closed over the
village like a lid, and those under it went on.

They are all still there. Look down from the edge of the Sunken Square and the doors stand ajar, every one, the way you
leave a door for somebody coming home late, and when a pilgrim leans over to look, they all stop what they are doing and
look up. The bells ring under the square at dusk, though the belfry fell a thousand years ago; the old woman says she rang
it herself, and that both things are true. The boatmen tie up to the bowed chapel arch to sleep, and call it the Kneeling
Arch.

Out in the western pool stands the Reed That Points Home. Stand beside it and it turns its head toward the village you
were born in, and pilgrims weep at it. The old woman of the reed hut has stood by it too. It points down.

At the one clean pool in the Fen stands the Well-Shrine of the Myriad, and the boatmen bow toward it as they pass. The
lowlands whisper that what the water carried down for a hundred years came back up one year, and the purifiers who went
to meet it did not come back. The Shrine Keepers do not tell that story.

Past the village the Fen gives up pretending to be water and becomes the Sunken Bog, where the mud clutches and lets go.
On a hump of the bog stands a crooked shack with chimney smoke and no fire, its doorstone worn by knees, not feet, because
those who came there came to beg. Hesk the Tallow-Mother lived there, a bog-witch who traded cures for years off a life
and kept her ledger on skin, and who at the last rendered the drowned into candles that walked. When she was put out, Esk
says, all her candles went out at once, and Esk felt it in her teeth.

## The Hollow Wood and the Root Deep

North of the Moor stands the Hollow Wood, the god's veins stood up out of the ground and become a forest. The trunks are
pale and warm a hand's depth in, and if you put your palm to the bark and wait, there is a slow beat in it. Cut one and it
weeps thin and clear, and the cut closes by morning. The hunters never cut them.

Count the trunks as you walk, because every tenth one has grown round something: a bell with only its lip showing, a
skull with the wood come in at the eyes, a candle still lit with the bark drawn back from the flame, a man's ribs with a
pilgrim's badge still warm in them. The old hunters say these are the things the god was carrying when it fell; the bell
was in its hand, the candle in its mouth. One hunter who believed this all his life counted a tenth trunk that had grown
round a pilgrim's lantern, the glass whole and the flame turned toward the road. The year before, that trunk had been a
ninth. He goes round it now.

The **Wick-Saints** come as the light goes, with painted faces, white with a little red at the mouth, and wide sails like
dusted paper. They swoop in a long curve and climb away, and when the sails fold back, one is coming. Strike while it is
low, the hunters say, and never look at the face after. They have kind faces, and that is the worst of them. In the
lowlands it is whispered that the faces were not always theirs: that they belonged to a court that sat in judgment on
souls in some age before, and the Wick-Saints wear them now.

The **Vein-Borers** you know by the ground: a ring of red bubbles, soft as something about to boil. They are severed veins
of the god, still pumping, and they rise under soft ground and never under a road. That is why the roads are where they
are. The young think the Tithed laid the roads. The old say the roads were there first, and the Wood grew away from them
the way skin grows away from a nail.

The one the woodcutters call the **Stalker Crone** keeps to the tall grass where the Wood thins. She is seen only at the
edge of the eye, and she comes when you turn your back. Nobody knows why the woodcutters chose that name, or whose she was
before she was the Wood's. The advice is short: do not turn your back on tall grass.

Every root in the Hollow Wood lies the same way, like hair combed by a hand, toward the mouth in the north where the Root
Deep opens. Its old name is Ne-no-kuni, the land under the Hide. It breathes in and out, and when it breathes out the
whole Wood leans a little toward it.

The Root Deep is courteous. Those who have gone down say they were spoken to politely, answered politely, and came up in
good order. Leave a coin on the milestone at its threshold and the coin will be gone, and something will be kinder to
you. The Horned walk down that road with their sacks, and
nothing walks up it. The lowlands say the Deep keeps a little of everyone who comes back up, as a lantern does.

## The Burnt Heath and the Pyre-Saints

Off the Sighing Ridge the land runs down into the Burnt Heath, its heather burned to wire. It burned for a year after the
god died, and nobody lit it. The **Pyre-Saints** walked there and walk still: martyrs whose faith still burns, the
fire-keepers say, though whose faith it was, and what they died for, nobody on the Heath can tell. They leave burning
footprints, and when the flames on them roar white they break open in fire. In the middle of the Heath is the ash-circle
where a Pyre-Saint knelt to burn, and its centre is still warm. Water puts them out, and pilgrims crossing the Heath keep
near the shallows.

In the corner of the Heath that the road bends away from lie seven great horned carcasses in a ring, every one facing the
same way, with smaller skulls heaped round them, and nobody knows whose. Everyone who comes to the Heath fire
wants to hear about the seven. Old Garrow, who kept the fire at the Broken Bridge, said they were the god's first
thoughts, sent walking to carry something, and they lay down when they got there. Brannoc the smith's girl said they knelt
to something coming up. A knight of the Pale Order said they lay down to hold a door shut with their weight, and went out
to count their bones, and counted twice, and would tell only the first number. The fire-keeper's mother had it in six
words, and the pilgrims carry them now: *The ground between them was opened once, and closed.* The ground there is darker,
and flatter than ground lies, and warm, and lanterns stop at its edge and go no nearer. Garrow went out there in the last
year of his life, and came back and said there was a seat under it, and someone sitting in the seat, very patient, waiting
to be asked.

## The Catacombs and the Ossuary Matron

Beyond the Fen the ground opens into the bone catacombs. The upper one is the pilgrim ossuary, where every wall is a sermon
laid in bone: a wall of thigh-bones, then a wall of jaws, then skull-caps stacked cup-down like tiles, and touch one on the
Cup Wall and ten thousand ring, faintly. Who laid those walls the camp does not know.

The ossuary is kept still. The **Ossuary Wardens**, knights of bone behind tower-shields made of skulls, walk its
corridors and salute each other as they pass, and do not salute you. Somewhere in the dark the **Marrow Duelists**, fencers
of bone, practise their forms for no one. And through the halls drift the **Choristers**, veils like the Gasps but singing
as they go, a slow dirge that drags at your feet. The grandmothers say this is why you never sing on the Moor at night:
some of the god's pieces have learned the tune. Others say the Choristers are the singing from under the crypt flags, come
up where the floor was thin. No one has asked one.

In the Reader's Bay stands a lectern with a book open on it, one page turned, and the hand on the page is yours, whoever
you are. The lowlands tell of a reading-stand somewhere in the ruins of the world with a book on it that is the reader's
own life, written one page ahead, and nobody is sure whether this is that book. Esk read it, once. Her counsel is not to
read it, but to bring it up closed, and let it keep you.

Below the kept bone lies the second catacomb, where the walls forget what patterns were for. The ceiling has sagged into a
face, and you cannot say whose. There is a chamber of skulls the pilgrims call the Miscount: count the skulls, count again,
and there is one more, and some say the one is you. A pick is set in the end wall with its shaft snapped. The camp says the
monks who dug these halls went down and down until they struck something warm, and there they stopped; which monks, they
cannot tell you. The bones down there are warm to the touch, and if you lean on them, the warmth spreads into you.

At the bottom, where the monks struck the warm thing, waits the **Ossuary Matron**. She stands up the dead, the camp says,
and counts the dead she has stood up, and when she is pressed she calls the Duelists and the bone-weepers round her. Whose
matron she was, and what she is counting toward, no one at the fire will guess aloud. Every pilgrim who goes down is told
the same thing: end the count. And it is said that the way out of the first lands, toward the white country of the
Barrens, opens behind her, so that whoever leaves the Hide's first lands leaves them through her chamber.

When a pilgrim comes back from there and sits at the fire, the man who is always already sitting at it says only, "Down.
Always down. The Barrens are waiting, and they are very dry." Then he banks the fire, because somebody always sits up.
