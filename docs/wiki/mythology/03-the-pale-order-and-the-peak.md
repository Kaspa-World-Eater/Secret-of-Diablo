# The Pale Order and the Peak

*These stories are told in two places that do not speak to each other: on the stairs of the Ossa, where the brothers of the Pale Order teach them to novices bent under the yoke, and in the lanes of Lower Stair under the god's knee, where the last of the Gilded Peak's monks and craftsmen tell them to anyone who will sit. Where the two tellings part, both are given.*

---

## The Falling

Before the Count there was no count, and the brothers of the Pale Order will tell you nothing of that time. Ask a tallier what came before, and he will say you have asked a novice's question.

Their story begins with the falling. The god fell, and its Bone, whom they call Oss-Vharoth, the Bearing Mother, came to rest as the frame of the world. The whole Hide hangs on her as a skin hangs on its frame, and she has never once set that weight down. Where her bones break up through the Hide they stand as mountains, and the Ossa are the whitest of them.

The Order says its first brothers stood at her side when she fell. They did not mourn her; they laid her. They went among the fallen bones and set each where it should lie, and from that labour came everything the Order is. So the brothers say the world was not made. It was set down, and it is waiting to be set down again.

Over the Ninth Stair of the great ossuaries, in the Order's own letters, it is cut: WE WERE HERE WHEN SHE FELL. WE WILL BE HERE WHEN SHE RESTS. Some who have copied it swear it reads WE STOOD BESIDE HER. Under those words there is an older line, in older letters, worn almost smooth. Scholars come from the lowlands to copy it, and their copies never match; no two scholars bring home the same number of lines. The brothers do not copy it at all.

A novice who asks who cut the stairs is told: we were here when she fell. But sooner or later every novice goes down to the lowest stair of his ossuary, where each riser stands as high as his knee and is worn hollow in the middle by feet, and he cannot climb it without his hands. The Order says its first brothers cut those stairs, and says no more.

## The Laying and the Count

The holy thing to the Pale Order is not the dead, and not the soul. It is the bone, because the bone is the part that stays. *The flesh is lent,* they say. *The bone is kept.* A bone lying out of its place is an open wound in the world, and it must be carried home.

Home is the ossuaries: nine great ones and a hundred lesser, cut down into the white ridges of the Ossa in stairs and niches, going into the dark. Nine Stairs is the first of them. Each is kept by a Warden, and each Warden is called an Ossuarch, which is the name of the office and of no one man. An Ossuarch keeps the bones of his ossuary, tends its marrow, and lets finish what is ready to finish. Those are the Order's three offices, and every brother serves one of them all his life.

When a bone is brought in, it is given the Laying. It is washed, and anointed joint by joint with marrow, and set in its niche by a brother who speaks its name aloud. If the name is lost, he speaks the name of the bone instead, plainly: the long bone of the left arm, from the lowlands. Nothing goes into the wall nameless. A bone is always held in two hands, because one hand is a grasp and two hands are a bearing. A dropped bone is a sin; a broken bone is a mourning.

But a name is not enough. The Hush, the Silence that unsaid the god, took the god's names first, one at a time, and has been taking names ever since. In the oldest book on the Ninth Stair whole lines stand blank where names were written, and the numbers beside them are still there, in the same ink. The Hush reaches for a number, the Order says, and finds nothing to hold. So every bone laid is given its name, for the Mother, and its number, for the Great Count. In the Order's books a brother is written by his number, never his name.

Every count is carried down to its root, by adding its figures until one figure stands alone. Nine is the Mother's root, because nine keeps itself: take any count nine times, and its root is nine again. So there are nine great ossuaries and nine stairs in each, and prayers are said in nines. A whole man is two hundred and six bones, and two and nought and six come to eight: a man is an open count, one short of the Mother all his life, and he closes only in the niche, when his number is cut beside him. Every novice ties a cord of two hundred and six knots and tells it in the dark until his fingers know the end without him.

A child is born with more bones than a man dies with, and over a life they knit and are fewer. So the fewer is the holier, every frame walks toward the fewer, and the Mother herself seeks the One Bone. When every bone of her is counted, the Count will close at the Last Number, and she will set the world down, and rest. *Rest* is a word the brothers never say of anything that is not yet dust, or of her.

Once a year, at the Feast of the Laying, every wall in every ossuary is counted, the brothers going along with their tallies while the sweepers hold the lamps. And here the novices are told a small and happy thing: every year the count is higher than the year before, and often higher than the dead the brothers carried in. The Warden calls the difference the Mother's gift, and the brothers say the words after him. The gift is kept out of the book, on a second leaf, year under year, in a hand no tallier has ever seen at work. A tallier may add across that leaf. He may never add down it. No reason is given.

The old sweepers tell other stories, only to the young ones who will take their brooms: of a room behind a long wall that no chronicle lists, with niches too long for any body the world has; of a stair counted in youth and again in old age, that has more steps than it did. The Order does not tell them at all.

## The Wells and the Marrow Pontiff

The Order's second office is the marrow, and it is the Order's wealth. Beneath the ossuary floors the brothers have sunk wells into the god's living bone, and the Brothers of the Well turn the windlass, counting in nines, until the rope goes heavy and the bucket comes up full of marrow: amber, slow, and warm however long it is kept. Every year the rope is spliced longer, and the bucket still comes heavy at the same turn. Ask the brothers how deep the wells go, and they will tell you the Mother gives it freely.

From the marrow they render chrism. With it they anoint the bones at the Laying, light the slow lamps of the deep roads, and close wounds that nothing else will close. Lowlanders under the Ossa say the places it heals stay hard and warm, and swell a little with the years.

One well under Nine Stairs, the Fifth, is capped with a stone sealed in chrism, and no brother draws there. The Wardens say the Marrow Pontiff keeps it. That is the Order's highest office, above the Wardens in Agreement, and it is an office that kneels at the bottom of the Fifth Well and never comes up. The Wardens call it the eldest of all things in the ossuaries, and say it went down before the stair was cut. Some brothers whisper that the office has been held by one bone since the beginning, never replaced. Yet a pilgrim woman once came to the gate of Nine Stairs swearing it had risen to her, out of an old well far out on the white ground. What it wants, the Order does not say. In the lowlands there is a story that it asks whoever comes near enough to hear it to kneel, and nobody who tells the story agrees whether that is a prayer, a command or a plea.

## The Standing Dead and the Three Funerals

In this world nothing that was ever the god lies still, and the Order's dead do not rest either. A brother anointed with chrism and set in his niche stands there facing the stair, keeping the vigil, and when a brother asks, the dead step down: to carry bones home, to guard the stair, to go with a Warden into the world. The Order says it does not raise them. It asks, and they were always going to stand.

When a brother passes with a lamp, the skulls in the niches turn a little to follow him. The brothers say the dead are keeping watch. Ask them what for, and they fall silent; in the novices' catechism that question is ruled and left empty. On the lowest stair, where the risers are knee-high, the long skulls do not turn at all. They were already facing the stair when the first sweeper came down to them.

When a brother dies, his flesh is carried up to the high ledges of the Pogost and laid out in the wind, and the cold and the Myriad take it. That is the first funeral. His bones come down again, anointed, and stand in his niche to keep the vigil: that is his second service. And at last, after ages, when his bones have gone brittle and fallen to powder, the sweepers gather the dust, saying the name of the niche as they sweep.

Dust is the holiest thing the Order knows. Everything that was ever the god is still dying, the stone and the stair and every man, and dust alone has finished. A brother may kiss a skull, but over dust he holds his breath. The sweepers are the oldest brothers, and their office is the highest honour the Order has.

Before it goes up, the dust is weighed, and the weights are kept on the Ninth Stair, which only an Ossuarch descends. Every Ossuarch reads them once. What they say, no Ossuarch has ever told. *Ask nothing of the Ninth Stair,* the Rule ends, and the brothers do not.

Then, before first light, the sweepers climb to the Sky Pogost on the highest peak, lift their pans and open their hands, and the wind takes the dust. That is the second funeral, and the last; though the lesson-books, counting the niche as one, call it the third. You must watch the dust until it is gone, the sweepers say, and it is a long time going. In all the ages of the Order, nobody has ever seen it come down.

The sky over the Ossa is paler than anywhere else on the Hide, and the brothers are proud of it. The villages below say it hangs a little lower every year. Pilgrims coming home from the white ridges say, carefully, that the sky over the Ossa is not all sky.

## The Unfallen

Look up on a clear night from the roof of an ossuary, and the brothers will tell you that every cold point of light up there is a piece of the Mother. When she fell, most of her came down to the Hide and was laid where she lay. Some of her stayed up, and hangs in the dark still, uncounted. They call these the Unfallen, and the Count cannot close until the last of them has come down.

So on the roof of every great ossuary a frame of long bones is strung with cords, nine by nine, and under it the star-watchers lie on their backs and count one square of sky each night. When a star falls, the Seekers walk its line to where it ends, twenty days or a year away, and what they bring home is always bone: white, colder than any bone of the niches, with no marrow in it. It is laid on the Wall of the Come Down with its number, and a name, which is the night it fell.

On the lower stairs it is said that an Ossuarch can ask them down early, and that they fall to him straight as a line drawn with a rule. The watchers who have seen it say only that when they counted the square of sky the bones fell from, it held as many lights as it had the night before.

## The Knights and the Pale

The Order is monastic, but it is not gentle. Its knights wear plate of carved bone, cut with the names of the brothers who wore it before, and they ride the low road down into the lowlands with the standing dead walking behind them, two abreast, carrying the chests. They go wherever bones lie out of their place, kneel, say the name of the place so that it knows it is being answered, and lift the bone in two hands as a man lifts a sleeper.

Every faith touches the dead differently, and so the knights have feuds with all of them. The Bleeding Maiden's processions want the flesh, and on the oldest barrows the two faiths have kept the old terms for centuries, one taking the meat and the other the frame. The Veiled Crone's circles keep souls in glass and let bone lie, which the Order calls neglect; the circles call the standing dead a desecration. The Myriad's Shrine Keepers give their dead to the water, and the knights wade in after them. The Order carries the bones home anyway, courteously, and does not bargain. The villages under the Ossa give it a tithe of sons as well, who go up the stair and are not seen again. *Nobody thanks the floor,* the knights say.

A knight rides the low road and comes home. An Ossuarch sent out of his ossuary does not; he walks the Pale. That is the name of a path out of the Ossa and across the Hide, and a lowlander would see nothing of it but, here and there, a white stone by the road: a Warden sets a Pale-Stone wherever he has gathered bones, and goes on, always outward. The lowlands have a saying from it: of a man who has gone too far to be reached, they say he has gone *beyond the Pale.*

## The Mouth and the Gold Bell

Now the second order, and the people of Lower Stair will tell this part, because they lived under it.

Where the god's knee-bone broke up through the Hide when the body came down, there stands a mountain, and on it stood the monastery of the Gilded Peak, which its monks called the Kinrei-shū, the Gold Bell. The monks served the thing that unsaid the god, the Silence that most people call the Hush. They called it Ur-Nihl, plainly, as only they and the mad would do.

Their story of the beginning is not the Order's. Before anything there was only the Silence, and in the Silence a mouth opened, and the opening was the god. It drew one breath in and held it, and everything that has ever been was inside that one held breath. It was small. The Silence did not notice it. So the Peak taught that creation is a pause held too long; that the Silence is the space between words, holding all meaning from the start; and that it is not the god's murderer but its rest. Every living thing on the Hide is a wound not yet allowed to close, and the god is tired, and the Hush came in to let it lie down.

The monks owned a bowl, a robe, and in time a lantern, and nothing else. They chanted in single breaths: one long syllable, then a silence as long again. The lowlands believed the syllables held the miasma off the slopes, for it lay in the valleys and never climbed the Peak. The novices were taught that the syllables were only fences, and the silences were the prayer. And no novice was ever allowed to listen to the silence alone. There was always a shoulder pressed against his, a brother breathing beside him. The novices thought it was kindness.

The Silence, the Peak taught, has two hands, and both are empty. Radiance, the open hand, erases with light: noon with no shadow, a brightness so complete it leaves nothing to see. Absence, the closed hand, erases with dark: the swallowing palm, the gate shut over the sun. Neither hand keeps anything, and so the lowlands called every monk of the Peak an Empty Hand, long before the name meant any one of them.

For Radiance, the Peak gilded everything it honoured: bells, lamps, the faces of its saints, in leaf so thin a breath would lift it, until in full noon there was nothing to see of them but light in their shape. *To be looked at and not seen* was the Peak's word for holiness. The monks called the gold mercy. The pilgrims called it frightening. People at Lower Stair say both were right.

## The Bowls

The monks came down from the Peak at dusk, in fours, barefoot, with small brass bells at their belts, and the lowlands heard the bells before they saw them. They held out plain wooden bowls worn black at the lip, and they asked for one thing. *Put a name in it.*

The lowlanders learned to whisper into the bowl the names they wanted gone: a grief, a debt, a shame, a man. The monk laid his palm over the bowl, closing it, and bowed, and the monks went back up the mountain. At noon, at the lip of the east stone, the abbot gave the names to the open hand. And the lowlands forgot them. Gratefully, mostly.

The forgetting, they say in Lower Stair, has no edge. A woman who gave a name to the bowl kept boots by her hearth that came up past her knee, and oiled them every spring, and could not wear them. A town went a whole winter without a name of its own. The Peak never asked where the names went. Or it asked, the lowlanders say, and the open hand stayed open, and that was the answer. Some say the bowls grew heavier over the years, though nothing was ever put in them that anyone could see.

The Peak did the same for its own. A monk carried his teacher in a lantern that burned with a black flame and gave light anyway, and the teacher burned his own name for fuel, slowly, down to nothing; when the name was gone, the lamp went out. And when a monk died, the Peak did not bury him, or burn him, or stand him in a niche. It gilded him, and gave him to the Silence, and left nothing. No bone. No dust.

The oldest masters were gilded and set in the hundred and eight niches of the inner sanctum, and those were the Peak's saints. Every dawn a novice wiped their faces with a dry cloth, because dark tracks ran from their eyes each night. Pilgrims said the saints wept for the living. The monks never corrected them. One niche, the last on the left, was always empty; the abbot said it was kept.

## The Nine Days

How the Pale Order learned what the Peak did with its dead, nobody now tells the same way. In Lower Stair they say a knight on the low road asked where the Peak laid its dead, and an old woman told him: nowhere, brother, they give them away. On the Ossa the brothers say only that the Order came to know it. However it came, to the Order there was no worse sacrilege on the Hide. A bone out of its place is a wound; a bone given to nothing is a wound that can never close, a gap in the Count forever.

The Wardens in Agreement sent the fourth chapter. In the Order's book it is written that two hundred and six of the standing dead were sent, anointed joint by joint, a whole frame's number. They crossed the lowlands and stood at the foot of the Peak's stair, and did not climb. For nine days they waited there, and in the long silences of the chant the monks heard them, a small dry sound like beads told on a cord. The dead were counting.

On the ninth day the last abbot of the Peak wrote a letter to the brothers who were below in the lowlands with their bowls, and folded it into the shirt of the youngest novice on the mountain, a girl the monks called Sō, which is only the sound of a breath let out. He sent her down the old water-cut, the steep meltwater channel that was the novices' quick way down. *Do not count the steps,* he told her. *If you count them, you will owe someone the number.* Then he opened her hand, laid nothing in it, shut it, and said: *Keep that for me.*

The letter survives. In it the abbot wrote that the Order believed the Peak had given their Mother's bones to nothing, and that the Order was right. The Order believed a bone out of its place is a wound in the world; the Peak believed a bone kept in its place is a wound held open. Both faiths had told the truth, he wrote, and so there would be blood on the stair. His monks were too light to strike, and he would have forbidden it if they were strong. *A blow is a name shouted, and we have spent our lives unlearning how to shout.* They would be in the hall, one breath each, and when each breath ended they would let the silence go on.

At the end he asked a strange thing, and gave no reason. The first of his brothers up the stair when it was quiet must go to the inner sanctum before looking for anyone, open both its doors wide, and set a stone against each. He must not wipe the faces that morning, nor count them, nor stand in the doorway, but to one side, with his back to the wall and his eyes on his own open palm, until noon. Then he must take his bowl and go down, and ask for names as he had been taught.

## The Tenth Night

It was the spring of the one thousand and ninety-eighth year from the last breath. The brothers of the Ossa point out that the figures of that year come down to nine, and they say it as though it settled something.

On the tenth night the standing dead climbed.

Here the tellings part, and they never meet again.

The Pale Order tells it short. The dead went up the stair, a thousand and ninety risers cut into the god's knee, to carry the Peak's bones home before the Hush could take them. The monks did not fight, and so each was cut down where he stood and raised before he touched the flags, so that not one more frame would be given to nothing. The Order calls that a mercy: the monks were given what their monastery had denied them, a frame to stand in and a place in the Count. Of what happened in the courtyard after the monks were raised, the Wardens in Agreement say nothing. The knights say nobody under forty in plate knows what the dead did up there, and nobody over forty will say.

The Peak's survivors tell it from the hall. The monks sat as the abbot had promised, one breath each, and the abbot carried his own teacher's lantern in with a syllable or two of the old man's name still burning, and said that if it went dark before the dead reached the top step he would take it as leave. They let themselves be cut down and raised, because the abbot had told them to let the Order have its mercy. What came after, the survivors do not know. No one who was in the hall came down.

The lowlands tell it from the valley. A traveller who lay that night at a ford beneath the Peak says he heard nothing all night long, and has come to think that nothing was what there was to hear.

## The Morning of Glass

When the sun came up, the flagstones of the courtyard had gone to glass.

Everyone agrees on that much. The glass is black and smooth and flat as still water, warm under the hand by noon and by night. The dead were gone: not one of the Order's two hundred and six came down, and there was no bone anywhere on the stair, or in the courtyard, or in the dormitories. And the doors of the inner sanctum, each leaf three times the height of a man, stood open.

The Order sent a knight-scribe up in the season after, to count. He counted the risers going up and coming down, and the two numbers agreed, and he wrote that it was the only count on the Peak that did. In the glass he found shapes, faint as a face in a dark window, lying at every depth, all turned toward the sanctum doors. Some stood. Some knelt, and he wrote to his Warden that the Order's dead do not kneel. He counted the shapes and found two hundred and six. He counted again and found two hundred and nine. On the third morning he did not count them.

In the sanctum the saints stood in their niches, gilded to the fingernail, facing the doors, with dark tracks running from their eyes. He wiped one, and by morning the track was back, and wet. The lintel gave the Peak's own tally, a hundred and eight. He found a hundred and seven niches filled, and one empty, with gold dust on its ledge and no other dust, and from it to the doors the marks of bare feet in the gold on the floor, going one way. Beneath every niche hung a lamp of the Peak, dark, its oil still full; beneath the empty niche the hook was bare. And on the right-hand door someone had scratched four words: THEY WERE NOT WEEPING FOR US.

Who scratched them, nobody says. Who opened the doors, nobody agrees. The abbot asked that the first brother up should open them, and some say one did. But Sō, who waited at the foot of the water-cut all that season, swears nobody went up past her. When she had asked the abbot who would open the doors if the brothers below were slow, he told her: *someone always comes up first.*

They quarrel even over the empty niche. Sō says it was always empty, kept by the abbot's word. The knight-scribe says there were footprints leading out of it.

## What Each Side Says Now

The Pale Order calls it a war. The Peak never called it anything. It is still the bloodiest quarrel on the Hide.

The Order says it did what the Rule demands. The Peak was giving the Mother's bones to the Silence, which takes with no rite at all, and the Order went to carry its Mother home, and something on that mountain took its dead from it. The knight-scribe who counted the glass, whom the brothers now call the Counter of the Peak, says he still carries that count in his head, two hundred and six and two hundred and nine, an open count he cannot close. Nobody numbered the three he left out of the book. He wants to know where they rest.

The Peak's people say the abbot was right, and both faiths told the truth. They say the Peak lets the dead finish, and the Order will let nothing finish but dust. And some of them say quietly that they are not sure what was waiting in that hall for the Order's dead, or whether it was the monks at all.
Not every monk of the Peak was on the mountain that night. Those who were down in the valleys with their bowls were spared, and they still walk the lowland roads, barefoot, asking for names, with a black flame following a little behind. These are the Empty Hands now: any monk of the Peak who has heard the space between words and carries nothing. What they strike leaves smooth black glass, a little dust, or nothing, and never a corpse. The Shrine Keepers hunt them. The Order's knights, meeting one on the low road, do not speak of the Peak, and neither does the monk.

When the knights of the Kostyak speak of the Peak at all, they say: *the Peak leaves glass where the Order wants bone.* When the Peak's last monks speak of the Order, they say nothing; they hold out the bowl, and wait the length of a syllable, and the length of the silence after it.
