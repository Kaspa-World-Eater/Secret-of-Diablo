# The Making and the Fall

*These are the oldest stories of the Hide, as the grandmothers tell them at the banked fires of the Ashen Moor on Kneel-Night, when a stranger has come in from the road and must be told where they are standing. Where the faiths tell it otherwise, the teller says so, because the tellers of the Cheek have heard every version on the road and believe none of them entirely.*

---

## Before the first before

Put your hand flat on the ground. Feel how it is warm, in every season. Keep your hand there. That warmth is where every story starts.

Before there was anything, there was the Hush. That is what we call it on the Moor, and we say it in a whisper, because in the beginning you could not say it at all. There was nothing in it that could be named, and so nothing in it could stand. It had no edge. The old people call it the Edgeless when they must call it anything, and most of them would rather not.

What was in it, or whether anything was, nobody knows. Every fire on the Hide begins its telling here, and every fire stops here a little sooner than the stranger would like. The Pale Order, who keep the bone-halls in the white ridges, forbid their brothers to speak of what came before; in their old books that place is written as a gap, and they will not let you put your finger in it. The circles of the Veiled Crone say that before their goddess began to spin, the whole world was like the east wall of the reading-room at Greywell: dry in every season, unmoving, held by nothing. The monks of the Gilded Peak say that before there was anything there was only the Silence, and that it lacked nothing.

A traveller once asked a deaf salt-boiler, with his hands, what came before the god. The salt-boiler laid both palms over the traveller's mouth, held them there a while, and nodded. That is the best answer anyone on the Hide has given.

## The god who held a room open

Then, in the Hush, there was a god.

How it came to be there, no one can tell you. On the Moor we do not even agree that it came. Some say it was always in the Hush, the way a word is always somewhere in a mouth before it is spoken. Some say it opened. The Gilded Peak teaches that a mouth opened in the Silence, and that the opening was the god; that it drew one breath in and held it, and that everything that has ever been was inside that one held breath. It was a small thing, the monks say, and the Silence it opened in did not notice it. They call creation a pause held too long.

The Myriad, whom common folk call the Shrine Keepers, tell nearly the same story and mean something else by it: the great one breathed in once, long, with every small god of every stone and ford and bell warm inside that breath, and then began to breathe out, and the world is the out-breath.

The Veiled Crone's circles tell it as a thread. Their goddess drew the first thread out of nothing and tied the first knot, and the knot was the world, and every life is a length of that one thread. Some of the circles add, very quietly, that someone had tied a knot before she began, and the eldest do not like to hear it said.

The processions of the Bleeding Maiden say the world began as a wound that opened and did not close. What came out of it was flesh, and the flesh grew because nothing told it to stop. The Maiden is that first opening; the Mother is what grew from it. And the Pale Order says nothing of any making at all. The world was not made, the brothers say. It was set down. The Bearing Mother fell, and her frame came to rest as the world, and the first brothers stood beside her and laid her bones where they lay. Everything began at that falling, and the Order counts from it.

On the Moor, we tell it the way our grandmothers told it. There was a god, and the god was three. And here the children say it back:

> *Soul for the thread and Bone for the frame,*
> *Flesh for the feeding, and none for the name.*

The Soul was the thread the god wove its fates on. The Bone was the frame that held it up. The Flesh was the stuff that filled the frame and grew. And all three were one god at once, the way a knot is three turns and still one thing.

What the god's own name was, nobody has kept. "The Triune," "the Three-in-One," "the God Beneath": these are only the shapes people make with their mouths when they point at it. A copyist named Aumery, who kept a scriptorium by the Ashwake milestones for forty years, spent his life trying to write that name down. Whatever ink he used, in the morning the leaf was clean. In his last years he only held the pen over the leaf and thought the name, and in the morning there was a pale place in the paper the size of his palm, thinner than the rest. He kept that leaf between two boards and did not open it after dark.

And you should know, before we go on, that not everyone on the Hide agrees it was a god at all. Some say it was the greatest of all gods. Some say it was something else, older and duller, that never had a mind. Some say it is not dead. The things that live down under the Hide, in the country beneath the skin, do not call it anything. What every faith agrees on is only this: the ground is warm, the dead do not stay down, and things still come up out of the body.

## The Held Space

The god held a room open inside the Hush. That is the oldest thing we are sure of, or as sure as anyone is of anything that old.

It was wide as the Moor and wider, our grandmothers say, and warm as two hands cupped round a candle. There was no night in it. Light lay evenly over everything and came from nowhere, and the whole room smelled, the old voices say, of being held. The scholars call that time the Age of the Held Space, and nobody has found a better name.

We were not the first ones in it. That is a hard thing to hear at a fire, and the grandmothers do not like to tell it, but it is true, and the stranger should know. Others had been in the Held Space a long while before us, and they had work. There was a Choir that lived behind the god's ribs, in a long groove that runs under the Rib Crypts still, and breathed in turns so that the god's note was always held. There were builders, who say they stood up out of the bone and began cutting at once, and that the first thing they cut was the room; their low halls with their high steps lie under the ossuaries of the Pale Order, and the Order does not say whose they are. There was a court of white faces that judged the god's thoughts. And there were others the tellers cannot name.

Near the end, and only near the end, there was a congregation. They knelt inside the god, as many as you can count and as many again, and they sang to it from inside it. They knelt all day and all night, though there was no day or night to kneel through. What they sang nobody kept. The grandmothers keep only the tune: three notes, low, going down, and then the place where a fourth should be. The fourth goes up, like a question. No one on the Cheek sings it, and if a child wakes knowing it without being taught, the whole camp moves before grey.

Those kneeling people were our grandmothers' grandmothers' grandmothers. That is what makes us the Tithed. Everything else on the Hide is a piece of the god. We alone are something else.

What the old ones in the Held Space thought of us, you may hear in the deep places if you are foolish enough to listen. The Choir says the congregation "answered badly, with names." The builders say the god listened too far to us, and forgot to close her hand. And there is a thing, two of the old voices say, that the god set down among the kneeling ones and kept there, the way a fire is kept overnight under the ash. What it was, nobody at any fire will say. Keep your children's names low when you pass the old drifts. The miners say so, and they are right.

## The loud gods

Here is a part of the story the grandmothers of the Cheek do not tell, because they do not know it. It comes from the scholars of the Citadel, who read it off broken tablets no one can read whole.

There was an age, they say, when the gods walked and were loud with worship.

To understand that age, you must know the rule the whole world runs on. A god is a belief that grew a body. Fear and worship are what it lives on, and the more who fear it or pray to it, the more real it becomes and the farther it reaches. A god that nobody remembers starves. And belief does not pass through a god and away; it settles. In the bones of anything long feared or long prayed to, belief hardens into marrow. That is what godmarrow is, the warm slow stuff the brothers draw up their wells, and every faith of this age has learned to spend it.

So the powers of that age ruled by terror, because a city that trembles is a city that worships. They were many, and most of them had nothing to do with one another, or with us.

The greatest of them, perhaps, were the dragons. The scholars write their name as the Ythraal and call them the First Terrors, and what they lived on was the fear of a world that had no walls. They were not the only power, and the scholars are careful to say so: they were one among many, perhaps the greatest, perhaps only the ones that lasted longest.

There were others. There were the Gorrhaun, the Famines, god-bodies the height of hills that had been worshipped so long they never stopped moving after they died; whole kingdoms worshipped them by walking up the ramp into them. There was the Tower That Drinks, a single living ooze that filled a tower from cellar to crown, full of eyes, every eye once someone's. There were the Walking Walls, cities that worshipped their own walls until the walls stood up and walked away with the city still inside.

And there were people who learned the rule and used it. Kings and abbots and generals ruled through dread long enough to be believed in, and some became small gods without meaning to. Some mortals learned to live on fear directly and made themselves gods that could not die, each one a faith of one.

Not everything was a god, even then. Some things lived by other rules or none, and some of them ate gods.

How this age fits beside the Held Space, nobody can tell you. The grandmothers of the Cheek go straight from the kneeling to the walking-out and have no room in their story for dragons. The scholars put the loud gods before the last breath, and cannot say where the god of three natures stood among them, or whether it was one of them at all, or whether the loud gods walked on the outside of it while we knelt within. The Pale Order says its first brothers stood beside the Mother when she fell, and that nothing was lost, only not yet counted. Most of what is known about that age is wrong, and everyone knows it, and no one knows which part.

## The Unsaying

Now the god died. This part every fire tells, and every fire tells it differently.

On the Moor it goes this way. The Hush came pressing at the god, the way the cold presses at a tent. It did not break in. It took names. It took the little names first: the name of the god's smallest finger, the name of one eyelash, the name of the hour before morning. And each time it took one, the god forgot a little. The people inside knelt closer together and sang louder to fill the gaps. It was no use. The Hush took and took, and one night the god forgot how to be three.

The Choir that lived behind the ribs tells it differently again, and more terribly. They had breathed in turns since the beginning, so the god's note was always held. And once, only once, every one of them drew breath at the same moment. There was a rest in the song. And the Hush came in through that rest. The Choir remembers it as the first silence, and calls it the holiest and sharpest day they ever knew.

And there is a preacher who sat at the Dry Ford on the Barrows road, three dusks running, who said the sages had it wrong. The god was not murdered, he said. The god was eased. "You have sat by a bed where someone could not let go," he told the carters. "You have loosened the fingers yourself, one at a time, and the last fingers hold hardest." On the fourth dusk he did not preach. Where he had sat there was a smooth black mark in the shape of a man with his hands cupped before him, and no body, and no smell of one.

However it was done, the god forgot the knot, and the three natures came apart. The Soul went off unravelling, weaving with no pattern. The Bone stood where it stood, and refused to lie down. The Flesh went on growing with no mind to tell it to stop, and no one to thank. That was the dividing of the god, the grandmothers say. Nobody cut it. It came undone.

And the god died with the people still inside it, still on their knees.

"Were they frightened?" the children always ask.

They kept singing, says the grandmother. That is what brave is.

Not everyone tells it so. There are people on the roads, and some of the faithful themselves, who say the god did not come undone of its own accord. They say the faithful divided it. When the god lay dying, or dead, or only sleeping (they disagree about that too), its worshippers took knives to it and cut it apart and shared it out, the Bone to some, the Soul to others, the Flesh to others again, each carrying off the piece it loved best. The grandmothers will not have that told at their fires. Which telling is true, nobody living can say. Perhaps both are wrong.

## What became of the pieces

Every scrap of the god forgot what it belonged to. A finger forgot the hand. A breath forgot the mouth. A prayer forgot whom it was for and grew skin. Everything that walks on the Hide that is not one of us is a piece of the god, forgetting.

You will meet them on the road. And the Tithe-Hands, the god's own hands, cut loose from it, the size of a grown man, come walking on their fingers out of the ash in threes and fives and circle round behind you. Those who hold that the god was cut apart say each of them is the god's right hand, taken off at the wrist when the faithful divided him; the grandmothers say only that a hand forgot its arm. Put a wall at your back.

They do not hate us. That would be simpler. They remember one thing only, and it is being warm, with people kneeling inside them, singing. Some of them want us back inside, where we were. Some of them want to put us on like a coat, and kneel, and be us. Either way they are homesick, and we are the only home they have left to smell. So you do not sing on the Moor at night, not even to yourself. They know the tune.

And the powers that were left took their own roads. The Bone, which the Pale Order calls the Bearing Mother, its old name Oss-Vharoth, stands in the white ridges and the brothers tend it. The Soul, Yh'Anuul, is the Veiled Crone to her circles, who read its threads in polished glass. The Flesh, Nol-Shogthuth, is the Mother to the processions of the Bleeding Maiden, who give it blood in measure with the Words said over it so that it knows whom it is for. The god's last breath, which has no name at all, carries the small gods the Myriad keep shrines to. And the Silence itself is kept by the monks of the Gilded Peak, who keep nothing on purpose.

Whether the faiths cut their pieces from the god or only picked them up where they fell, the lowland people say, bitterly, that they shared out the dead god among them like heirs round a bed. Each faith calls its own craft holy and the others' theft, and has for a thousand years.

## How we came out onto the Cheek

When the god died, the room went cold. The walls went slack. And the people got up off their knees for the first time in all their lives, and it hurt; that is why your knees ache when the ash is wet. They could see nothing. So they took hold of each other's belts in the dark, the first by the second and the second by the third, and they walked.

It was a long road. They went through places that were soft and places that were hard, and places that pulsed a little and then stopped. At the end of the road there was a door, warm and wet, that opened and shut, opened and shut. That was the mouth. The god's last breath was going out of it, as it goes out still, and our people walked out on the breath and fell down here, on the cheek of the god, on the ash. They kissed it. That is why the Tithed of the lowlands live on the Moor, and why we keep our fires low, so as not to scorch the place where we were let out.

"Did they all come out?" the children ask.

All of them, says the grandmother. Every one.

On the second night, telling it again, she says: most of them.

That is why, once a year, when the ash is wettest, every camp on the Cheek keeps Kneel-Night. We kneel round a banked fire from dark to grey, with no singing, and nobody may stand. At first grey we all stand at once, and it hurts, and we take hold of each other's belts and walk one slow ring round the fire. Nobody can tell you why the fire must be banked. And that is why every belt on the Cheek is knotted at the back, so that whoever walks behind you has something to hold. Even alone. Even asleep.

## The Silence, as each tells it

You will hear the Silence called many things on the road, and every faith that names it tells you a different story about what it is. Here are the ones I know.

On the Moor it is the Hush, the thing that unsaid the god. We fear it, and we do not speak of it loudly, and we keep our true names behind our teeth. A mother gives her child a name in her mouth the hour it comes and tells nobody, and says it aloud only once, at the hearth, when the child is weaned. A name spoken is a name that can be unsaid. My own I have not heard since my mother said it, and I will know it when I hear it again.

The Pale Order says the Hush takes names one at a time, as it took the god's, and so the brothers trust numbers instead. A number outlasts a name, they say; in their oldest book whole lines stand blank where names were written, and the numbers beside them are still there in the same ink. Their novices lay their birth-names in a niche with their first teeth, so that the Hush has nothing of theirs to unsay.

The Veiled Crone's circles say the Silence is a weave with no pattern left in it, and they bar their doors against anyone who speaks its name; in their Reading it is the Void That Emanates, and it hears its name the way you hear your own across a crowded room. The processions of the Bleeding Maiden hate it most of all, because their end is a world where the Flesh has filled every hollow, every wound and mouth and grave, and the gap between one word and the next, and when they say so they say it carefully, because they know it means the end of the Silence too.

Everyone agrees on one thing about it, and it is the thing that frightens them. When anything made of the god dies, something stays: a bone, a husk, a smear, a smell, something to burn or bury or count. When the servants of the Silence are done with a thing, there is nothing. No corpse, no bone for the Order's niches, no blood for the Maiden's cups, no breath for the Myriad, no thread for the Crone's glass. A little smooth black glass. A little dust. Every faith on the Hide fears that above anything, and every faith, at some time or other, has been glad of it.

And there is the Scar, far off, where the ground will not close. It is said that the Silence came in there. Where the god lies open, the Silence is nearest, and every wound in the Hide is a door left on the latch.

But the monks of the Gilded Peak tell it another way altogether, and I will tell it as they tell it, though few on the Hide believe them. The Silence, they say, is not a killer. It is the space between words. A word means something only because of the quiet on either side of it; a note is music only because of the rest after it; take the silence away and every sound runs into one noise. So all the meaning in the world is held in the Silence, and it is written everywhere for those who can see it: in the gap between two letters, the pause in a chant, the blank margin of a page, the still air between two breaths, the empty place in a ring of standing stones. Gods are made of being spoken of, the monks say, and the Silence is the one power that is not spoken and cannot be, and every god is held inside it the way a word is held between two pauses. The old faiths that call it a murderer have misread it.

So the monks chanted one syllable, and then held the pause after it, and the pause was the prayer. They went down into the lowlands in fours with bowls, and asked for names, and carried the names away. Where the names went, nobody knows. Those who have heard the Silence once, it is said, never hear words the same way after. They hear the spaces, and what is held there.

That is the Peak's teaching, and the Peak is fallen, its courtyard turned to black glass the night the Pale Order's dead climbed its stair. Whether its monks were right, I cannot tell you. I have never been quiet enough to find out.

## The starving

The loud gods did not last. This, too, the scholars tell, and the tablets.

The gods starved, one by one, as their peoples died, or stopped believing, or were eaten. A god that starves to the very end does not simply die. It breaks apart into demons, the scholars say, the way a carcass breaks apart in the heat, and the demons go on living on fear alone. They bargain with people, because a bargain is the nearest thing to prayer they can get. You will find them everywhere, thinly.

The cities emptied. What was known was lost with the hands that knew it. And some powers did not die at all. They slept, or went under, or kept walking because no one told them to stop. The Tower That Drinks is awake, and a town in its sight has no dreams of its own.

The dragons went too, all but one. The last of them would not lie down. When its worshippers were gone it ate them, and when they were gone it ate its own flesh, and it wears still an iron crown it forged when it had subjects to fear it. The scholars call it Morrh-Ythraal, the Starved Crown. It sits, they say, on a broken throne in a citadel it consumed to stay awake, nearly a corpse itself, forgotten, and desperate to be feared again. It was once the awe of a whole land. Now it lives on nothing. Do not go looking for it, and if anyone ever tells you they are afraid of it, ask them who told them to be.

## The Age of the Last Breath

And so we came to now, which is the Age of the Last Breath.

The god's last breath is still leaving it. On the Moor after dark you can feel it as a steady pull at your back, always toward the same quarter, out past the Sighing Ridge toward the dark. Everything light enough rides that breath away: the small gods of the stones, smoke, a dropped name. Ash has never once come back. When you die, the grandmothers say, you go down with the breath, and it carries you on out like a leaf on a ford. Some say you go back in. The grandmothers do not say it.

The Pale Order counts the years from that breath, and by their count this is the eleven hundred and fourteenth year. Other faiths count otherwise and say so. On the Moor we rarely count at all.

It is an age of ignorance, and everyone knows it. What is left of the heights is roads that were avenues, a stair that nobody climbs, a citadel walking somewhere far off. The faiths of this age keep the dark back with a soul in a jar for a lamp. There is no oil in any lantern on the Hide. Each one is lit with someone who chose to be kept rather than go down into the Last Breath. A guild called the Wick-Binders was the first to keep a soul in a lantern, the tellers say, and was hunted out for it, and every lantern on every road is theirs still. When you fall, your lantern carries you back to the stones, and keeps a little of you each time. That is what it burns. Wipe the glass gently. It is someone.

## What the Tithed are for

So here is what we are, and what we are for, as the grandmothers tell it.

We are the Tithed: owed, and given. We are the only things on the Hide not made of the god. The breath going out of you is the god's, lent; every morning you give a little of it back, and you call that living. A newborn's first breath out is longer than its first breath in, and so the saying goes on the Cheek: we come in owing.

The scholars argue over what that makes us. Mahault the Blind wrote that a congregation is where a god keeps its name while it sleeps, and that if this is so, the name is in us. Aumery listened for it in his own voice at the edge of sleep, and did not hear it.

The grandmothers do not argue it. They say the god is dead, and everything that was in it is going, and our work is to keep what is left going a while longer. So we tend the lanterns. We bury our dead, and salt the graves, and watch the barrow-lids. We walk the roads, belt to belt, and count heads at first grey and again at dark. We keep our names behind our teeth. We keep our fires low and our voices lower. That is the whole of it: toil, and keeping, and not singing at night.

And when someone has given all they can, to a power, to a faith, to the work of keeping the world going, until there is nothing left of them to give, they do not always lie down. They have been tithed, hollowed to the last; they are the tithe that was taken. The emptied ones wait in rows out on the ash, and rise when three of them gather. We do not speak of them by name. They are Tithed as we are Tithed, and they gave everything.

Every faith has an end it is waiting for, and each condemns the others' end by name. The Tithed of the lowlands have no end of their own. We say only that the lanterns will go out one at a time. And that somebody always sits up.

So. Take your hand off the ground now and put it in your lap. Bank the fire. Sleep.

I will sit up. Somebody always does.
