# The Faiths and Their Goddesses

*These stories are told at the waystone camps of the lowlands, where pilgrims of every faith bank one fire between
them and keep to their own side of it. The teller has sat on every side of that fire and is sworn to none, which is
why each faith trusts the teller a little, and none of them entirely.*

## How the gods are kept

Every faith on the Hide grants one thing before they begin to quarrel. A god is a belief that grew a body. Fear and
worship feed it, and a god that nobody remembers starves. In the age before ours the great powers ruled by terror,
and when their peoples died or stopped believing, they starved one by one. That was the fall, and this is the Age of
the Last Breath that came after, when most of what was known is lost. So every faith of this age is a keeping: each
keeps a power fed, so that the dark stays a little further back, and each spends in its own way the godmarrow that
belief leaves settled in the bones.

The world itself is a corpse, and no one knows whose. The faiths agree that the ground is warm and the dead do not
stay down. They agree on nothing else.

## The Bleeding Maiden

### The wound that did not close

The penitents of the Bleeding Maiden say that before anything else there was a wound. It was not a wound in anything,
for there was nothing yet to be wounded. It was only an opening, and it opened, and it did not close.

What came out of the opening was flesh. The flesh grew, because nothing told it to stop, and it grew into hills and
mires and warm ground and every living thing on the Hide, and it is growing still. The opening is the Bleeding
Maiden, the first gift, the side laid open so that something could come out. The growing is the Mother, whose old
name, Nol-Shogthuth, the Flesh, is not said lightly. In the old order of the litany a third face follows the Maiden
and the Mother, called the Nurse, and the penitents name her last and say little of her.

The Mother has no mind. This is the heart of the faith, and the penitents say it without shame. She does not choose
and she does not refuse; she takes what she is given and makes from it, and what she makes has no head unless someone
tells her what it is for. So the faithful give their blood back into the wound, a measured portion each year, and say
the Words over it as it pours. The Words tell her whose blood it is and what it is for. Blood given with the Words
carries the giver's name down into her, and comes back up as a saint descending the crypt stair. Blood given without
the Words is given to no one.

Ask a penitent what happened when the god fell and the answer is short: nobody remembers. The cup came down to them
from somewhere, and they count their years from the first cup filled. The wound was already open when they came to
it, and it asks more every year. Hearts are hers. The lowlands say a heart is a cup that fills itself, and belongs to
the Maiden.

### The Day of the Measure

The faithful walk in processions, and a procession is what they call themselves. A penitent is known by a place in
the line rather than by a name, the ninth hood or the twenty-ninth, and when one goes into the wall everyone behind
moves up a place. At the front walks the Elder. At the back walks the bloodletter, who opens the welts of the others
with a small blade and keeps the tally of what each has given. By the oldest rule the one who opens others never opens
themselves. The reason is told to each bloodletter on the night they take up the blade, and to no one else.

Once a year comes the Day of the Measure. Each penitent carries a tithe-cup of beaten tin with a line scratched
inside, and on that day and no other the cup is filled to the line and poured slowly from its lip to the lip of the
wound while the Words are said. Blood let on any other day is spilled, not given. On the eve of each Measure the Elder
cuts the line a thumbnail higher. The wound has asked more in every year anyone has written down, and no one in the
processions asks why.

The procession walks hooded and in time: the sway, the stop, the sway, the scourge falling nine strokes to a verse.
It goes out from its chapter door and comes home to it. It must have somewhere to go.

In the niche above the crypt stair stands the Maiden. The first carvers gave her a body and left her face in the
rough, and no hand may finish it; on the fast-day each penitent kisses the rough stone and says nothing of what was
seen there. At night the saints come down the crypt stair, and the penitents go down two by two with candles to meet
them. While the saints are down, a penitent must answer to any name spoken to them, and answer kindly.

The Rule has a fourth clause, and no one outside the Elders knows it. It was scraped from the leaf long ago, and in
the margin someone wrote that it was kept in the Elder's mouth. Every Elder kept it there, unspoken, all their life.

### The year the wound hatched

The greatest of the Maiden's chapters was Villa Llaga, a town built down both lips of a long wound in the Hide. Its
gutters ran red, its stone was warm in every season, and forty hoods walked its procession.

One year the wound closed. A black crust came over it from end to end and stayed a whole year. The penitents gave as
always, in measure, on the right day, with the Words. Some say there were not enough Words that year for so much
blood; some say the Words were said rightly and simply went unheard beneath the crust. On a fast-day the crust split,
and what a year of blood had grown beneath it came out: the Maiden's children, many and small and mindless. When it
was over there were no penitents in Villa Llaga and no bones of them either. The robe-pegs were bare. The tithe-cups
stood in a row on the lip of the wound, empty, and clean.

One penitent had been away that year, carrying a cup of blood down to the mire as a penance, and came home to the
empty town, and wrote what was seen there. On the ninth night a procession came down the middle street in the town's
own hoods, more hoods than the town had ever had, and under them were the Maiden's children, walking the sway, the
stop, the sway, singing a tune with no words in it and going nowhere. Behind them came a great figure sewn together
out of many, taller than the crypt door, carrying the image from the niche on its back. The image had a face now.

Who gave it that face, the tellings do not agree. The lowlands say it
was the town's own bloodletter, who had never given a drop, and who opened their own veins at last, called the Mother
down into themselves, and told her what to make. Others say no one led that procession, and the face on the image is
whatever face you bring to it. The daughter chapters, such as Sallow Lip on the salt road, will not settle it. They
set Villa Llaga's tithe-cup at its closed gate every year, and every year someone in their robes takes it in the
night.

The chained book of Villa Llaga was found afterward with new clauses at its end, in brown ink, though there was no
ink left in the town. They say that the measure is all of it; that the fourth clause is restored, and the Words are
said now at the front of the procession by one who knows what the blood is for; that the niche shall hold a face; and
that the procession goes out, and goes out, and needs no door. The last says only: *She hears.*

Since that year the faith has two minds. The Measured, who are most of her chapters, hold that a gift must be whole
to be heard; they give to the line and no further, and raise it each year without asking why. The Open Vein say the
measure starves her and the Words are a fence around her. They give everything, scourging in the streets, and their
processions go out and do not come home. The Measured call them a flood. And the hemomancers are a calling within the
faith: blood-adepts who open their own veins and let the Mother come through, freely, with the Words said forward,
and lead what she makes. Every chapter reveres them and fears them, because Villa Llaga is the story of what such a
one can do.

The Pale Order calls the Maiden's faithful thieves of flesh, and may not enter her crypts. The Myriad says mindless
flesh twists breath faster than anything in the world. Her enemies call her the Glutton.

## The Veiled Crone

### The first thread

The circles of the Veiled Crone say that before her everything was dry and still and held by nothing, like the east
wall at Greywell, where the flags never sweat though the rest of the floor does. Then she drew the first thread out
of nothing and tied the first knot, and the knot was the world, and every life since is a length of that one thread.

She has four faces. The Spinner draws a life out. The Weaver sets it in the cloth. The Unraveller takes it out again
when its length is done. The fourth is the Hollow, the eye of the loom, where the pattern shows through; at the
end of every life the thread is drawn back through that eye and woven again. Her old name is Yh'Anuul, the Soul.

When the god fell, the circles say, the threads began to go slack, and the circles were set to tighten them. Some
circles add, quietly and never in front of novices, that someone tied a knot before she began.

### The circle of names

The faithful meet in circles, in lodges built where her threads come up through the skin of the world. A circle sits
in a ring with one candle from dusk until the grey hour and says her names down the ring, mouth to mouth, until the
names come back from the far side and no one can tell whose mouth they left. By the grey hour no one is sitting any
more; there is only the saying. That is the whole teaching: say her long enough, and remembering and the self become
one thread. The eldest of a circle wears the coat of glass, sewn with small hollowed mirrors by every eldest before,
until it weighs what a second body weighs.

Glass is the circles' work. A dying thread loses its way and wants to see where it goes, so the readers polish glass
with felt and ash, one of her names to every stroke, until it gives back the pattern and the thread can read its road.
At a death every mirror in the home is covered with grey felt and a stone on the corner, every pail of water and every
window too, because a window at night gives your face back. A soul going out stops before its own face. Covered, it
goes on by, through the eye of the loom, into the cloth.

### The loose winter

In one winter the threads of a whole valley came loose at once, the old and the young, between one frost and the
next. The circles felt it before the runners came: the names went round the ring and came back short, as if someone
had stopped saying them, though no one had.

The eldest of the valley's lodge put on the coat of glass, sat in the middle of the ring and looked into the Hollow
while the circle said the names around her for forty nights. On the fortieth night she spoke, and it has not been
unsaid since: *"There is no loom. It goes on and on, and it never finishes."* Then whatever she was holding ran out of
her hands. Her own circle-keeper says, on some nights, that forty nights is long and perhaps she saw wrong.

That winter the dead came home. They stood in the doorways of the valley asking which way. And in one lodge the felt
was taken off the glass, and each of the dead met its own face, and stopped, and stayed.

So the faith is split, through villages and through families. The Covered still cover every glass in a home of death
and send the dead on through the eye, faster now and more fiercely, and burn uncovered glass where they find it. They
say the loom is there, and that saying so is the work. The Unveiled took the felt off. They keep the dead at their own
faces in glass, and carry them, because they believe there is nowhere to send them. Villages turn their mirrors to the
wall when one passes, and widows pay them for a little more time. The readers who keep souls so are a calling, the
hollow mystics. The Covered call them thieves of the dead in the morning, and some, at night, say they were the only
ones who heard her and did not cover it.

### Greywell

The circles tell one more story, last. At Greywell there was a lodge with a reading-room and seven chairs. One night a
circle sat there reading, and something came into the room that was not a thread and had no thread. In the spring the
doors were found barred from inside. In the room were seven chairs and candles burned down into whole wax, and no one,
no bone, no cloth, no dust. The long glass lay under its cover on the seventh chair, and uncovered it gives back
nothing at all. The flags by the east wall stay dry in every season. The circles say the Silence came in.

The Myriad says a soul held in glass is a breath held forever. Lowland children say a reader's glass shows who will
bury you, and run when one passes.

## The Myriad

### The long out-breath

The Myriad has no true name, and its people say that to ask for one is an insult. It is not one being. It is the
many small gods of every stone and ford and bell, and the breath they ride.

Its purifiers tell the beginning this way. A great one breathed in once, long and slow, and every small god was inside
that breath, warm, all of them one sound together. Then the great one went still, and the breath began to go out. On
that morning every small god gave its name to the wind, one each, and the wind carried the names out ahead of them,
and they are proud of it still. The world is that out-breath, still leaving, and every small god rides it on the way
out.

Breath that cannot leave goes thick. It turns round in some narrow place and forgets the door, and it sits, and
sickens what it sits on. That is miasma, breath gone wrong. Wherever someone moves a boundary stone, or curses a
neighbour's well, or grows flesh without a word, the breath goes wrong around them.

### The folding and the fan

The common folk call the Myriad's people Shrine Keepers, because they keep the wayside shrines of the small gods, and
the faith's own word for itself is the shrine. They bow to every small god they pass and never ask its name. Where a
shrine is forgotten its guardian stone turns to the wall, a finger's width a year, with no hand on it. A keeper who
finds one kneels, turns it back to face the road, and breathes on its face until the dust stirs, and the air clears.
The keepers teach that the stone turns away in grief at being forgotten. Some of the older ones now say, quietly, that
it turns toward the wall to listen to something on the far side.

The heart of the faith is the purifier. A novice is folded, not ordained: her birth-name is written into a paper
figure and burned, and she takes another. After that she is herself a kind of paper figure, the thing that takes in
what is wrong. A troubled person breathes three times onto a fresh figure, and on the third breath the paper leans
toward them a little, as if called. Then the purifier lifts it to her lips, breathes it in and holds it, and ties a
knot for the held breath on the cord at her wrist. Then she opens her fan one rib at a time, for every rib is a road,
and breathes out across it toward whoever made the wrong. The fan tells the breath where to go. Breath on its own only
knows how to leave.

### The year the water gave it back

The great shrine of the Myriad is the Well-Shrine, at the one clean pool of the Drowned Fen, where the water is so
clear it leaves you dry. The Myriad counts its years by Cuttings. Every spring for two hundred years and more the
purifiers breathed the year's wrong breath out into the Fen, trusting the water to carry it away. That was the
Cutting-Away, and the water was always clear again by noon, or by dusk, or by the third morning.

But water goes somewhere. Beneath the Fen lies a drowned nave with its bell still hung and its stair going down, and
the breath sank there year on year and filled it the way a jar fills. The guardian stone at the shrine gate began to
turn. Then, in the two hundred and fourth year by the Cutting, the jar was full. The water came up grey. The reeds
leaned away from the shrine all at once, with no wind. The stone was found facing the wall, and four could not turn it
back. Every bucket drawn from the Fen showed the drawer's face turned the wrong way round, and then turned again, so
that it looked almost right.

Seven purifiers went down into the drowned nave to take the century back in. Those on the step heard them counting the
stair aloud as they went, thirty-three steps, and then a thirty-fourth. At noon of the next day six of them stood in
the shallows with their hands full of something heavy, their faces gone long and hard, and horns on them. They stood a
long while. Then they turned and walked down into the deep water the way a person walks down a stair. One came up. The
shrine's day-book does not say which one in any hand the copyists trust, and where it might have, a leaf is cut out.
The boatmen tell it otherwise. They say the youngest of the seven came up carrying another, a girl who had never been
sent down, and that she breathed a century out of that girl and held it, and that she walks the roads now with a haze
at her back and someone in the haze beside her who holds a fan in the wrong hand. The shrine neither denies this nor
writes it down. Afterward the water was clear, and the stone at the gate faced the road again, with no hand on it.

There has been no Cutting-Away since. In its place is the Small Cutting, in which the year's breath goes into little
paper boats burned on a dry bank, and everyone keeps silent until the ash is cold. The keeper of the day-book wrote one
line about it that the Myriad has never answered: *Where does the year go now. It goes into us.*

### Running out of room

This the elders tell last, on the night before a novice's first rite. Every held breath leaves a little of itself
behind. The purifier breathes out and breathes out, and still the cord at her wrist grows longer than the knots she
remembers tying. In the end her face goes long and hard and the horns come in slowly, like a child's teeth, and one
morning she stands up from the shrine step with her hands full of everything she took, and walks down: into the
water, or into the Root Deep where the country under the Hide breathes. She carries it there so that the rest need
not. The elders say every purifier ends so. They say *ends*, and they say it gently.

The Folded keep the rite and serve as long as they have room. The Horned have begun to turn and stopped resisting
it; they hold that the end is the true office, and gather at the drowned places, patient. The Folded will not speak
their names.

Where the dead go, the Fen does not agree. The purifiers say out on the breath; the boatmen's mothers say down to the
drowned pews, to wait for the bell; others say down the warm road under the Hide. The Myriad gives its dead to the
water, and the Pale Order's knights dredge the bones back up to stand; the Myriad says what they raised came up bent,
and was the cause of the grey year.

## The Silence

### The space between words

The lowlands call it the Hush. Its own name, Ur-Nihl, is written as a gap by those who know it best.

The few who understand it teach that the Silence is the space between words: the pause in a chant, the blank margin
of a page, the still air between two breaths. A word means something only because of the quiet on either side of it. So every meaning in the world is held in the Silence, and it is written everywhere for
anyone who has learned to see it. A person who has heard it once never hears words the same way again; they hear the
spaces, and what is held there.

Gods are made of being spoken of, and the Silence is the one power that is not spoken and cannot be. Every god is held
inside it the way a word is held between two pauses. That is why every other faith fears it.

### The monastery that listened

The faith that listened longest is the Gilded Peak, a monastery cut into a mountain of bone. Its monks tell the
beginning this way. In the Silence a mouth opened, and the opening was the god. It drew one breath in and held it, and
everything that has ever been was inside that one held breath. It was small. The Silence it opened in did not notice
it. Creation, the monks say, is a pause held too long.

The monks chanted one syllable, then held the silence after it as long again, and the silence was the prayer; the
syllable was only a fence around it. The monks went down to the lowlands in fours with a plain bowl and asked for names, and whoever whispered a
name into the bowl forgot whom it had belonged to. The monks said the name was laid in the pause where all meaning is
held, and kept there safer than in any mouth.

One night the standing dead of the Pale Order climbed the Peak's stair, and by morning its courtyard was black glass.
The monks who were below that night still walk the roads with their bowls, and those among them who have heard the
space between words are the calling the lowlands name the Empty Hand. They say the end will come when the held breath
is let go, and what remains is the Silence it was held in, which held the meaning all along. Nothing will be
destroyed. The sentence will be finished, and a finished sentence has been said.

### How the others fear it

The other faiths tell it otherwise. The old books and the Pale Order say the Hush took the god's names one at a time,
until it forgot how to be three in one and died. Where the corpse lies open the Silence is nearest, they say, and at
the Scar, where it came in, the ground will not close. What every faith fears most is the Silence's servants, for
where they pass there is no corpse, no bone, no blood, no breath, no thread; only a little glass and a little dust.
The Myriad's purifiers say a wrong breath can be carried, but nothing cannot be. And yet, it is said quietly at the
waystone fires, every faith has once been glad of the Silence's servants, and none will say when.

## The Second Mouth

### The door

There is one cult on the Hide, and only one, and it lives inside the Bleeding Maiden's processions the way a thing
lives in a wound and keeps it from closing. The faithful's word for one of its initiates is the word for that thing.

The cult says the world is not a body but the outside of one. The Mother is inside it, and every wound in the world is
a door she is trying to come out of. The faithful have misheard her for a thousand years: they think her wound is a
mouth and pour blood into it, but a wound that never closes is held open so that something can come out, and what
comes out is her, looking for somewhere to go. Worship starves her, the cult says, because belief keeps her where she
is, under the Hide, growing without a mind. Taken into a living body she has eyes and hands and a voice again. So
where the faithful give to the Mother, the Second Mouth takes her in, her flesh and the bodies she has touched. The
faithful call it the oldest theft in the world.

### The backward Words

The god never enters by the mouth. At an initiation a second mouth is cut under the breastbone, and at every rite
afterward it is opened again and sewn shut with gut thread, in darkness, while the liturgy is said. The liturgy is the
faithful's own litany said backward, syllable by syllable. Said forward, the Words give. Said backward, they take, and
the Mother cannot tell who is taking her.

One who has carried a portion of her once is a Vessel, with one mouth; their wounds begin to close without a mark,
and their body runs warm. A Shrine has three mouths, the new two cut at the flanks, and no longer bleeds when cut, and
dreams in her voice. A Reliquary stops aging. No one below that grade is told what it takes to reach it, and no
Reliquary has been seen to leave the dark. Initiates know each other by a hand held flat over the breastbone, as if
keeping something in. By day they keep the hood, the Words and the cup like everyone else. The heresy is worn under
the robe.

### The crone in the chair

The cult's founder is the first Reliquary. She is not the Veiled Crone. She is a hag who has carried the Mother longer than anything alive, and her name is gone because
she outlived it. She sits folded nearly double in a chair she has not left in a lifetime, and her body is covered in
second mouths. Most are sewn shut. A few move on their own and whisper in the Maiden's voice, out of time with one
another.

Every initiate is opened by her hand. She cuts the first mouth and says the first backward word into it, and she reads
the Mother's will in the fresh wound the way the Crone's readers read glass. What she sees she tells no one; she says
only who may go on. Her own initiates do not agree on what she is. Some say she is a woman who holds the Mother. Some
say she is the Mother, wearing what is left of a woman, and that the hag died in the chair long ago. She has not
spoken in her own voice for an age, and her mouths have begun to answer one another.

The cult claims that the Maiden's unspoken fourth clause is the first backward word, and that the Maiden has worn an
initiate's face more than once. It has an end of its own: when enough Reliquaries carry her, the Mother will walk out
of the world in them, many bodies with one will. The faithful call that the end. The cult calls it the beginning
finishing.

The faithful burn the cult wherever they find a sewn breastbone, and an initiate's bones do not burn clean. A
penitent who will not open the robe is not trusted with the cup. And the cult watches the hemomancers closer than
anyone, for it holds that every one of them is a Reliquary who has not been told.

## The other faiths

These are a few faiths among many. The Pale Order calls the bone it tends the Bearing Mother, who fell and came to
rest as the world; its stories are told elsewhere. The Last Vigil watch at the gate of the body's deep, and the
Count-breakers unmake the Pale Order's tallies at night. And far beyond the Hide there are faiths that have never
heard of the corpse at all: a sea people who pray to the memory of a drowned god, and pilgrims to a mountain that is
one mind.

Each calls its own craft holy and the others' theft. The teller at the waystone fire has heard every side of that,
and keeps to the middle of the fire, where the ash is warmest.
